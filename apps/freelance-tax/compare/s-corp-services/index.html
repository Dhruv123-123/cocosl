The break-even math

As a sole proprietor you pay 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of profit. As an S corp you pay the same 15.3% only on the salary you give yourself; the rest comes out as a distribution without it. But you must pay a reasonable salary, and you take on payroll, a separate return (Form 1120-S) and state fees.

2026, federal only$50,000 profit$100,000 profit
Self-employment tax as a sole prop$7,065$14,130
Payroll tax on a reasonable salary$5,355 (on $35,000)$8,415 (on $55,000)
Saved before costs$1,710$5,715
Typical extra costs a year$2,000–$4,000+ (payroll, 1120-S prep, state fees)

At $50,000 the costs eat the saving. Around $100,000 it starts to pay. These are rough: a smaller QBI deduction, state taxes (California charges an $800 minimum franchise tax, for example) and your real salary all move the line. Most freelancers below roughly $60,000–$80,000 of profit are better off staying a sole proprietor.

How to elect

File Form 2553 with the IRS, usually by March 15 for the election to cover the whole year (late-election relief exists). You need an entity (an LLC or corporation) first. A service like Collective, or a CPA, does this for you.

Questions

At what income should a freelancer become an S corp?

Often somewhere around $80,000–$100,000 of profit, once the payroll tax saved clearly beats the extra costs. Run your own numbers; salary and state rules move it a lot.

What is a reasonable salary?

What you'd pay someone else to do your job. The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages if your salary is too low, so don't pick a tiny number to save tax.

Does an LLC save taxes?

Not by itself. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietor unless it elects S corp status.
