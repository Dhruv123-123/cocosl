Setting a freelance rate

Why is the rate so much higher than my old salary per hour?

Three things an employer used to pay for are now yours: the employer half of Social Security and Medicare (self-employment tax is 15.3% on 92.35% of profit), benefits such as health insurance and retirement matching, and the hours you work but can't bill. A $55,000 take-home salary works out to about $26 an hour over 2,080 hours. The same take-home as a freelancer, with typical costs and 1,200 billable hours, needs about $75.

How many billable hours are realistic?

Fewer than you think. Finding clients, proposals, invoicing, email and learning all take time no one pays for. Planning on 20 to 30 billable hours a week over 46 to 48 weeks is common. If you're new, start at the low end.

Is this the rate I should charge?

It's your floor: the least you can charge and still reach your target. Compare it with what experienced people in your niche charge. If the market pays more, charge more. If it pays less than your floor, sharpen your niche or your offer, or package the work into fixed-price projects, before you cut the rate.

Hourly, day rate or project pricing?

The hourly floor is the building block. A day rate is it times the hours in a billable day. For projects, estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, and add a buffer for revisions and the unexpected. Many consultants quote by project and use the hourly floor only to check the price.

What does this leave out?

Credits, other household income, itemized deductions, and the fact that health insurance and retirement contributions are often deductible, which would lower the tax a little. It's built to be slightly conservative. Figures are 2026 federal.
