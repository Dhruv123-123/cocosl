This guide explains how common US investment accounts and index funds work so you can make your own decisions. It is not financial, tax, legal or investment advice, it doesn't know your situation, and it doesn't recommend any company, fund or product. Tax rules and limits change every year: anything marked verify should be checked at irs.gov before you rely on it. All investing involves risk, including losing money. Chapter 2

Before you invest: a sensible order

Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years. Before that, it helps to have the ground under you solid. Many people work through something like this:

1

A starter cash buffer

Some emergency savings in an insured bank or credit union account, so a surprise bill doesn't force you to sell investments at a bad moment or go into card debt. A common starting target is one month of essential bills, growing toward three to six months over time.

2

Capture any employer match

If your workplace plan matches contributions, that match is part of your pay. Contributing enough to get all of it is one of the few no-brainers in personal finance, even while you work on other goals. Page 6 shows how matches work.

3

High-interest debt

Paying off a credit card charging around 20% to 30% is a guaranteed saving of that rate. No investment reliably beats that, so many people clear high-rate debt before investing beyond the match. Low-rate debt (say under 5%) is more of a judgment call.

4

Then: invest regularly

An IRA, more in your workplace plan, or a taxable account, depending on the next few chapters. The habit (a fixed amount, every payday, automatically) matters more than the starting amount.

Quick self-check

QuestionYes / No
Do I have some emergency cash that isn't invested?
Am I getting my full employer match (or is there none)?
Are my credit cards paid in full each month (or on a payoff plan)?
Is this money I won't need for at least five years?

Four yeses: you're in a good spot to start this weekend. A no doesn't mean you can't invest; it just tells you what else deserves attention. This order is a common rule of thumb, not a rule. Your situation may call for a different one.

Chapter 4

Workplace plans: 401(k) and 403(b)

A 401(k) (companies) or 403(b) (schools, hospitals, non-profits) lets you invest straight from your paycheck. If you have one, it's usually the first place to look.

How the match works

Many employers add money when you contribute, up to a limit. Two common formulas, for someone earning $50,000 a year (illustrative):

Match formulaYou put in (per year)Employer addsTotal invested
100% match up to 4% of pay$2,000$2,000$4,000
50% match up to 6% of pay$3,000$1,500$4,500

Contribute less than the match limit and that extra pay simply isn't paid. Your plan's exact formula is in the plan documents or benefits portal; look for "employer match" or "matching contribution".

Vesting

Your own contributions are always yours. The employer's match may "vest" over a few years: leave before then and you could forfeit some of it. The plan's summary plan description tells you the schedule.

Traditional or Roth 401(k)?

If your plan offers both, the same question as for IRAs applies: pay tax now (Roth) or later (Traditional). See page 7. Employer match money has traditionally gone in pre-tax; check how your plan handles it.

What to pick inside the plan

Plans offer a menu. Look for low-cost broad index funds or a target-date fund near the year you expect to retire. Check each fund's expense ratio (page 12). If you do nothing, money may go into a default fund; check what it is.

Changing jobs

Your vested balance stays yours. You can usually leave it, move it to a new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Avoid cashing out: taxes and penalties can take a big bite.

This weekend

Log in to your benefits portal. Note: is there a plan, is there a match, what's the formula, what are you contributing now? If you're below the match, raising your contribution percentage takes five minutes and may be the most valuable thing you do all weekend.

Chapter 5

IRAs: Roth or Traditional?

An IRA (individual retirement account) is one you open yourself, at the provider of your choice. Both types grow without yearly tax. The difference is when you pay tax.

Traditional IRA

Contributions may be deductible now (lowering this year's tax). Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income. Tax later.

Roth IRA

No deduction now. Qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, including all the growth. Tax now.

The one question: is your tax rate higher now, or later?

Take $1,000 of pre-tax income and assume it grows 5.7× over 30 years (a hypothetical 6% a year). Today's tax rate is 22%. What you'd have after tax:

Your tax rate in retirementTraditional: invest $1,000, pay tax at the endRoth: pay 22% now, invest $780Better here
12% (lower than now)$5,054$4,480Traditional
22% (same as now)$4,480$4,480Tie
24% (higher than now)$4,365$4,480Roth

When the rates are equal, the result is identical. So the choice is really a guess about your future tax rate. Early in a career, when income (and tax rate) is often lower than it will be later, many people lean Roth. In high-earning years, the Traditional deduction is worth more. Plenty of people split the difference and use both over time.

Other Roth features

  • Your contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn any time without tax or penalty.
  • Earnings are tax-free once you're 59½ and the account has been open five years (with some exceptions).
  • No required withdrawals during the original owner's lifetime.

Rules to check verify

  • Roth IRA has an income limit; above it you can't contribute directly.
  • Traditional IRA deductions phase out at certain incomes if you (or a spouse) have a workplace plan.
  • You need earned income at least equal to what you contribute (spousal IRAs are the exception).

Illustration only: one lump sum, a fixed made-up return and flat tax rates. Real outcomes depend on your income, deductions, state taxes and future tax law. Not tax advice.

Chapter 6

Taxable brokerage accounts

A regular (taxable, "individual") brokerage account has no tax perks and no rules about who, how much, or when. That flexibility is the point.

When it can make sense

  • You've used the tax-advantaged room you want to use this year.
  • The goal is 5+ years away but isn't retirement (a home deposit in 7 years, say).
  • You want money you can reach at any age without retirement-account rules.

How it's taxed (simplified)

  • Dividends are taxed in the year you receive them, even if reinvested.
  • When you sell for a profit, the gain is taxed. Held over one year: long-term, usually lower rates. One year or less: short-term, taxed like income.
  • Your provider sends a tax form (1099) each year.

Broad index funds tend to be relatively tax-efficient because they trade less, which is one reason they're popular in taxable accounts.

Red flags: pressure to decide today; "guaranteed" returns; anyone recommending products they earn a commission on without telling you; a push toward complex products (options, leverage, crypto, high-fee annuities) for your first account; sign-up bonuses that require trading frequently.

Chapter 9

Index fund basics

An index fund buys every investment in a list (an "index") in proportion, instead of paying someone to pick winners. One purchase gets you a slice of hundreds or thousands of companies.

Why beginners like them

  • Diversified: one company's bad year barely registers.
  • Low cost: no stock-picking team to pay, so expense ratios are often a fraction of actively managed funds.
  • Simple: you don't need opinions about individual companies.
  • Transparent: you know what you own.

What they don't do

  • They don't avoid market drops. When the market falls, a stock index fund falls with it, sometimes a lot, sometimes for years.
  • They don't guarantee any return.
  • A single-country stock fund is still concentrated in one market.

Common types

TypeWhat it holdsTypical role
Total US stock marketPractically every publicly traded US company, large to smallCore holding
S&P 500About 500 large US companiesCore holding (large companies only)
Total internationalCompanies outside the US, developed and emerging marketsAdds global diversification
Total bond marketMany government and corporate bondsSteadier; smaller drops and lower expected growth
Target-date fundA mix of the above that shifts from stocks toward bonds as the target year approachesAll-in-one, hands-off

ETF or mutual fund?

Both can track the same index. ETFs trade during the day like a stock, usually with no fund minimum. Index mutual funds trade once a day at the closing price, sometimes with a minimum first purchase, and are easy to automate in fixed dollar amounts. For a long-term investor, either works. Compare expense ratios.

How many funds do I need?

Fewer than you'd think. Some people hold one target-date fund. Others hold a US fund and an international fund, adding a bond fund as they get closer to needing the money. More funds isn't more diversified if they all hold the same companies.

Fund categories are described generally. Before buying any fund, read its summary prospectus or fact sheet: what it holds, its expense ratio and its risks.

Chapter 10

Why fees matter, with numbers

The expense ratio is the yearly cost of a fund, as a percentage of what you have in it. It's taken out quietly, so you never see a bill. That's exactly why it's worth checking.

yearly cost ≈ amount invested × expense ratio
e.g. $10,000 × 0.50% = $50 a year  ·  $10,000 × 0.05% = $5 a year

$45 a year sounds like nothing. But the fee is charged on your whole balance, every year, and the money it takes can't compound for you. Here's $300 a month for 30 years, assuming a hypothetical 6% a year before fees:

Expense ratioYearly cost per $10,000Balance after 30 yearsLess than the 0.05% fund
0.05%$5$298,489–
0.50%$50$274,084$24,405
1.00%$100$249,678$48,811

Same contributions ($108,000 in total), same market. The only difference is the fee: about $48,811 less with a 1% fee than with a 0.05% one.

Where to find it

On the fund's page at your provider, its fact sheet, or its prospectus. Look for "expense ratio" or "net expense ratio".

Other costs to look out for

Sales charges ("loads"), account fees, advisory fees (common with managed accounts and some robo-advisors) and trading commissions. Add them up to compare the all-in yearly cost.

Calculation: monthly contributions at the end of each month, monthly compounding, fee approximated by subtracting it from the assumed annual return. The 6% return is a made-up round number for illustration, not a prediction. Real returns vary year to year and can be negative.

Chapter 12

Opening the account, step by step

The exact screens vary by provider, but every one follows roughly this path. Allow about 30 minutes, plus a few business days for money to arrive.

1

Choose the account type

"Roth IRA", "Traditional IRA" or "Individual brokerage". Opening an account doesn't commit you to investing any amount yet.

2

Enter your details

Name, address, date of birth, SSN, employment and some financial questions. These are legally required (to verify identity and assess suitability). Answer honestly.

3

Name a beneficiary

Who inherits the account. It's quick and easy to skip, and skipping it can create a mess for your family. Add a primary and, if you can, a contingent beneficiary.

4

Turn on security

Two-factor authentication and a unique password. Never share login codes with anyone who contacts you.

5

Link your bank and transfer money

With routing and account numbers or a bank login. For an IRA, choose which tax year the contribution is for if asked.

6

Buy your investment. Don't skip this.

Money you transfer usually lands in a cash "settlement" or "core" position. It isn't invested until you buy something. Search for your chosen fund, enter a dollar amount and confirm. A surprising number of people find their IRA sat in cash for years.

7

Automate it

Set a recurring transfer from your bank on payday and an automatic investment into your fund. Check both are switched on; some providers treat them separately.

8

Then leave it alone

Check in a few times a year, raise the amount when your pay goes up, and resist selling when the market drops. Drops are normal and expected.

Chapter 14

Glossary

401(k) / 403(b)
Employer-sponsored retirement plans funded from your paycheck. 403(b)s are for schools, hospitals and non-profits.
Asset allocation
How your money is split between stocks, bonds and cash. The biggest driver of both growth and how bumpy the ride is.
Beneficiary
The person (or people) who inherit an account. Named on the account, separate from a will.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest. Usually steadier than stocks, with lower expected returns.
Brokerage account
An account for buying and holding investments. "Taxable" or "individual" means no special tax treatment.
Capital gain
Profit when you sell for more than you paid. Long-term (held over a year) is usually taxed at lower rates than short-term.
Compounding
Earning returns on previous returns, so growth builds on itself over time.
Diversification
Spreading money across many investments so no single one can sink you.
Dividend
A share of company profits paid to shareholders. Funds pass them on; you can reinvest them automatically.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount on a schedule, regardless of price. It's what automatic monthly investing does.
Earned income
Pay from work (wages, self-employment). Needed to contribute to an IRA.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund: a fund that trades during the day like a stock.
Expense ratio
A fund's yearly cost, as a percentage of your balance in it.
Fiduciary
Someone legally required to act in your best interest. Ask any adviser whether they are one, at all times, in writing.
Fractional shares
Buying part of a share, so every dollar can be invested.
Index
A list of investments that represents a market (for example, the S&P 500).
Index fund
A fund that holds everything in an index, rather than picking investments.
IRA
Individual retirement account, opened by you. Traditional (tax later) or Roth (tax now).
Match
Money your employer adds to your workplace plan when you contribute.
Mutual fund
A pooled fund priced once a day. Can be index or actively managed.
Rebalancing
Selling a bit of what's grown and buying what's lagged to get back to your chosen mix.
RMD
Required minimum distribution: yearly withdrawals the IRS requires from most pre-tax retirement accounts from a certain age. Roth IRAs have none for the original owner.
Roth 401(k)
A Roth option inside a workplace plan: after-tax contributions, tax-free qualified withdrawals.
Rollover
Moving a retirement account to another provider or plan without triggering tax, when done correctly.
Settlement fund
The cash holding where deposits land before you buy. Not the same as being invested.
SIPC
Securities Investor Protection Corporation. Protects customers of a member firm that fails, within limits. Doesn't protect against market losses.
Target-date fund
An all-in-one fund that gets more conservative as its target year approaches.
Vesting
When employer contributions become fully yours. Your own contributions are always 100% vested.

How the examples were calculated

Every number in this guide's examples was computed, not estimated: monthly contributions added at the end of each month, monthly compounding at the stated annual rate divided by 12, no taxes, no inflation. Fee examples subtract the expense ratio from the assumed annual return. The people (Alex, Sam) and the $50,000 salary are made up. Assumed returns of 4%, 6% and 8% a year are round numbers chosen to show sensitivity; they aren't forecasts and they aren't based on any particular fund.
