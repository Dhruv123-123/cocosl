Your rate floor in five steps

The Rate Calculator tab does this for you. Here's the logic, with the example already in the file.

1
Take-home you want: $55,000 a year, after tax.
2
Add what an employer paid for: health insurance $6,000, retirement $5,000. After-tax money needed: $66,000.
3
Gross up for tax: $84,000 of profit leaves about $66,430 after 2026 federal tax ($17,570, single, no state tax). That 21% is self-employment tax plus income tax, not your bracket.
4
Add business costs: $6,000. Revenue needed: $90,000.
5
Divide by billable hours, not total hours: 48 weeks × 25 hours = 1,200. $90,000 ÷ 1,200 = $75 an hour.
The floor, not the goal

$75 is the least that hits this target. Check it against what experienced people in your niche charge. If the market pays less than your floor, change the niche or the offer before you cut the price.

Pricing

Price projects, not just hours

Clients buy results. Hourly billing punishes you for getting faster; a project price rewards it.

Use the Project Pricer tab

1
List every task, including the invisible ones: the discovery call, email, file prep, project management.
2
Estimate hours honestly, then compare with your last similar project. Most people underestimate by a third.
3
Add a buffer (15% in the example) for revisions and surprises, and a rush surcharge when a client needs it fast.
4
Round up and set a deposit. The example: 31 hours × $75 = $2,325 + 15% = $2,700, with a $1,350 deposit.

Offer two options

Give the client a choice of scope, not a choice of whether to hire you: Option A (the full project) and Option B (a smaller scope at a lower price). If they push on price, move them to B instead of discounting A.

Packages for repeat work

PackageExamplePrice logic
Fixed projectLogo + brand sheet, 3 concepts, 2 revisionsHours × rate + buffer

Day rateOn-site shoot, workshopHourly floor × billable hours per day
Minimums

Set a minimum project size. Small jobs carry the same admin as big ones and quietly drag down your real hourly rate.

Winning the work

A proposal that closes

One or two pages. Written in the client's words. Ending in one clear next step.

SectionWhat makes it work
The goalRepeat their problem back in their words. It shows you listened, and it frames the price around the outcome.
What I'll do / what you'll getOutcomes first, then exact deliverables: how many, what format, by when.
Not includedThe single most useful section. Anything listed here can become a paid change order later without an argument.
RevisionsA number, not "unlimited". Two rounds is common.
TimelineDates that depend on their feedback times, stated plainly.
InvestmentOne price per option, the deposit, and payment terms. No hourly breakdown unless they ask.
Next steps"Reply to accept and I'll send the agreement and deposit invoice." Then follow up after 3–4 days.

Before you send it

  • Price checked against the Project Pricer, not a gut feeling.
  • "Not included" has at least three lines.
  • The deposit is big enough that you'd be fine if they disappeared after paying it.
  • It has an expiry date (30 days), so your calendar isn't held forever.

Getting it in writing

The agreement, clause by clause

Plain English on purpose. Read it, adjust the [brackets], and have a lawyer in your state review it before you rely on it.

ClauseWhy it's there
1–2 Scope + timelinePoints to the proposal, so the agreement stays short. Delays on their side move your dates.
3 Fees and paymentNon-refundable deposit, due dates, a late fee (check your state's limits) and the right to pause work while an invoice is overdue.
4–5 Expenses + revisionsStock images and extra rounds are paid for, not absorbed.
6 Client responsibilitiesFeedback on time, and they own what they give you.
7 OwnershipRights transfer when you're paid in full. You keep drafts, unused concepts and your own tools.
8 PortfolioYou can show the work unless they ask you not to.
9 ConfidentialityBoth ways.
10 Independent contractorYou set your own hours and tools, and handle your own taxes. Supports contractor status.
11 CancellationPaid for work done, deposit kept, optional kill fee.
12 LiabilityCaps your exposure at the fees paid. Pair it with professional liability insurance.
13 GeneralGoverning law, changes in writing, e-signatures count.
Scope creep, handled

When a client asks for something outside the proposal, reply the same day with the Change Order: what's changing, why it's extra, the cost and the new date. Start once it's approved.

Getting paid

Invoices that get paid on time

  • Invoice the day you deliver. Every day you wait is a day added to when you're paid.
  • A real due date (14 days is common), not "net 30" buried in small print.
  • A pay-now link plus bank details. Card fees are a deductible expense; slow payment isn't.
  • Reference the proposal and subtract the deposit, so the total is obvious.
  • Log it in the Invoice Log. The status column flags anything overdue.

The follow-up schedule

WhenWhat you send
3 days before dueFriendly reminder with the link.
Day 1 overdue"Could you check it's in your payment run?"
Day 7Ask for a date. Mention the late fee from the agreement.
Day 21Pause work, as the agreement allows.
Day 30+Final notice, then small claims or collections if needed.

Watch one number

The Invoice Log's average days to pay. If it creeps past your terms, ask for bigger deposits or move slow clients to pay-upfront packages.

Every January

Raise your rate on purpose

1
Re-run the Rate Calculator with this year's costs and tax tables. Your floor usually moves up.
2
New clients first. Quote the new rate to every new inquiry from today.
3
Existing clients with notice: 30–60 days, a clear new number, and anything already booked stays at the old rate.
4
Expect a few to leave. If nobody ever says no, you're probably still underpriced.

Your set-aside goes up too

The Rate Calculator shows your tax set-aside % at your target profit (21% in the example, before state tax). Move that percentage into a separate tax savings account the day each payment lands.
