Chapter 1

Why freelancers pay four times a year

The US tax system is pay-as-you-go. Employees pay through withholding on every paycheck. Nobody withholds from a freelancer, so the IRS asks you to send estimated payments during the year instead.

Who has to pay estimates

Generally, you need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file, after withholding and credits. Once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400, you also owe self-employment tax and generally need to file.

The "quarters" are uneven on purpose: 3, 2, 3 and 4 months. June's payment comes just two months after April's, which is why it catches people. A deadline on a weekend or holiday moves to the next business day.

What happens if you don't

If you pay too little, too late, the IRS can charge an underpayment penalty. It works like interest: it's figured on how much was short and for how long. It isn't a fine for being a freelancer, and it's avoidable. Chapter 4 shows the two ways to stay clear of it.

The one-sentence version

Save a fixed percentage of every payment the day it lands, pay a calculated amount on four dates, and settle the small remainder when you file in April.

Forms you might (or might not) get

A client generally only has to send you a 1099-NEC once they've paid you $2,000 in the year (it was $600 before 2026; from 2027 the $2,000 rises with inflation). Payment platforms send a 1099-K after $20,000 and 200 transactions. Many freelancers will get few forms or none. None of that changes the rule: all business income is reportable. That's why the Income tab exists.

Three things this shows

  1. Self-employment tax is the big one at this income: more than double the income tax. It starts with your first dollar of profit, with no standard deduction.
  2. Both taxes are figured on profit, not revenue. Every legitimate business expense lowers both.
  3. State tax is extra. Most states add somewhere between 0% and about 10% of income. That's why the usual set-aside is 25–30%, not 20%.

Social Security's 12.4% part stops at the 2027 wage base of $192,300 of combined wages and self-employment earnings. If you also have a W-2 job, your wages count toward that first. The spreadsheet handles this from the Setup tab.

Chapter 3

The set-aside: how much to save

The habit that makes everything else easy: the day a payment lands, move a fixed slice of it into a separate savings account that you don't touch.

Pick your starting percentage

Your situation (single filer, rough guide)Start at
Profit under ~$30,000, state with no income tax20%
Profit $30,000–$100,000, average state25–30%
Profit over $100,000, or a high-tax state30–35%
Freelancing on top of a full-time job (your wages already use the low brackets)30–35%

These are starting points, not calculations. The spreadsheet's Set-aside check (bottom of the Quarterly tab) tells you whether your savings will cover the rest of the year. Positive: you're fine. Negative: raise your percentage.

The three-account system

1
Income (business checking). Every client payment lands here. Business costs come out of here. Nothing personal.
2
Tax (a separate savings account). Same day a payment lands, move your percentage here. Pay each estimate from this account. A high-yield savings account earns a little while the money waits.
3
Pay (your personal account). Every two weeks, transfer yourself a fixed amount, like a salary. Leave the rest in Income as a buffer for slow months.
Example

A client pays $3,000. At 30%, $900 moves to Tax the same day. $2,100 stays in Income. On payday you transfer your usual $1,800 to Pay. Nothing to remember in April: the money is already there.

When the set-aside is too much

If the check is comfortably positive after you file, you've over-saved. Good problem. Leave a cushion for next year's first payment, then move the extra to an emergency fund or a retirement account (SEP IRA or Solo 401(k)), which also lowers next year's income tax.

Chapter 4

Two ways to calculate a payment

To avoid the underpayment penalty, your payments by each deadline generally need to reach the lesser of two amounts. The spreadsheet works out both and suggests the smaller.

Method A

Prior-year safe harbor

Pay 100% of last year's total tax in four equal parts. If last year's AGI was over $150,000 ($75,000 married filing separately), it's 110%.

Pros: you know the number on January 1, and it holds even if this year is much bigger.

Cons: if this year is bigger, you'll owe the difference in April, so keep saving your percentage.

Method B

Current-year (annualized)

Pay 90% of this year's tax, spread by when you earn it. At each deadline the sheet annualizes your profit so far and requires 22.5%, 45%, 67.5% and 90% of the resulting tax.

Pros: smaller payments in a slow year or slow start.

Cons: changes every quarter. Lumpy-income years may need Form 2210 when you file.

Which should you use?

  • Your income is growing (common in year two or three): the safe harbor is usually smaller and simpler.
  • Your income dropped, or most of it comes late in the year: the current-year method is usually smaller.
  • First year freelancing after a salaried job: last year's tax might be low because your employer withheld most of it, so the safe harbor can be small. That's fine for the penalty, but the April balance will be big. Keep saving your percentage.
  • Last year's tax was zero and you were a US citizen or resident for all of last year (and it was a full 12-month year): you generally owe no penalty this year at all. The sheet still shows the current-year amount so you can pay along anyway.
Suggested payment = lesser(Method A so far, Method B so far) − W-2 withholding so far − estimates already paid

Paying on time matters as much as paying enough: the penalty is calculated per installment. A late payment is still much better than none, because the penalty grows with time.

Chapter 5

The 15-minute pre-deadline routine

Put a reminder one week before each deadline. When it goes off, do this.

1
Catch up the logs (5 min). Compare your business bank statement with the Income and Expenses tabs. Add anything missing. Add any drives to Mileage.
2
Open Quarterly (1 min). Find the column for this deadline. Read Suggested payment.
3
Pay the IRS (5 min). Go to irs.gov/payments. Use IRS Direct Pay (free, from a bank account) or your IRS Online Account. Choose Estimated tax, 1040ES and the right tax year (the January payment is for the previous year). Card payments work too, but processors charge a fee.
4
Record it (1 min). Type the amount in the yellow Actually paid row. Save the confirmation number in a note or in your email.
5
Pay your state (2 min). If your state has an income tax, it usually has its own estimated payments, on its own schedule and website. The State estimate row is a rough guide; your state's worksheet is the real one.
6
Check the set-aside (1 min). Look at Set-aside check at the bottom. Negative? Raise your percentage on Setup.

Avoid these

  • Paying for the wrong tax year (common with the January payment). If it happens, call the IRS to have it moved.
  • Using a site that isn't irs.gov. Searching "pay IRS" can show ads for third-party processors that charge extra.
  • Skipping a quarter because money's tight. Pay what you can; partial is better than nothing.
Calendar it now

The Deadlines tab lists every date for the tax year on Setup. Copy all seven into your calendar with a one-week reminder.

Sam's year: the example, explained

Sam is fictional: a single freelance designer in their first full year, with no other job. Last year Sam had a salaried job, total federal tax $6,500 and AGI $52,000. Sam's rows come pre-filled in the spreadsheet.

Suggested payment$1,625$1,625$1,625$1,625

*Q4 shows Sam's numbers as of late October, before November and December income is logged. Your figures will change as you add rows.

What Sam's numbers say

  • The safe harbor wins every quarter. Sam's tax last year ($6,500) is lower than this year's pace, so a quarter of it, $1,625, is all Sam needs to pay on time to avoid the penalty.
  • But it isn't the whole bill. Sam's full-year federal tax is heading for $10,000–$12,000. After four payments of $1,625 ($6,500), the rest is due in April.
  • The set-aside covers it. Sam saves 30% of every payment. With $9,000 in tax savings after three payments, the Set-aside check shows Sam is ahead, even after a rough 5% state estimate.
  • Year two changes. Next year Sam's safe harbor becomes this year's full tax, so quarterly payments roughly double. The sheet picks this up when Sam updates Setup in January.

Try it: change Sam's filing status to Married filing jointly on Setup, or set last year's total tax to 0, and watch the Suggested payment row change. Then delete Sam's rows and add your own.

Records that make tax time boring

Self-employment tax and income tax are both figured on profit. Good records lower your bill legitimately and make filing an afternoon instead of a weekend.

What to keep

  • Income: every payment with date, client, what for and amount (Income tab), plus bank statements.
  • Expenses: receipts or invoices for every business cost, with a note on what it was for.
  • Mileage: date, where, business purpose and miles, written the same day (standard rate in Tax Tables; update it when the IRS announces 2027's).
  • Home office: measurements and a photo of the space, if you claim it.
  • Mixed-use items: how you worked out the business-use % (for example, a month of phone logs).
  • Estimated payments: every IRS and state confirmation number.

The IRS generally recommends keeping records for at least three years after you file; some situations need longer. Cloud storage plus a photo of each receipt is enough.

Commonly deductible for freelancers

Payment and bank feesPlatform and processing fees are business costs.

Some valuable deductions aren't business expenses and don't go in the Expenses tab: half of self-employment tax (the sheet already applies it), self-employed health insurance premiums, SEP IRA / Solo 401(k) contributions and HSA contributions. They lower income tax, not self-employment tax, so the tracker's estimate will run slightly high if you have them. That's the safe direction to be wrong.

Chapter 8

FAQ, 2027 figures and your checklist

I started freelancing mid-year. Do I owe the earlier quarters?

Each payment is generally for income earned in that period, so start with the next deadline after your income began. The annualized method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) recognises this when you file.

I also have a W-2 job.

Enter your expected wages and withholding on Setup. Another option is a new W-4 at work to withhold extra; withholding counts as paid evenly through the year.

Married?

Pick Married filing jointly on Setup. Enter your household's combined other wages and withholding.

Do I need an LLC or an EIN?

Neither changes this method. A free EIN from irs.gov lets you avoid putting your Social Security number on client W-9s.

Will this match my tax return exactly?

No. It's a planning estimate built to keep you safe from the penalty. Your return (or your tax professional) has the final number.
