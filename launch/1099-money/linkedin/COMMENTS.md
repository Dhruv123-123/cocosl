# Comment templates for engaging by hand

Use these on other people's posts about freelancing, consulting, pricing, clients and self-employed taxes.
They're starting points: rewrite the first line every time so it answers *that* post, and fill every
`[bracket]`. A comment that could sit under any post gets ignored.

**Rules**
- No links, no "check out my calculator", no mention of 1099 Money. People click your name when a comment
  is useful; that's the whole point.
- Add something: a number, a step, a question, or a different angle. Never just "Great post!"
- 2 to 4 sentences. Long enough to say something, short enough to read.
- Answer tax questions in general terms and say what it depends on. Don't tell anyone what they personally
  owe, and don't claim experience or clients you don't have.
- 5 to 10 a day, on posts from the last few hours, ideally from people in your target audience
  (freelancers, consultants, agency owners) rather than other "finance tips" accounts.

## When someone shares a win

**1. First client / first big project**
> Congrats on [the first client / landing X]. One thing worth doing this week while it's fresh: write down
> how they found you and why they said yes. That's the start of your next five clients.

**2. Raised their rates**
> Good move. The math usually works even if a client or two leaves: at a 13% increase you can lose about
> 1 in 8 billable hours and still come out ahead. Did anyone push back?

**3. Hit a revenue milestone**
> Well done on [milestone]. Curious how it splits across clients. Getting to [milestone] with no single
> client above a third of it is a different kind of milestone.

## When someone asks a question

**4. "How much should I charge?"**
> Working backwards helps: take-home you want, plus health insurance, retirement and business costs, plus
> tax on the profit, divided by the hours clients will actually pay for. That last number is usually 20 to
> 30 a week, not 40, and it changes the answer more than anything else.

**5. "How much should I set aside for taxes?"**
> It depends on profit and state, but for federal tax alone, somewhere around 19 to 22% of profit is common
> at $40K to $100K, because self-employment tax (15.3%) starts on the first dollar. Add your state rate and
> round up. Moving it to a separate account every time you're paid makes it painless.

**6. "Do I need to pay quarterly taxes?"**
> If you expect to owe $1,000 or more for the year, the IRS expects estimated payments: April, June,
> September and January. The next one is January 15, 2027. Worth checking now rather than in April, since
> missing them adds a penalty.

**7. "Hourly or project pricing?"**
> Depends on how well you can define the work. If you can describe the deliverable in two sentences,
> project pricing usually rewards you for getting faster. If you can't, start hourly or with a short paid
> discovery, then quote.

**8. "What should go in my contract?"**
> The ones people skip and regret: the number of revision rounds, a late-payment fee with the right to
> pause work, and what happens if the project ends early. Not legal advice, but those three cover most of
> the horror stories.

**9. "A client hasn't paid"**
> Hope it gets sorted. A short message asking for the date they'll pay (not just "following up") tends to
> get a real answer. And if the person you work with isn't the one who pays, asking who processes
> invoices can move it faster.

## When someone shares advice you agree with

**10. Add a number**
> Agree. To put a number on it: [one figure that supports their point, e.g. "a 20% discount means 25%
> more work to get back to the same revenue"].

**11. Add a step**
> This is right. The step I'd add before [their step] is [your step], because [one-line reason].

**12. Add the "why"**
> Strong point on [X]. The reason it matters so much for solo people: [reason, e.g. there's no employer
> covering the gap when a client pays late].

## When you see it a little differently

**13. Respectful nuance**
> Mostly agree, with one caveat: [where it doesn't hold, e.g. for someone in their first year, or with
> lumpy income]. In that case, [what you'd do instead].

**14. "It depends" done well**
> I think both work, depending on [factor]. If [situation A], [approach A]. If [situation B], [approach B].
> Which one were you thinking of?

**15. Correcting a tax detail, kindly**
> Small addition: [the correct detail, e.g. "for 2026 the 1099-NEC threshold went up to $2,000"]. It still
> doesn't change the income being taxable below that.

## When someone shares a struggle

**16. Slow month**
> Slow months happen to almost everyone who works for themselves. A buffer helps, but so does a small
> weekly habit: a couple of check-ins with past clients every week, even when you're busy.

**17. Burnout / too many clients**
> Sounds like the rate might be doing the work hours should be doing. If you raised it enough to drop your
> lowest-paying client, would the numbers still work?

**18. Scope creep**
> "Happy to add that, here's what it changes in time and price" usually works better than a flat no. It
> puts the decision with them.

**19. Tax surprise in April**
> That's a rough one, and really common in the first year of freelancing. Setting aside a fixed percentage
> from every payment and making the quarterly payments is what stops it happening twice.

## Open questions (to start a thread)

**20.** > How did you decide on your rate when you started, and what would you change?

**21.** > Do you tell clients your hourly rate, or only quote projects?

**22.** > What's the one number about your business you check every month?

**23.** > What's one thing in your contract you added after a bad experience?

**24.** > For people who went from employee to freelance: what surprised you most about the money side?
