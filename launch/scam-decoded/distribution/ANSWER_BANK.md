## Part 1: Quora answers (18)

### Q1. "I got a text saying I owe an unpaid toll. Is it real?"
Almost certainly not. The unpaid-toll text is one of the most common scam texts in the US right now. It names a real-sounding toll service, gives a small amount like $4.15, and threatens a late fee if you don't pay today through a link.

Three tells: a small amount (so you don't think twice), a deadline, and a link instead of telling you to sign in to your account.

What to do:
1. Don't tap the link or reply.
2. If you really use toll roads, open your toll account yourself, by typing the address you already use or through the official app, and check for a balance.
3. Forward the text to 7726 (it spells SPAM), which reports it to your carrier, then delete it.

If you already entered card details, call your card company now using the number on the back of the card and ask for a new card. Watch your statement for small test charges.

*(Disclosure line if asked who you are: "I run a scam-awareness channel called Scam Decoded.")*

### Q2. "My grandmother got a call from 'me' saying I was in jail. How is that possible?"
I'm sorry, that's frightening for both of you. It's called the grandparent scam, and two things make it convincing now.

First, the details come from public sources: your name, your city, sometimes a recent trip, from social media. Second, voice cloning. A few seconds of someone's voice from a video can now be used to make a short, crying, believable clip. The caller usually says "please don't tell Mom", and then a "lawyer" or "officer" asks for bail in cash, gift cards, or by courier to the door.

What protects her:
- **Call back.** Hang up, and call you, or your parent, on the number she already has.
- **A family safe word.** Pick a word only the family knows, never posted online. Anyone claiming to be family in trouble has to say it.
- **The money rule.** No real court or lawyer collects bail by gift card or sends a courier for cash.

If she already paid, call her bank right away, then report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or to the National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-372-8311.

### Q3. "How do I know if a call from the IRS is real?"
The real IRS almost always starts with a letter in the mail. So a surprise call is the first red flag.

The IRS won't:
- threaten to have local police arrest you,
- demand a specific payment method like gift cards, a wire, a payment app or crypto,
- ask for a credit card number over the phone without you having received a bill by mail first,
- text or email you out of the blue about a refund or a bill.

To check: hang up, and either sign in to your IRS online account (type IRS.gov yourself) to see any balance or notice, or call the IRS using a number from IRS.gov. If a letter came, look up the notice number printed on it on the IRS site before calling any number on the letter.

You can report IRS phone impersonation to TIGTA, the Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration.

### Q4. "Someone from my bank's fraud department asked me to read a code to them. I did. What now?"
Act now. That code is often what lets someone sign in to your account or approve a payment. Real bank fraud teams never ask you to read back a code.

Right now, in this order:
1. Call your bank using the number on the back of your card (not the number that called you, which can be faked). Say: "I shared a one-time code with someone pretending to be you. Please lock my account and check recent activity."
2. Change your online banking password, and the password on your email if it's the same, from the app or by typing the address.
3. Turn on two-step sign-in if it isn't on.
4. Check for new payees, changed contact details, or new devices on the account.
5. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Then a rule for the future: if anyone contacts you and asks for a code, it's a scam. Hang up and call the number on your card.

### Q5. "Is it safe to freeze my credit? Does it cost anything?"
Yes, and it's free. By federal law, freezing and unfreezing your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion costs nothing.

A freeze stops new lenders from seeing your credit report, which stops most new-account identity theft: credit cards, loans and phone contracts opened in your name. It doesn't affect your credit score, and your existing cards keep working.

How it works: you create an account with each of the three bureaus (on their own websites, typed yourself) and freeze. When you want to apply for credit, you lift it temporarily, which usually takes minutes online.

Two tips: write down your login or PIN for each bureau somewhere safe, and you can freeze your children's credit too. It takes about 10 minutes per bureau. Of everything you can do against identity theft, this is probably the most effective free step.

### Q6. "A pop-up says my computer is infected and to call Microsoft. Should I call?"
No. Real virus warnings never give you a phone number to call. That pop-up is just a web page designed to look scary, often with a loud alarm, sometimes going full-screen so it feels like the computer is locked.

What to do:
- Close the browser tab. If it won't close, close the whole browser (on Windows, Ctrl+Alt+Delete then Task Manager; on a Mac, Command+Option+Escape).
- If that doesn't work, restart the computer.
- When the browser reopens, don't restore the previous tabs.

If you already called and let them connect to your computer:
1. Turn the computer off or disconnect it from the internet.
2. Call your bank if you logged in to banking or gave card details while they were connected.
3. Have someone you trust run a security scan, or take it to a local repair shop.
4. Change passwords from a different device.
5. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

### Q7. "My dad (78) has an online girlfriend who needs money for customs. How do I talk to him?"
This is so hard, and how you start the conversation matters more than the facts.

What usually backfires: "You're being scammed." He'll defend her, and the scammer will tell him your family is jealous or controlling.

What tends to work better:
- **Ask, don't tell.** "Have you two ever video chatted? What happened when you suggested it?" "Where's she working right now?" Let him notice the gaps himself.
- **Offer to do something together.** Reverse image search her photos side by side. Seeing the same face under another name lands harder than anything you say.
- **Name the pattern gently.** Long-distance romance scams use the same jobs (oil rig, overseas doctor, military) and the same money stories (customs, medical bills, investment). Show him that, without making it about him.
- **Keep the door open.** Say "I'm on your side" a lot. Scammers are always kind; if you're harsh, he'll go back to the kind voice.

Practical steps: ask his bank about adding a trusted contact. If money has gone, report it to the FBI's IC3 and ReportFraud.ftc.gov. The National Elder Fraud Hotline (1-833-372-8311) also helps families.

### Q8. "What's the best way to protect my elderly parents from phone scams?"
A few changes do most of the work, and they're not technical.

On the phone:
- Turn on "Silence Unknown Callers" (iPhone) or the call-screening option (Android). Calls from contacts still ring; others go to voicemail.
- Save the real numbers for their bank, pharmacy, doctors and family under clear names.

Rules, written by the phone:
- "Call [your name] before you pay anyone you didn't call first."
- "No gift cards, wire transfers, crypto or cash couriers. Ever."
- "Never read a code to anyone."
- A family safe word for anyone calling "in trouble".

With the bank: ask about adding a trusted contact, setting alerts for large withdrawals, and lowering daily transfer limits.

Credit: freeze it at all three bureaus. It's free.

Then keep talking about it, like you'd check in about health: "Any weird calls this week?" Parents who feel judged stop telling you. Parents who feel listened to tell you before they pay.

### Q9. "I sent money to a scammer through Zelle. Can I get it back?"
Maybe. Speed matters, so do this today.

1. **Call your bank's fraud line** (the number on your card or the bank's official site). Say it was a scam, not a mistake, and ask them to try to recall or reverse the payment. Ask for a case number.
2. **Report it in writing**, through the bank's app or secure message, too.
3. **Report it** at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI's IC3. Keep every message and screenshot.

Honest expectations: payments sent to someone you authorized are harder to reverse than unauthorized ones, but banks do sometimes recover money, especially when it's reported quickly, and policies have been changing.

And a warning: after a scam, people often get contacted by "recovery services", lawyers or "investigators" who promise to get the money back for a fee. That's a second scam. Real recovery goes through your bank and law enforcement, and they don't charge upfront.

### Q10. "Got an email saying my account will be deleted for inactivity. Real?"
Possibly a phishing email, and the safe way to check is the same either way: don't use the link.

These emails create a deadline ("48 hours") and a fear ("all your photos will be deleted") so you'll sign in quickly on a fake page. Whoever gets that password often tries it on your other accounts too.

Instead:
1. Open the app, or type the website address yourself, and sign in there. If there's a real warning, you'll see it.
2. If you want to keep the account, change the password to one you don't use anywhere else and turn on two-step sign-in.
3. If you don't need it, download anything you want and close it yourself. An account that doesn't exist can't be stolen.

Old, forgotten accounts are exactly what attackers like, because nobody notices when they're taken over.

### Q11. "A friend messaged me on Facebook asking me to vote for her niece. Then asked for a code. Scam?"
Yes. Your friend's account has most likely been taken over. Don't send the code.

How it works: the scammer, controlling your friend's account, messages all her contacts with something easy and kind ("vote for my niece"). The link asks you to sign in, or they say "you'll get a code, just send it to me". That code is usually your own account's reset code. Send it and they take your account next, then message your friends.

What to do:
- Don't send codes to anyone, even friends.
- Tell your friend by phone or text, not in that chat, so she can recover her account.
- If you already sent it: change your password from the app, turn on two-step sign-in, sign out of other devices, and warn your friends it wasn't you. Facebook's help centre has a hacked-account recovery process, and the FTC has a guide too.

### Q12. "Is the 'free government solar panels' offer real?"
Be very careful. There are real tax credits and some local programs for solar, but "the government will give you free panels, sign today" is a sales pitch, and the FTC has warned about solar companies that misrepresent government programs, savings and tax credits.

Often "free" means a loan or lease that lasts 20 years or more. On a lease, the company usually owns the panels and claims any tax credit, not you. Some outright scams take a deposit and never install.

Before signing anything:
1. Ask for the total cost over the whole contract, in writing, and who owns the panels.
2. Get at least three quotes.
3. Check the company's license with your state and read independent reviews.
4. Ask a tax professional (not the salesperson) whether you'd actually qualify for any credit.
5. Read the full contract at home. For many sales made in your home, the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule gives you three days to cancel.

### Q13. "A contractor knocked on my door after the storm offering to fix my roof today. Should I hire him?"
Not today. He may be honest, but door-knockers after storms are exactly how roofing scams start, so check first.

Red flags: he found you (rather than you finding him), it has to be today, he wants a big deposit in cash, there's nothing in writing, or he offers to "handle your insurance" if you sign a form.

Safer steps:
1. Photograph the damage and call your insurer.
2. Only do a temporary fix (a tarp) for now, and keep receipts.
3. Find contractors yourself: neighbours, your insurer, local businesses with a real address.
4. Check each license with your state or city, and read reviews in more than one place.
5. Get at least three written estimates.
6. Pay in stages as work is done, by check or card, not a large cash deposit.
7. Read anything that assigns your insurance claim very carefully before signing.

### Q14. "Someone called saying my late mother owed them money. Do I have to pay?"
I'm sorry for your loss. Generally, no, you don't have to pay a deceased parent's debts from your own money. Valid debts are paid from her estate, by whoever is handling it, and only after checking they're real. The FTC has a page explaining this.

Also, some of these calls are scams. Criminals read obituaries and call families with fake debts, or pretend to be the funeral home saying a payment failed.

What to do:
- Don't pay or give card details on the phone.
- Ask the caller to send the claim in writing to the estate's executor.
- If they say they're the funeral home, hang up and call the funeral home on a number you already have.
- Tell the credit bureaus and Social Security about the death, to reduce the chance of identity theft in her name.
- If you're the executor and unsure, a probate attorney or legal aid can tell you what applies in your state.

### Q15. "A company says they have a buyer for my timeshare if I pay closing costs. Legit?"
That's one of the most common timeshare scams, and the FTC warns about it. The buyer usually doesn't exist; after the "closing costs" come taxes, transfer fees and more.

A general rule: real resale agents usually get paid when a sale closes, not before, and they don't cold-call you with a buyer already lined up.

Safer options:
1. Call the resort or developer directly and ask about a deed-back or exit program. Many have one.
2. If you hire help, use a licensed real estate agent or attorney in the state where the timeshare is, check their license yourself, and get every fee in writing.
3. Be extra careful with "we'll recover what you lost to timeshare scams" calls. That's the same trick a second time.

### Q16. "How do I know if a moving company is a scam?"
Moving scams can be brutal, including the "hostage load", where they pack your things and then demand much more to deliver them.

Checks before booking:
- **Interstate move:** look up their USDOT number on the FMCSA website (the federal agency for movers) and confirm they're registered as a mover, not just a broker.
- **Local move:** check your state's licensing.
- Get at least three in-person or video estimates. A phone quote without anyone seeing your stuff is a red flag.
- Get the estimate in writing, with the company's full name and physical address.
- Avoid big deposits, especially in cash.
- Read complaints, not just star ratings.

On moving day: don't sign blank or incomplete paperwork, keep valuables and documents with you, and photograph furniture before it's packed.

If things go wrong on an interstate move, you can file a complaint with FMCSA, your state attorney general and the FTC.

### Q17. "I got a letter that looks like it's from the IRS with a phone number. How do I check it?"
Good instinct to check before calling. Some fake letters copy the logo and layout perfectly, and the phone number goes to the scammer.

Three checks:
1. **Find the notice number.** Real IRS letters have a code like CP2000 or LTR 525, usually in the top or bottom right corner. Look it up on IRS.gov's "understanding your notice or letter" page.
2. **Sign in to your IRS online account**, by typing IRS.gov yourself. Real notices and balances usually appear there.
3. **Call the IRS using a number from IRS.gov**, not the one on the letter, if you need to talk to someone.

If it's real, respond calmly by the deadline; a tax professional can help. If it's fake, you can report it to TIGTA. Either way, the real IRS won't demand gift cards, wire transfers or crypto.

### Q18. "What's the single best thing I can do to avoid being scammed?"
One habit beats almost everything: **if someone contacts you and asks for money, information or a code, stop and contact them back yourself, using a number or website you look up independently.**

That one habit defeats fake banks, fake IRS calls, fake delivery texts, fake tech support and the grandparent scam, because they all depend on you using the channel they gave you.

Two more rules make it nearly complete:
- **Strange payment = scam.** Gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, cash by courier, or a payment app to a stranger. Legitimate organisations don't demand these.
- **Never share a one-time code** with anyone who contacted you.

And one free setup step: freeze your credit at the three bureaus.

Scammers win on urgency. Slowing down by five minutes is usually enough.

**Heads up: someone going door to door saying they're from the "energy program"**
They ask to see your last power bill to "check your rebate". Your bill has your account number, which can be used to switch your supplier without you realising. Some ask to come inside.
✅ You don't have to open the door.
✅ Ask for ID, then call the number on your own bill to check if anyone is in the area.
✅ Never sign anything on the doorstep.

And a free tip: you can get an IRS Identity Protection PIN so nobody else can file a return using your Social Security number. Search for it on IRS.gov.

### F11. Community group: spring clean your accounts
**10-minute account spring clean 🧹**
1. Give your email a password you use nowhere else (it resets everything else).
2. Turn on two-step sign-in for email and banking.

### F12. Local or seniors' group: prize calls
**"You've won!" calls: one simple rule**
If you have to pay anything to receive a prize (taxes, insurance, fees), it's a scam. Real prizes are never paid for, and you can't win a lottery you didn't enter.
If someone you love keeps getting these calls, help them block the numbers and keep talking about it. Scammers share lists of people who've answered before.
