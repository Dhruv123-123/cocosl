How to use this kit

Scams win on two things: panic and secrecy. A plan your family makes calmly, in advance, takes both away. You don't need to be good with technology to do any of this.

The 15-minute family meeting

1
Minutes 0–3: Why we're doing this. Scammers now clone voices, fake caller ID, and know personal details from data breaches. "It sounded just like you" isn't proof anymore. Nobody here is gullible; these are professionals.
2
Minutes 3–6: Pick the safe word (page 3). Say it out loud. Don't text it, post it or write it on the fridge card.
3
Minutes 6–9: Agree the three rules: call back, no strange payments, call our trusted person first (page 3).
4
Minutes 9–12: Walk through the Scam Decoder (page 6). Ask: "Has anyone had one of these?" Most families have.
5
Minutes 12–15: Hand out jobs. Who freezes whose credit this week (page 4), who sets up the fridge card (page 7), who checks Mom and Dad's phone settings (page 5).

Tip for adult children: lead with "help me protect you and the kids", not "you might fall for something". People act on plans they helped make.

Our family plan

Keep this page somewhere private (not on the fridge). Everyone in the plan should know every rule.

Rule 1 · Our safe word

Anyone calling or messaging in a panic about money must say it. If they can't, or dodge the question, we hang up and call back. Pick something not findable online (not a pet's name, street or school). Don't write the word itself here; write a hint only you'll understand.

Hint:   Agreed on (date):

Rule 2 · We always call back

Any urgent call or message about money, from "the bank", "the police", "Medicare" or "your grandson": we hang up and call back on a number we already have or look up ourselves. Caller ID can be faked.

Rule 3 · No strange payments, ever

Nobody in this family pays someone who contacted us with gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps to strangers, cryptocurrency, or cash handed to a courier. No real agency, bank or company asks for these.

Rule 4 · Call our trusted person first

Before sending money anyone asked for, we call one of these people. Even if we were told to keep it secret. Especially then.

Credit freeze tracker

A freeze stops lenders from pulling a credit report, so nobody can open new accounts in that name. It's free by federal law, doesn't change a credit score, and existing cards keep working.

How, for each adult

  1. Type each bureau's website yourself (don't click ads or links in messages): equifax.com, experian.com, transunion.com. Look for "credit freeze".
  2. Create an account and turn the freeze on. Save the login in a password manager or a safe place, never in email.
  3. Bonus: Innovis (a smaller fourth bureau) and ChexSystems (used for bank accounts) also offer free freezes.
  4. Applying for credit later? Ask which bureau the lender uses and lift only that one, for a day or two. Online lifts are processed quickly (by law, within an hour).

For children

A child's clean credit file is valuable to thieves and often goes unchecked for years. Parents and guardians can request a freeze for a child under 16 at each bureau; this usually needs proof of identity for both of you (mailed or uploaded documents). Teens 16+ can request their own.

Also free: your credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com (weekly). Check each report once a year for accounts you don't recognise.

Account lockdown checklist

Most account takeovers aren't clever hacking. They're reused passwords and tricked codes. These five locks stop both. Do one a night.

1 · Email first

  • Long, unique password (a short sentence works)
  • Strongest sign-in option turned on (passkey or authenticator app)
  • Recovery phone and email are ours and current
  • Signed out of old devices

2 · Different passwords

  • Password manager installed
  • Bank, email, phone carrier changed first
  • Old reused password retired everywhere

3 · Better than text codes

  • Passkeys turned on where offered
  • Authenticator app for the rest
  • Backup codes printed and stored safely

4 · Lock the phone number

  • Carrier account PIN set (not the phone unlock code)
  • Number lock / port-out protection on, if offered
  • We know: sudden "No service" = call the carrier from another phone

5 · Alerts on everything

  • Transaction alerts on every bank and card
  • New-login alerts on email and bank
  • Alerts go to a phone someone actually checks

Bonus · Devices

  • Automatic updates on (phone, computer, browser)
  • Screen lock on every phone
  • Remote-access apps removed unless we installed them

The one rule that beats most account takeovers: never read a one-time code to anyone who contacted you. Not "the bank", not "support", not a buyer online.

If it happens

The first 60 minutes

It happens to careful, smart people every day. Shame is what scammers count on to stop you acting. Speed matters more than anything; money can sometimes be stopped if you move fast (not always).

1
Call your bank or card issuer on the number on the back of your card. Say "I've been scammed", ask them to stop or reverse the payment and lock the card or account.
2
Call where the money went. Gift card: call the card company on the number on the card or its official site; unspent balances can sometimes be frozen. Payment app or wire: contact the app or wire service directly.
3
Change passwords, email first, from a device the scammer never touched. Sign out of all devices. If you allowed remote access, disconnect that computer from the internet first.
4
Freeze your credit at all three bureaus (page 4) if you shared personal details like your Social Security number.
5
Report it. ReportFraud.ftc.gov for scams. IdentityTheft.gov if your identity was used (it builds a recovery plan). ic3.gov for internet crime. Your local police if your bank or insurer asks for a report.
6
Write everything down on page 9: names, numbers, dates, amounts, how you paid.

Watch for the second scam. Anyone who contacts you offering to recover your money for a fee, including people claiming to be lawyers, investigators or government agents, is very likely another scammer.
