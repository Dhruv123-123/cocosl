# Part Five: The Year {.part .unnumbered}

# A Money Calendar for the Year {#calendar}
Most of the system in this book runs weekly and monthly. But a few money moves belong to particular times of year, and they're much easier if you do them before the moment arrives rather than during it. This chapter walks through the year, season by season, with one or two moves for each.

You don't need to do all of them. Pick the ones that apply to you, and put them in your calendar as recurring reminders now, so next year they find you.

## January: the hardest money month

January catches people out because three things arrive together: the holiday credit card statement, yearly renewals (subscriptions, memberships, insurance, car registration), and, for many people, a long gap between the last paycheck of December and the first one in January.

The fix is to plan January in December. Spend fifteen minutes in mid-December on three things:

1. **List the January renewals.** Look at last January's statements for anything annual. Each one should already have a sinking fund; if it doesn't, decide now where the money comes from.
2. **Estimate the holiday card bill.** If you set a holiday number (see November, below), you'll know roughly what's coming. If you didn't, add it up now, before the statement surprises you.
3. **Check the gap.** When is your last December paycheck, and when is your first January one? If the gap is longer than usual, make sure your buffer in bills checking covers it, and consider lowering the weekly number slightly for the first January week.

Then, in January itself, do your monthly reset with a little extra care. It's a natural moment to raise goals, start a savings challenge, or set up the bill audit from Chapter {{bill-audit}}.

## Early in the year: free credit reports and tax season

**Pull your credit reports.** Any time is fine, but tying it to a time of year means you'll actually do it. Many people do it in January or February, alongside taxes. The audit takes about ninety minutes (Chapter {{credit}}).

**Plan your tax refund before it arrives.** If you usually get a refund, decide where it goes before you see it in your account. A refund is a windfall that's easy to spend in small pieces without noticing. A common split is half to a goal (debt or the emergency fund) and half to something you'll enjoy, but any split you choose in advance beats no plan.

**IRA contributions for the previous year** can usually be made until the tax deadline in April. If you meant to contribute and didn't, there's still time.

## Spring: the three-month review

By April, you've done three monthly resets of the year. It's a good time for the three-month question from Chapter {{routine}}: *which single bill could I lower?* Spring is also when many people's car insurance or renters insurance renews, which makes it a natural moment to get quotes.

## Summer: travel, and the halfway check

**Travel season** is when sinking funds prove their worth. If you're planning a trip, the travel fund should already be filling up. If it isn't, use the formula from Chapter {{sinking-funds}}: cost ÷ months until the trip.

**At the halfway point of the year**, look at your debt total and your savings total and compare them with January. Progress is often slower than you hoped and faster than you feared. Either way, it's useful to see.

**The second bill audit** of the year is a good fit for early summer, six months after the first. The list will be shorter, and it'll take less time.

## Fall: benefits enrollment and planning ahead

**Open enrollment** for workplace benefits usually happens in the fall. It's the one time each year when you can change most of your health, dental and other benefit choices, so read the options rather than letting last year's choices roll over automatically. Check:

- whether a high-deductible plan with an HSA would suit you better or worse,
- how much to put in a flexible spending account (FSA) next year, based on what you actually used this year, and
- your retirement contribution, and whether you're getting the full employer match.

**Raise your retirement contribution by one percent.** Many plans take a pay cycle or two to apply a change, so doing it in November means it's in place for January's paychecks. If a raise is coming in the new year, the 1% disappears into it before you ever get used to the money.

## November: pick your holiday number

Before the sales start, decide one number for everything the holidays will cost you. Not just gifts: presents, travel, the extra food, the gift exchange you forgot about, the outfit for the party. One number.

Sales are designed to make each purchase look small and sensible on its own. A total you picked in advance is the only thing that sees all of them together. If you've ever looked at a January statement and wondered how it happened, this is usually how: thirty sensible purchases.

1. **List the people and the events.** Names, trips, dinners. Rough is fine.
2. **Put a number next to each**, and add them up.
3. **Check it against what you actually have.** Look at your gifts and holidays fund and at what's left in your monthly plan. If the total doesn't fit, shrink the list now, while it's a list and not a stack of receipts.
4. **Move the money** into a separate savings bucket and spend from there. When it's gone, the holidays are paid for, and anything else waits for January.

## December: money that expires

Some money disappears on December 31 if you don't use it. Spend ten minutes finding it.

- **A flexible spending account (FSA).** FSAs are generally "use it or lose it": money left at the end of the plan year can be forfeited. Some plans give a grace period into the new year or let you carry over a limited amount, so check your plan's rules. Things an FSA usually covers that people forget: prescription glasses and contact lenses, sunscreen, first-aid supplies, some over-the-counter medicines, and copays for appointments you were going to book anyway. An HSA is different: HSA money rolls over every year and stays yours.
- **Benefits you're already paying for.** Many health plans cover one or two dental cleanings and an eye exam each year, and unused visits don't roll over. Some employers offer wellness or learning stipends that reset in January.
- **Expiring credits and points.** Airline credits, gift cards, store credit and card rewards that are about to expire.

Use them on purpose, not in a panic on December 30.

## The last week of the year: three numbers

Before the new year, write down three numbers, and nothing else:

1. **What you have.** Add up your checking, savings, and any investment or retirement account balances.
2. **What you owe.** Add up every card balance, student loan, car loan, and anything else you're paying off.
3. **What you saved this year.** Rough is fine: how much went into savings, retirement or extra debt payments.

The first minus the second is your *net worth*. Don't judge it, especially if it's negative. That's normal early in a career, and student loans alone put many people there. The point is to have a starting line. Next December, you'll write the same three numbers down and see the difference.

No resolutions required. If you want one, make it small and automatic. A $25 monthly transfer to savings on payday beats a big plan you stop in February.

## The calendar, at a glance

| When | Move |
|---|---|
| January | Careful monthly reset; renewals; start the year's goals |
| January to April | Credit reports; plan the tax refund; prior-year IRA deadline |
| April | Three-month review; insurance quotes |
| Summer | Travel funds; halfway check; second bill audit |
| Fall | Open enrollment; raise retirement contribution 1% |
| November | Pick your holiday number |
| Mid-December | Plan January; use FSA and expiring benefits |
| Last week of December | Write down three numbers |

# Your First Thirty Days {#first-30}
You've read the book. Here's how to turn it into a system in a month, without trying to do everything at once. Each week has one main job and a few small ones. If a week gets away from you, carry its job into the next week and keep going.

## Week 1: See your money

- **Day 1 (one evening, about an hour):** gather two months of statements and find your take-home pay, your bills and your funds (Chapters {{real-numbers}} and {{sinking-funds}}).
- **Day 2:** choose your goals and work out your weekly number (Chapter {{weekly-number}}). Write the plan on one page.
- **Day 3 (ten minutes):** turn on a low-balance alert, a large-transaction alert and a payment-due alert on every account (Chapter {{autopilot}}).
- **Day 5:** set your weekly check-in as a recurring calendar event, twenty minutes, same day every week.

## Week 2: Put it on autopilot

- **Open a savings account** for your funds and emergency fund, and a weekly spending account if you want one (Chapter {{autopilot}}).
- **Schedule the automatic transfers** for payday: savings, goals, and the weekly number.
- **Move bill due dates** closer to payday where you can.
- **Do your first weekly check-in.** It might take thirty minutes this time. That's normal.

## Week 3: Cut

- **Do the bill audit** (Chapter {{bill-audit}}): find every recurring charge, decide, and cancel cleanly.
- **Make one negotiation call** (Chapter {{better-price}}). Pick the biggest bill you marked "negotiate."
- **Give the freed-up money a job** at your next monthly reset.
- **Weekly check-in.**

## Week 4: Clear and build

- **List your debts** and choose avalanche or snowball (Chapter {{debt}}). Set up autopay for every minimum, and schedule the extra payment for the day after payday.
- **Pull your three credit reports** and freeze your credit (Chapter {{credit}}). The full audit can wait until next month if you're short on time; the freeze is fifteen minutes.
- **If you're ready to invest**, check whether you're getting your full employer match, and if not, raise your contribution (Chapter {{investing}}).
- **Weekly check-in.**

## Day 30: Your first monthly reset

Sit down a few days before payday and run the twenty-minute reset from Chapter {{routine}}: review the weeks, adjust bills and funds, assign the leftovers, and confirm the transfers. Expect to change something. The first month's plan is always a draft, and the second is always better.

After that, the system is running. Twenty minutes a week, twenty minutes a month, and a handful of seasonal moves. That's the whole thing.

## If you only do three things

If thirty days feels like too much right now, do these three, in this order:

1. **Work out your weekly number**, and spend from it for a week.
2. **Set up one automatic transfer to savings** on payday, of any size.
3. **Cancel one subscription you don't use.**

Each one takes less than an hour. Together, they're the start of everything in this book.
