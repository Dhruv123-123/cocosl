# Part One: See Your Money {.part .unnumbered}

# The Four Lines {#four-lines}
If you've tried budgeting before and quit, here's something worth hearing: it probably wasn't you.

Most budgets fail for a boring reason. They ask you to make dozens of small decisions every day. Groceries, eating out, fun, household, transport, gifts, "miscellaneous." Every coffee is a decision, and every decision is a chance to feel you've failed. By the third week of the month the categories that looked generous on the first are empty, you can't tell where the money went, and opening the spreadsheet just feels bad. So you stop opening it.

That isn't a willpower problem. It's a design problem. A system that needs you to be disciplined forty times a day will lose to real life, which is busy and tiring and full of other things to think about.

Three things make a budget stick instead:

1. **Fewer decisions.** One number to watch, not twelve.
2. **A short feedback loop.** You check in weekly, so a bad week is a small problem rather than a ruined month.
3. **Automation for everything that doesn't change.** Bills and savings move on their own, without needing you to remember or resist anything.

The system in this book is built on those three ideas. It asks for one decision a month and one check-in a week.

## Every dollar goes on one of four lines

Take your monthly take-home pay, the amount that actually lands in your account. Every dollar of it goes on one of four lines.

**Line 1: Bills.** Your fixed, recurring costs: rent or mortgage, utilities, phone, internet, insurance, subscriptions, and the minimum payment on every debt. They're roughly the same every month and they have to be paid.

**Line 2: Funds.** Money set aside every month for costs that are predictable but not monthly: car repairs and registration, birthdays and holidays, annual subscriptions, medical copays, clothes, travel. These are usually called *sinking funds*, and Chapter {{sinking-funds}} covers them in detail. They are the line most budgets forget, and forgetting them is the single biggest reason budgets "mysteriously" fail.

**Line 3: Goals.** Emergency savings, extra debt payments and investing. The important word here is *first*. Goals are paid at the start of the month, not out of whatever happens to be left at the end. If you wait to see what's left, the answer is usually nothing.

**Line 4: Weekly.** Everything else. Groceries, gas, eating out, coffee, fun, the little stuff. One amount per week. You spend it however you like.

The first three lines are decided once a month and then run on autopilot. Only the fourth needs your attention during the month, and it's a single number.

## Why a weekly number works

It would be simpler, on paper, to have a monthly spending number. In practice, a month is too long to steer.

**A week is short enough to correct.** If you overspend on Tuesday, you see it by Sunday and you have a whole new week to adjust. With a monthly number, you find out on the twenty-eighth, when there's nothing left to do but feel bad.

**One number is easy to remember.** "I have $61 left this week" is something you can hold in your head at the checkout. "I have $212 left in groceries, $38 in eating out and $14 in fun" isn't.

**There's no category policing.** Groceries, eating out and entertainment all come out of the same weekly pot. You decide the trade-offs in the moment, which is where they really happen anyway. Want dinner out on Friday? Then it's a cheaper grocery run this week. That's your decision to make, and the system doesn't need to know about it.

**Overspending is contained.** A bad week can't eat your rent, because rent was never in the weekly pot. The worst that can happen is that next week is a bit tighter.

## The one rule that makes it work

> Bills, Funds and Goals are paid first and automatically. You only ever watch the Weekly number. If the Weekly number is too small to live on, you change the plan at the monthly reset, not halfway through a week.

That last part matters. The plan is allowed to change, and it should, as your life changes. But it changes once a month, on purpose, when you're sitting down with the numbers. It doesn't change in the checkout line.

## Is this a new idea?

No, and that's a good thing. The four-line plan combines two old, well-tested ideas.

The first is *zero-based budgeting*: every dollar gets a job before the month starts, so nothing is left floating around waiting to be spent by accident. The second is *paying yourself first*: savings and goals come out before spending, not after.

What's different here is that the day-to-day part shrinks to a single figure and a twenty-minute weekly habit. You don't log every purchase. You don't sort transactions into categories. You don't need an app, although you can use one if you like. You need a few accounts, a few automatic transfers and one number.

## What you'll need

Before you start the next chapter, gather:

- Your last **two months** of bank and credit card statements (or log in to the accounts so you can scroll through them).
- A recent **pay stub**, or the amount of your last few paychecks.
- A notebook, a spreadsheet, or the back of an envelope. Anything that adds up numbers will do.
- About **an hour** you won't be interrupted.

The setup takes one evening. After that, the routine takes twenty minutes a week.

## The four lines, at a glance

| Line | What's in it | How often you touch it |
|---|---|---|
| Bills | Rent, utilities, phone, insurance, subscriptions, debt minimums | Once a month, at the reset |
| Funds | Car, gifts, holidays, annual renewals, medical, clothes, travel | Once a month, at the reset |
| Goals | Emergency fund, extra debt payments, investing | Once a month, at the reset |
| Weekly | Groceries, gas, eating out, fun, everything else | Twenty minutes a week |

# Find Your Real Numbers {#real-numbers}
A budget built on guesses fails quickly, because guesses are almost always too optimistic. Most people underestimate what they spend by a surprising amount, not because they're careless but because small, irregular costs are easy to forget. This chapter replaces guesses with real numbers from your own statements.

There are three steps: find your take-home pay, list every fixed bill, and add them up.

## Step 1: Your monthly take-home pay

Use what actually lands in your account after tax and deductions, not your salary. Your salary is a number on a contract. Your take-home pay is the money you have to work with.

If you're paid monthly, that's simple. If you're paid more often, convert one typical paycheck to a monthly figure like this:

| If you're paid... | Monthly take-home is... |
|---|---|
| Weekly | paycheck × 52 ÷ 12 |
| Every two weeks | paycheck × 26 ÷ 12 |
| Twice a month | paycheck × 2 |
| Monthly | paycheck |

Notice that "every two weeks" and "twice a month" aren't the same. Every two weeks gives you 26 paychecks a year, which is two more than twice a month. That's why you multiply by 26 and divide by 12, rather than just doubling it. We'll come back to those "extra" paychecks in Chapter {{autopilot}}, because they're a gift if you plan for them and they vanish if you don't.

**If your income varies**, because you freelance, work for tips or earn commission, look at your last six months and use your *lowest normal month*. Ignore a true outlier, like the month you were sick for two weeks, but don't use your average. Average months are a trap: half of all months are below average. Chapter {{life}} explains how to handle the extra money in good months.

Write down your monthly take-home pay. That's the top of your plan.

## Step 2: List every fixed bill

Now go through your statements line by line and write down every cost that repeats every month at roughly the same amount. Be thorough. This is the part of the process that pays off most, because it's where forgotten charges show up.

Include:

- **Housing:** rent or mortgage, renters or home insurance, HOA fees.
- **Utilities:** electricity, water, gas, internet, phone.
- **Transport:** car payment, car insurance, transit pass, parking.
- **Debt minimums:** the minimum payment on every credit card and loan. (Extra payments don't go here; they're a Goal.)
- **Subscriptions and memberships:** streaming, apps, cloud storage, gym, software, delivery memberships.
- **Anything else that comes out every month:** childcare, tuition payments, regular support you pay to a family member.

For bills that wobble with the seasons, like electricity in summer and gas in winter, use a slightly high average so you're not short in the expensive months. If a bill was $90 in spring and $160 in July, call it $130, not $110.

**A shortcut:** search your statements for words like *monthly*, *subscription*, *renewal*, *membership* and *autopay*. It catches most recurring charges in a few minutes. If you find subscriptions you'd forgotten about, write them down anyway. You'll deal with them in Chapter {{bill-audit}}, and it's satisfying to see the total drop.

## Step 3: Add them up and sanity-check

Total your bills, then work out what share of your take-home pay they take:

> bills share = total bills ÷ monthly take-home pay

There's no magic percentage, and anyone who tells you your rent "should" be exactly 30% of your income hasn't seen rents lately. But the number tells you something useful about where your effort should go.

- **Below about 50%:** you have room. The weekly number will probably feel comfortable, and your goals can be generous.
- **About 50% to 60%:** normal for many people, especially in expensive cities. The plan will work, but it'll be worth trimming bills over time.
- **Above about 60%:** your weekly number is going to feel tight, and the biggest wins will come from the bills themselves, not from skipping lattes. Housing, the car, the phone plan and insurance quotes are where real money moves. Chapters {{bill-audit}} and {{better-price}} are your priority.

This is worth saying plainly, because a lot of money advice gets it wrong: if your fixed costs are high, cutting small daily pleasures won't fix it. A $4 coffee three times a week is about $52 a month. One phone call to your internet provider can sometimes save half that, every month, without you giving up anything. Big, boring bills first; small, joyful spending last.

## What you have now

At the end of this chapter you should have two numbers written down:

1. Your **monthly take-home pay**.
2. Your **monthly bills total**, with a list of what's in it.

Don't worry yet about whether the plan works. There are two more pieces before you can tell: the costs that aren't monthly, and the goals you want to pay first.

# Sinking Funds: The Expenses That Aren't Surprises {#sinking-funds}
Here is a pattern most people recognize. The budget looks good on paper. Then the car needs new tires. Then it's a friend's wedding. Then the annual software subscription renews, and it's December, and there are gifts to buy. Every month has "one unusual expense," and every month the budget breaks.

The trick is to notice that none of these expenses are unusual. The car will need tires; you just don't know which month. People will get married and have birthdays. December comes every year. These costs are *predictable but not monthly*, and a budget that only plans for monthly costs will be wrong every single month.

Sinking funds fix this. A sinking fund is money you set aside a little at a time, every month, for a cost you know is coming. When the cost arrives, the money is already there. It stops being an emergency and becomes a withdrawal.

## Find your funds

Go back through the last twelve months of statements, or think through the year month by month, and list the costs that didn't come every month but were completely foreseeable. Common ones:

- **Car:** maintenance, tires, registration, inspection, the deductible if you have an accident.
- **Gifts and holidays:** birthdays, December holidays, weddings, baby showers, the office collection.
- **Annual and quarterly renewals:** software, memberships, domain names, warehouse clubs, insurance paid yearly.
- **Medical:** copays, prescriptions, dental work, glasses.
- **Clothes and shoes:** especially for growing kids.
- **Travel:** the summer trip, visiting family, flights home for the holidays.
- **Home:** appliance repairs, furniture, the security deposit for your next apartment.
- **Pets:** vet visits, vaccinations, boarding.

Each one becomes a fund.

## How much to set aside

For a cost that comes up throughout the year:

> monthly amount = expected yearly cost ÷ 12

For a specific, dated cost, like a trip in August or car registration in May:

> monthly amount = cost ÷ months until it's due

So if you spend about $600 a year on gifts, that's $50 a month into the gifts fund. If a $900 trip is seven months away, that's about $129 a month until then, and then the fund either closes or rolls into next year's trip.

If you've never tracked these costs, your first year's numbers will be guesses. That's fine. Make a sensible guess, round it up a little, and adjust at each monthly reset as you learn what things really cost.

## Where to keep the money

Sinking fund money should sit somewhere separate from your everyday checking account, so it doesn't get spent by accident. The simplest setup is one savings account that holds all your funds, with a note (or a spreadsheet) that tracks how much of the balance belongs to each fund.

Many banks now let you split a single savings account into named "buckets" or "goals." If yours does, give each fund its own bucket. It's a small thing, but seeing "Car: $340" and "Gifts: $415" makes it far easier to spend from the right place and far harder to raid one fund for something else.

A high-yield savings account is a good home for this money, because the balance can be several hundred or a few thousand dollars and it might as well earn some interest. It doesn't belong in investments: you'll need it within the year, and you can't have the car fund drop 20% the month before the brakes go.

## How a sinking fund works in practice

Say your car fund gets $40 a month. In March you need two new tires, which cost $260. You've built up $280 by then.

1. You pay for the tires with your debit card (or your credit card, which you pay off in full).
2. At your weekly check-in, you move $260 from the car fund to checking to cover it.
3. You update the fund's balance: $280 − $260 = $20.
4. The $40 a month keeps going in, and the fund starts rebuilding.

Notice what didn't happen: the tires didn't touch your weekly number, your bills or your goals. Nothing broke.

If a fund runs out, say the car needs a $700 repair and the fund has $300, you have options at the next reset: pause another fund for a month, take it from the emergency fund and refill it, or lower a goal temporarily. What you don't do is pretend the extra $400 will come out of the weekly number without a plan. That's how the card balance starts growing.

## Funds versus the emergency fund

People often ask how sinking funds are different from an emergency fund. It's a fair question.

**Sinking funds** are for things you know will happen: the car will need maintenance, December will arrive. **The emergency fund** is for things you can't predict and might not happen: losing your job, a medical bill, a burst pipe, an emergency flight. You hope never to use it.

Keeping them separate protects the emergency fund. If the emergency fund also has to cover Christmas and tires, it's never really there when the real emergency arrives.

## An example

Here's how Maya, who appears throughout this book, sets up her funds. Maya is 26, rents a one-bedroom apartment and takes home $3,500 a month. (She's fictional; the numbers are realistic.)

| Fund | Expected per year | Per month |
|---|---|---|
| Car maintenance | $480 | $40 |
| Gifts and holidays | $600 | $50 |
| Travel | $480 | $40 |
| Clothing | $360 | $30 |
| Medical and copays | $300 | $25 |
| Annual renewals | $180 | $15 |
| **Total** | **$2,400** | **$200** |

That's $200 a month, or $2,400 a year, that Maya's old budget simply didn't account for. No wonder it broke.
