# Your Credit Report {#credit}
Your credit reports affect a lot: the interest rate on a car loan or mortgage, whether a landlord accepts your application, sometimes your insurance prices and the deposit a utility asks for. Mistakes on credit reports are common enough that it's worth checking yours every year. This chapter turns that into a ninety-minute job.

It covers US consumer credit reports. Rules and bureau policies change, so check the official sources named here, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and AnnualCreditReport.com, for the current version.

## Get all three reports, free

There are three nationwide credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. Each keeps its own report on you, and they don't always match. Check all three.

**AnnualCreditReport.com** is the site set up under federal law for free credit reports. You can get a free report from each bureau there, and at the time of writing the bureaus offer them every week. Type the address yourself; look-alike sites exist. You don't need a credit card, and you don't need to sign up for anything. If a site asks you to pay or start a free trial, you're on the wrong site.

1. **Request all three at once**, and download each as a PDF. Save them in a folder named with today's date.
2. **Expect identity questions** about past addresses or loans. If you can't get through online, you can request your reports by phone or mail through the same site.
3. **Skip the upsells.** You don't need to buy a score, monitoring or a paid "credit lock" to get your reports.

Checking your own report is a "soft" inquiry. It does not lower your score.

### What's in a report

- **Personal information:** names, addresses, date of birth, sometimes employers. It isn't used to calculate your score, but wrong entries can be a sign of a mixed-up file or identity theft.
- **Accounts (sometimes called tradelines):** every credit card and loan, with the lender, the date it was opened, the limit or original amount, the balance, the status, and a month-by-month payment history.
- **Collections:** debts that have been sold or assigned to collection agencies.
- **Public records:** mainly bankruptcies.
- **Inquiries:** "hard" inquiries from applications for credit, and "soft" ones, like pre-approved offers or your own checks, which don't affect scores.

What's *not* in your report: your credit score, your income, your bank balances, and usually your rent and utility payments, unless a service reports them. Your score is calculated from the report, which is why fixing the report matters more than chasing the score.

## The line-by-line audit (45 minutes)

Go through one report at a time with a highlighter. Mark anything that's wrong, missing, or that you don't recognize. Don't dispute anything yet; just mark.

**Personal information**

- The name spellings and variations are yours.
- Every address is one you've lived at.
- Your date of birth and Social Security number digits are right.
- There are no accounts belonging to a relative with a similar name, which is a common Jr. and Sr. mix-up.

**Each account**

- You recognize the account and the lender.
- It's listed once, not duplicated.
- The open date and credit limit are right.
- The balance roughly matches your latest statement. (Reports lag by about a month.)
- The status is right: open, closed, "closed by consumer," or paid.
- There are no late payments in months you paid on time.
- Paid-off loans show a $0 balance.
- Debts discharged in bankruptcy show as discharged.

**Collections**

- You recognize the original debt, and the amount is right.
- The same debt isn't listed twice, with both the original lender and a collector showing a balance.
- Paid collections show as paid.
- Nothing is older than about seven years from the original delinquency.

**Inquiries**

- Every hard inquiry is from something you applied for.
- None are older than two years.

**Across all three reports**

- An account that's missing from one bureau usually isn't an error. Lenders don't have to report to all three.
- The same account showing different late payments on different reports is a sign one of them is likely wrong.

### Red flags for identity theft

Accounts you didn't open, inquiries from lenders you never contacted, or addresses you've never lived at. If you see any of these, skip ahead to "If you find accounts that aren't yours," later in this chapter, and deal with that before anything else.

## Errors versus real negatives

The law lets you dispute information that's inaccurate or incomplete. Accurate negative information generally stays on your report for a set time and then falls off on its own. Knowing which is which saves you weeks of wasted effort.

- **A late payment you didn't make late:** dispute it, with proof such as a bank statement or payment confirmation.
- **An accurate late payment:** stays up to seven years. You can't dispute it, but you can ask the lender for a "goodwill" removal (there's a letter for this below). They don't have to agree.
- **A collection that isn't yours, or is for the wrong amount:** dispute it with the bureau and the collector.
- **An accurate collection:** stays up to seven years from the original delinquency. Paying it may still help with lenders, and some newer scoring models ignore paid collections.
- **Medical collections:** the three bureaus have said they no longer report paid medical collections, or medical collections under $500. If one appears, dispute it.
- **A hard inquiry you didn't authorize:** dispute it, and check for identity theft.
- **A hard inquiry you did authorize:** shows for two years, and affects scores less over time. You can't dispute it.
- **A Chapter 7 bankruptcy:** stays up to ten years. You can only dispute it if the details are wrong.
- **Wrong personal information:** dispute it. It's a quick fix and worth doing.

**Prioritize** in this order: identity theft first, then wrong late payments and collections, then wrong balances or limits (because they affect your utilization), then personal information and old inquiries.

### About credit repair companies

Anything a credit repair company can legally do, you can do yourself for free. Under federal law (the Credit Repair Organizations Act), they can't charge you before they've done the work, and nobody can legally remove accurate, current negative information. Be wary of anyone who promises a specific score, tells you to dispute everything on your report, or suggests creating a "new credit identity." That last one is illegal.

## How to dispute, step by step

You can dispute with the credit bureau that shows the error, with the company that reported it (called the "furnisher"), or both. Start with the bureau.

1. **Gather proof:** statements, payment confirmations, letters, a paid-in-full letter, or an identity theft report. Send copies only; never send originals.
2. **Choose online or mail.** Each bureau has an online dispute center on its own website, which is fastest and lets you upload documents. Mail, sent certified with a return receipt, gives you the strongest paper trail and lets you explain in your own words.
3. **One item, one clear sentence.** For example: *"Account ending 4821 shows a 30-day late payment in March 2026. I paid on time on March 3. Proof attached."* Specific beats long.
4. **Write it down:** the date you sent it, how you sent it, and which bureau.
5. **Wait for the result.** Bureaus generally have thirty days to investigate, and it can be up to forty-five in some cases, for example if you send more information during the investigation. They must send you the results, and a free updated report if anything changed.
6. **If it comes back "verified" and you still think it's wrong:** dispute directly with the furnisher, send any new evidence to the bureau, ask the bureau to add a short statement to your file, and consider submitting a complaint to the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint.

Get the current dispute address and online link from each bureau's own website. Search for "[bureau name] dispute," and make sure you're on equifax.com, experian.com or transunion.com. Addresses change, so this book doesn't print them.

Keep a paper trail: save confirmation numbers, screenshots, certified mail receipts and every letter you get back, all in the same dated folder as your reports.

### A dispute letter to a credit bureau

Fill in the brackets, delete what doesn't apply, and attach copies of your proof, your ID and proof of your address.

> | [Your full name]
> | [Your address]
> | [Date of birth] · [Last four digits of your Social Security number]
> | [Date]
>
> | [Credit bureau name]
> | [Dispute address from the bureau's website]
>
> Re: Dispute of inaccurate information, report number [if shown]
>
> To whom it may concern,
>
> I am writing to dispute the following information in my credit file. The items I dispute are also circled on the attached copy of my report.
>
> 1. [Company name], account ending [last four digits]. This account shows [describe exactly what is wrong]. This is inaccurate because [reason]. I am enclosing [describe proof].
>
> I request that these items be corrected [or deleted], and that you send me written results of your investigation and an updated copy of my credit report.
>
> Enclosures: [list each document]
>
> | Sincerely,
> | [Signature and printed name]

This follows the structure of the sample dispute letters published by the FTC and the CFPB, which you can also find on consumer.ftc.gov and consumerfinance.gov.

### A goodwill letter, for an accurate late payment

This is a polite request, not a dispute. The lender doesn't have to agree, and many won't, but it costs a stamp. It works best when the late payment was a one-off and your account is otherwise in good standing.

> I've been a customer since [year] and I value the account. In [month and year] my payment was late because [one honest sentence, for example "I changed banks and my autopay didn't transfer"]. I paid as soon as I noticed, and [I have paid on time since / I have set up autopay].
>
> I understand the late payment was reported accurately. I'm writing to ask whether you would consider a goodwill adjustment to remove it from my credit reports, as it doesn't reflect my usual payment history. Thank you for considering it.

## Freeze your credit (15 minutes)

A credit freeze stops most lenders from seeing your report, which stops most new accounts from being opened in your name. It's free, it doesn't affect your score, and you can lift it whenever you need to apply for credit.

- **Freeze at all three bureaus.** A freeze at one doesn't cover the others. Search each bureau's website for "credit freeze."
- **It's free by federal law**, both to place and to lift. Decline any paid "lock" product you're offered along the way; you don't need it to freeze.
- **Lifting is quick.** Requests made online or by phone generally have to be processed within an hour. You can lift for a set period while you apply for a card, loan or apartment, and then it re-freezes on its own.
- **Keep the login or PIN safe** in a password manager.
- **A freeze doesn't stop existing lenders** from using your report, and it doesn't stop misuse of accounts you already have. Keep checking your statements.

A **fraud alert** is a lighter option. It's free, and it tells lenders to take extra steps to verify it's you. Place it with one bureau and that bureau must tell the other two. A standard alert lasts a year. A freeze is stronger; an alert is easier while you're actively applying for credit.

### If you find accounts that aren't yours

Go to IdentityTheft.gov, which is run by the FTC. It walks you through a recovery plan and creates an Identity Theft Report you can send to bureaus and lenders, which gives your disputes extra weight. Then freeze your credit at all three bureaus, and dispute each fraudulent item.

## Utilization: the fastest thing you can change

After payment history, how much of your credit card limits you're using, called *utilization*, is one of the biggest factors in most credit scores. It's also the fastest to change, because it's based on the balances that are being reported right now.

> utilization = total card balances ÷ total card limits
>
> for example, $1,200 ÷ $4,000 = 30%

- **Lower is generally better.** A common rule of thumb is to stay under 30%, and people with the highest scores are often under 10%. It's looked at both overall and card by card.
- **The balance that's reported is usually your statement balance**, not what you owe on the due date. Paying part of the balance before the statement closes lowers the number that gets reported.
- **Closing an old card removes its limit**, which can push your utilization up. If it has no annual fee, keeping it open and using it occasionally is often better.
- **Utilization has no long memory** in most scoring models. Once balances drop and are reported, the effect usually follows within a month or two.

## Your ninety-day plan

- **Day 1:** Pull all three reports. Freeze your credit at all three bureaus. Set autopay for at least the minimum on every card and loan, because late payments are the most damaging item on a report.
- **Week 1:** Do the audit. Send disputes for the highest-priority errors. Write down what you sent and when.
- **Week 2:** Work out your utilization, card by card. Pick a target, and plan payments before each statement date.
- **Days 30 to 45:** Check dispute results. Re-dispute, or go to the furnisher, if needed. Send any goodwill letters.
- **Day 90:** Pull fresh reports and confirm the corrections. Set a reminder to do the audit again in six to twelve months.

Fixing errors stops your report working against you. Paying down balances, the subject of Part Three, is what moves it forward.

# Start Investing With $100 a Month {#investing}
You don't need a lot of money, or a finance degree, to start investing. You need four things: the right account, a simple fund, a low fee and an automatic transfer. This chapter gets you all four, in plain English, without stock picks or hype.

It uses US accounts and 2026 contribution limits. Limits and tax rules change each year, so check IRS.gov for the current figures. Investing involves risk, including the risk of losing money. The returns in this chapter's examples are assumptions for illustration, not predictions or promises, and nothing here is a recommendation to buy a particular investment.

## Three checks first

Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years. Before you start, make sure investing won't push you into expensive debt the first time something goes wrong.

1. **No high-interest debt growing faster than investments could.** A credit card at 25% APR costs you 25% a year, every year, for certain. No investment reliably beats that. Pay those debts off first, with one exception: always take an employer retirement match, which is explained below.
2. **A starter emergency fund.** Many people start with $500 to $1,000 in a separate savings account, then build toward three to six months of essential costs. Without it, a surprise bill means selling investments at a bad time, or borrowing on a card.
3. **A monthly amount you can keep up.** $100 every month for years beats $1,000 once and then nothing. Pick an amount you won't need to stop. $25 or $50 is a fine start.

**What about low-interest debt?** For debts with lower rates, like many car loans, federal student loans or a mortgage, it's common to invest while paying them on schedule. There's no single right answer; it depends on the rate and on how you feel about debt. A rough rule many people use: below about 5%, investing alongside is reasonable; above about 8%, paying the debt down first usually wins; in between is a judgment call.

**Where the $100 comes from.** If you don't have a spare $100 yet, it usually comes from one of three places: a cancelled subscription or two (Chapter {{bill-audit}}), a cap on weekly spending (Chapter {{weekly-number}}), or a debt that just got paid off, where you keep making the payment, to yourself. Then automate it, so it leaves before you see it.

## What investing actually is

When you buy a fund, you own tiny slices of hundreds or thousands of companies, or bonds. As those companies earn money and grow, your slices tend to become worth more over long periods, though with big ups and downs along the way. You're not trying to guess which company will win. You're owning nearly all of them, cheaply, for a long time.

## The order: where each dollar goes first

Here's a common order of priorities. It's a guideline, not a law, but it puts the most valuable moves first. You don't need to finish one step to the last dollar before thinking about the next.

1. **Your workplace plan, a 401(k) or 403(b), up to the full employer match.** If your employer matches your contributions, the match is part of your pay. Not taking it is turning down money.
2. **Pay off high-interest debt**: credit cards, store cards, payday loans, anything above roughly 8% to 10%.
3. **An emergency fund** of three to six months of essential costs, in a high-yield savings account. More if your income is irregular.
4. **An IRA, Roth or Traditional.** It has lots of fund choices and low costs. For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older, across all your IRAs combined.
5. **More in your workplace plan, beyond the match.** The 2026 employee contribution limit is $24,500, higher if you're 50 or older.
6. **A regular, taxable brokerage account** for anything beyond that, or for goals that come before retirement.

### Starting with $100 a month?

If your employer offers a match, your first $100 goes to the 401(k) through payroll, up to whatever earns the full match. If there's no match, or no 401(k), open a Roth IRA and set up a $100 automatic monthly contribution. That's it. That's the whole first step.

### Have a high-deductible health plan?

A Health Savings Account (HSA) can be worth a look. Contributions can be tax-deductible, growth isn't taxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical costs are tax-free. Many people put it right after the 401(k) match. Check your plan's fees and fund options.

## The accounts, explained

An account is the container. What you buy inside it is separate, and comes next. Picking the right container is mostly about taxes.

**401(k) or 403(b).** Your employer offers it, and you join through payroll. With the traditional version, you get a tax break now and pay tax when you withdraw. Many plans also offer a Roth option. The fund menu is limited, so check the fees, and note that an employer match may "vest," meaning become fully yours, over a few years.

**Roth IRA.** You open it yourself, at any brokerage. There's no tax break now, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Income limits apply. You can take out your contributions, but not the earnings, at any time without tax or penalty.

**Traditional IRA.** You open it yourself. Contributions may be tax-deductible now, and you pay tax when you withdraw. Whether you can deduct them depends on your income and whether you have a workplace plan.

**Taxable brokerage account.** You open it yourself. There's no special tax break; you pay tax on dividends and on gains when you sell. There are no limits and no withdrawal rules, which makes it good for goals before retirement age.

### Roth or Traditional: the one question

> Do you expect your tax rate to be higher now, or in retirement?

- **Lower now than later**, which is common early in your career or if you expect to earn more: Roth usually wins. You pay the tax at today's lower rate.
- **Higher now than later**, which is common in your peak earning years: Traditional usually wins. You take the break now, and pay a lower rate later.
- **No idea:** splitting between both is a perfectly reasonable hedge.

If your tax rate is exactly the same now and later, both come out equal.

For 2026, the amount you can contribute directly to a Roth IRA phases out between about $153,000 and $168,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers, and between $242,000 and $252,000 for married couples filing jointly. Above that, ask a tax professional about your options. IRA contributions for a year can be made until the tax deadline the following April. Roth earnings are tax-free after age 59½, once the account is at least five years old; IRS Publication 590-B has the details.

## What to actually buy

Opening the account is step one. Money sitting in it as cash isn't invested. You have to buy something, and for most beginners, simple is best. Three simple options:

**A target-date fund.** One fund, picked by the year you expect to retire, for example a "2065" fund. It holds a mix of stock and bond index funds, and it gets more cautious automatically as that year approaches. It's the simplest option there is.

**A total market index fund.** It owns nearly every public company in the US, or in the world, for a "total world" fund. Add a bond index fund if you want a steadier ride.

**A three-fund portfolio.** A US stock index fund, an international stock index fund and a bond index fund, in percentages you choose and rebalance once a year.

### Index funds, ETFs and mutual funds

- **An index fund follows a list of companies** (an index) instead of paying a manager to pick. That keeps costs low, and over long periods most actively managed funds have trailed their index after fees.
- **ETF versus mutual fund is mostly a difference in packaging.** ETFs trade like a stock during the day; mutual funds trade once a day and often allow automatic investing in exact dollar amounts. Both can be index funds.
- **In a 401(k)**, look down the menu for a target-date fund or an index fund with a low expense ratio. If your plan automatically put you in something, check what it is.

### How to check a fund in two minutes

- What does it hold? Look for the word "index" and what it tracks.
- What's the expense ratio? Ideally well under 0.20% for an index fund.
- Is there a sales charge (a "load") or an account fee? Avoid both.
- For a target-date fund, is the year roughly when you'll retire?

### What this chapter doesn't cover

Individual stocks, crypto, options and anything that needs watching daily. They aren't needed to build long-term wealth, and they're where many beginners lose money. If you want to dabble, many people cap it at a small slice, say 5% of their investments, and only money they could afford to lose entirely.

## Fees: the small number that costs the most

A fund's *expense ratio* is the percentage of your money it charges every year. It looks tiny. Over decades it isn't, because the fee comes out of money that would otherwise have kept growing.

> 0.04% of $10,000 is $4 a year. 1.00% of $10,000 is $100 a year.

Here's an example: $200 a month for thirty years, assuming the investments themselves earn 7% a year before fees. (That's an assumption for illustration, not a forecast.)

| Fund's yearly fee | Ending value | Lost to fees vs 0.04% |
|---|---|---|
| 0.04% (a typical broad index fund) | $242,077 | — |
| 0.20% | $234,581 | $7,496 |
| 1.00% (common for actively managed funds or some advisers) | $200,903 | $41,174 |

Same deposits, $72,000 in total, and the same market. The only difference is the fee, and it's worth about $41,000.

Other fees to watch:

- **Account or maintenance fees.** Most large brokerages charge none for standard accounts.
- **Trading commissions.** Most large brokerages now charge $0 to trade stocks and ETFs online.
- **Sales loads.** A percentage taken when you buy or sell some mutual funds. Avoid them.
- **Advice fees.** Often around 1% of your balance a year for a human adviser. That can be worth it for complicated situations, but it's the same drag as the table above. Robo-advisers usually charge less, often around 0.25%. For a simple plan, a target-date fund does a similar job inside the fund.
- **401(k) plan fees.** Look for your plan's annual fee disclosure, and pick the lowest-cost funds on the menu.

## Open the account and automate it (about 30 minutes)

When you choose where to open an IRA or brokerage account, look for:

- no account fees and no minimum to open,
- low-cost index funds or ETFs, with no commissions,
- automatic recurring investments, ideally straight into the fund and not just into cash,
- fractional shares, so $100 buys exactly $100 of a fund, and
- SIPC membership, which protects you if the brokerage fails (not against market losses).

Large, long-established brokerages such as Fidelity, Charles Schwab and Vanguard meet most of these for standard accounts. Compare their current terms yourself. This book isn't affiliated with any of them.

### Step by step

1. **Open the account.** Choose "Roth IRA" or "Traditional IRA" specifically; a general "brokerage account" is the taxable kind. Have your ID, Social Security number, employer details and bank details ready.
2. **Link your bank and make your first contribution.** Choose the tax year if you're asked.
3. **Invest the cash.** Search for your chosen fund and buy it. This is the step people forget: money you transfer in sits as cash until you buy something.
4. **Set up the automatic plan:** a monthly transfer from your bank and an automatic investment into the fund, for the day after payday.
5. **Name a beneficiary.** It takes two minutes, and it keeps the account out of a long legal process if the worst happens.

**For your 401(k):** log in to your benefits portal, set your contribution percentage to at least enough for the full match, choose your funds, and turn on automatic annual increases if your plan offers them, for example 1% a year. You'll barely notice each increase, and over time they add up to a much bigger habit.

### Raise it by one percent

If you already contribute to a workplace plan, one of the easiest moves you can make is to raise your contribution by one percentage point, from 4% to 5%, say. Traditional 401(k) contributions come out before income tax, so a 1% contribution shrinks your take-home pay by *less* than 1%. On a $50,000 salary, 1% is $500 a year, about $19 a paycheck if you're paid every two weeks; after tax, your paycheck might drop by something closer to $15. Your plan's website usually shows the effect on your paycheck before you confirm. If a raise is coming, time the increase to match it, and you'll never notice the difference.

## What $100 a month can grow into

Nobody knows future returns. So here's $100 a month at three assumed average yearly returns, to show the range. Real returns vary a lot from year to year, and some years are negative.

| After | You put in | At 4% a year | At 6% a year | At 8% a year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 years | $12,000 | $14,725 | $16,388 | $18,295 |
| 20 years | $24,000 | $36,677 | $46,204 | $58,902 |
| 30 years | $36,000 | $69,405 | $100,452 | $149,036 |
| 40 years | $48,000 | $118,196 | $199,149 | $349,101 |

These assume monthly contributions and monthly compounding, with no fees, taxes or adjustment for inflation. They're illustrations only, not a prediction or a promise of any return.

Two lessons. First, **time does most of the work**: in the later decades, growth adds far more than your deposits do. Second, **raising the amount matters**: increasing your monthly contribution a little each year, by 3% say, makes a big difference over decades.

## When the market falls (it will)

Historically, stock market drops of 10% or more have come roughly every couple of years on average, with bigger crashes from time to time. If you invest for decades, you will see several. Here's what to do:

- **Keep the automatic investment running.** Your $100 buys more shares when prices are low.
- **Don't sell to "wait it out."** Many of the best days in the market come right after the worst ones, and they're impossible to time.
- **Check less often.** Once or twice a year is plenty.
- **If a drop would keep you up at night, your mix may be too aggressive.** Adjust it calmly, with more bonds or an earlier target-date year, rather than selling everything.
- **Money you'll need within about five years doesn't belong in stocks.** Keep it in savings.

It helps to decide in advance what you'll do. Write it down now: *"When the market drops 20%, I will..."* For most people, the right answer is "keep my automatic investment running and not log in for a while."

## Common questions

**Is $100 a month even worth it?** Yes. It builds the habit and the system, and the table above shows it adds up. You can raise the amount any time; the setup stays the same.

**Should I wait for the market to drop before I start?** Nobody can reliably predict that. Investing a fixed amount every month, called *dollar-cost averaging*, takes the guessing out of it.

**What if I need the money?** That's what the emergency fund is for. With a Roth IRA, your contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn without tax or penalty, which is a backstop, not a plan. Early withdrawals from a 401(k) or Traditional IRA usually mean tax plus a 10% penalty, with some exceptions.

**What happens to my 401(k) if I change jobs?** The vested part is yours. You can usually leave it where it is, move it to your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Avoid cashing it out.

**I'm self-employed, or my job has no 401(k). Now what?** Start with an IRA. Self-employed people can also look at a SEP IRA or a Solo 401(k), which allow much higher contributions.

**Should I use a robo-adviser?** It's a reasonable option if you want everything picked and rebalanced for you. Compare its yearly fee with simply holding a target-date fund, which does a similar job.

**Do I need to pick stocks or watch the news?** No. Broad index funds already own the winners, whoever they turn out to be.

**How do taxes work in a regular brokerage account?** You may owe tax on dividends each year and on gains when you sell. Gains on investments held for more than a year usually get a lower tax rate. Your brokerage sends you the forms you need.

**Is this advice for my situation?** No. It's general education. For personal advice, look for a fee-only fiduciary financial planner, who is paid by you rather than by commissions.

## The whole system, in one line

> Automatic transfer, then automatic investment, into a low-cost index or target-date fund, checked once or twice a year.

That's genuinely all it takes.
