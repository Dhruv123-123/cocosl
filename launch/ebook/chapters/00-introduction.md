# Introduction: Why This Book Is Short {#introduction .unnumbered}

Most money books are long because they try to cover everything. This one is short because most people don't need everything. They need a handful of habits that work, set up once, and a clear idea of what to do next.

Here is the whole book in five sentences:

1. Split your take-home pay into four lines: **bills**, **funds**, **goals** and **one weekly spending number**.
2. Put the first three on autopilot, and check the fourth for twenty minutes a week.
3. Cut the recurring charges you don't use, and ask for a better price on the ones you keep.
4. Give your debt a finish line, and pay it down in an order you'll stick with.
5. Once the expensive debt is gone and you have a cushion, invest a fixed amount every month in a low-cost fund and leave it alone.

Everything else in these pages is the detail that makes those five sentences work in real life: how to find your actual numbers, what to do when a week goes wrong, word-for-word scripts for the phone calls nobody enjoys, how to read a credit report, and what to buy when you open your first investment account.

## Who this book is for

This book is for you if any of these sound familiar:

- You've tried budgeting before, and it fell apart somewhere around the third week of the month.
- You earn a reasonable amount but can't say where it goes.
- You have some debt and no plan for it beyond "pay more when I can."
- You know you should be investing, but the jargon puts you off.
- You'd like a system that runs without you thinking about money every day.

It is written for readers in the United States. The ideas work anywhere, but the accounts (401(k), IRA), the credit bureaus and the consumer-protection agencies mentioned are American.

## How to read it

The book has five parts, and they're in the order most people should do things.

**Part One: See Your Money** explains the four-line plan and walks you through finding your real numbers, including the costs that aren't monthly but aren't surprises either.

**Part Two: Run It** turns those numbers into a weekly spending number, sets up the accounts and automatic transfers that do most of the work, and gives you the twenty-minute weekly routine and the monthly reset. It ends with the everyday side of living on one weekly number, and what to do when life doesn't fit the plan.

**Part Three: Cut and Clear** is about freeing up money: auditing every recurring charge, negotiating the bills you keep, and building a dated plan to pay off debt.

**Part Four: Build** covers your emergency fund, saving for big goals, your credit report and starting to invest.

**Part Five: The Year** gives you a calendar of money moves for the year, a plan for your first thirty days, and answers to the questions people ask most.

You can read it in an evening or two. You'll get the most from it if you read Part One with your last two months of bank and card statements open next to you.

## About the examples

The people in this book (mainly Maya and Jordan) are made up. Their numbers are realistic and the math has been checked, but they are illustrations, not anyone's real finances. Where the book shows what money might grow to, or how much interest a plan might save, those are worked examples based on stated assumptions, not predictions or promises.

## The honest bit

This is a book about organizing your own money. It is general education, not financial, tax, legal or investment advice, and it can't know your situation. Rules, limits and company policies change; where a number depends on the year (like retirement account limits), the book says which year it uses and where to check the current figure.

If you are behind on payments, being contacted by collectors, or feel that your debt is unmanageable, please don't wait until you've finished the book. Chapter {{lower-rate}} lists the options and where to find low-cost, trustworthy help. A non-profit credit counselor is often the best first call, and it's usually free or cheap.
