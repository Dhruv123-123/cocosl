# Questions People Ask {#questions}

These are the questions that come up most often about the system in this book, with short, straight answers.

**Is this really only twenty minutes a week?**

After setup, yes, for most people. The first two or three check-ins might take thirty minutes while you learn where things are. Setup itself takes about an hour, or more if you have a lot of accounts.

**I've quit every budget I've tried. Why would this one be different?**

Because it asks much less of you. You don't track categories or log every purchase. You check one number, once a week. Most budgets fail because they need daily discipline; this one needs a calendar reminder. It's also built to survive bad weeks, which every budget eventually has.

**Do I need a spreadsheet or an app?**

No. The method works on paper, in a notes app, or in a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet does the math for you, and an app can show you a weekly spending total, which makes the five-minute "log" step even quicker. But the method doesn't depend on any tool.

**Where do groceries go?**

In the weekly number. That's deliberate. Groceries are usually your biggest flexible cost, so they're where your week-to-week decisions have the most effect.

**What if I go over my weekly number?**

Next week's number is reduced by the overspend. If it keeps happening, raise the weekly number at the next monthly reset and lower something else. Don't take the difference from bills or the emergency fund.

**Should I pay off debt or save first?**

A common approach is a small starter emergency fund first, then a focus on high-interest debt, then building savings further. Your situation, including your job stability, your interest rates and whether you have an employer match, may change the right answer. It's a good question for a fee-only financial planner.

**Avalanche or snowball?**

The avalanche usually costs less in interest. The snowball usually gives you a win sooner. If you've given up on a debt plan before, choose the snowball. Otherwise, choose the avalanche. Either is far better than no plan.

**My income changes every month. Can I still do this?**

Yes. Build the plan on your lowest normal month, pay yourself a steady amount from a holding account, and keep the extra from good months as a buffer. Chapter {{life}} has the details.

**My partner and I handle money differently. Will this work for us?**

Often, yes, and it can reduce arguments. Shared bills, funds and goals come from a joint account; each of you gets your own weekly number, with no questions asked. Do the monthly reset together.

**What should I do with a raise?**

Decide before the first bigger paycheck arrives. A common split is half to goals and half to the weekly number, so you enjoy some of it and your future self gets the rest. If you're contributing to a workplace retirement plan, a raise is a good moment to increase your contribution by a percentage point.

**What about a windfall, like a tax refund or a bonus?**

Give it a plan before it lands. Many people send most of it to a goal, like debt, the emergency fund or a big goal, and keep a part for something they'll enjoy.

**I'm already behind on bills. Where do I start?**

With the people you owe. Contact your lenders and providers before you miss more payments, and ask about hardship options. Then talk to a non-profit credit counselor, in the US an NFCC member agency, who can look at the whole picture with you, often for free. Chapter {{lower-rate}} explains the options. Budgeting helps, but it isn't the first step when the numbers simply don't add up.

**Does checking my credit report hurt my score?**

No. Checking your own report is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect your score. Applications for new credit are hard inquiries, which can have a small, temporary effect.

**Is $100 a month really enough to start investing?**

Yes. It builds the account, the habit and the automatic system, and it adds up over time, as the table in Chapter {{investing}} shows. You can raise the amount whenever you're ready.

**What if the market crashes right after I start?**

Keep your automatic investment running. When prices are low, your monthly amount buys more. If you're investing for decades, early falls matter much less than steady contributions.

**Does this work outside the United States?**

The budgeting, bill-cutting and debt chapters work anywhere, in any currency. The credit report and investing chapters are specific to the US; the general ideas, like low fees and automatic contributions, apply elsewhere, but the accounts and rules are different.

**Is this financial advice?**

No. It's a general method for organizing your money, and general education about how credit, debt and investing work. For decisions about investments, taxes, debt relief, or anything specific to you, talk to a qualified professional.
