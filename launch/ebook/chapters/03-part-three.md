# Part Three: Cut and Clear {.part .unnumbered}

# The Bill Audit {#bill-audit}
Recurring charges are the quietest leak in most budgets. Each one is small, each one was a reasonable decision when you signed up, and together they can add up to hundreds or even thousands of dollars a year. Because they renew automatically, they never ask for your attention again.

This chapter is a one-hour audit. You'll find every recurring charge, decide what to do with each one, and cancel cleanly the ones you don't want. You'll end with a number: how much you've freed up per year.

## The rule of the audit

> Always look at the yearly cost, not the monthly one.

$14.99 a month sounds small. $180 a year is a plane ticket. Companies price subscriptions monthly precisely because monthly numbers feel small. Convert everything to yearly before you decide:

> monthly × 12 = yearly · weekly × 52 = yearly · quarterly × 4 = yearly

## Step 1: Find every recurring charge (10 minutes)

Subscriptions hide in five places. Check all five, and write down each charge. Don't decide anything yet; just list.

1. **Your bank and card statements, last three months.** Search or scroll for anything that appears more than once. Three months catches quarterly charges. Then search the last twelve months for words like *annual*, *renewal*, *membership* and *.com* to catch yearly ones.
2. **Your phone's app store.** On an iPhone, it's Settings, then your name, then Subscriptions. On Android, it's Google Play, then your profile picture, then Payments and subscriptions. These charges often appear on your statement as just "Apple" or "Google," so they're easy to miss.
3. **PayPal and other digital wallets.** In PayPal, look under Settings, Payments, then Manage automatic payments. Check any "buy now, pay later" apps you use as well.
4. **Big retailers.** On Amazon, look under Accounts and Lists, then Memberships and Subscriptions, which covers Prime, Subscribe and Save, and add-on channels. Check other retailers and warehouse clubs you use.
5. **Your email.** Search your inbox for *receipt*, *your subscription*, *renews*, *trial* and *membership*. This finds things billed to a card you forgot you had.

Don't forget the "bills" that are really subscriptions:

- **Home:** internet, TV packages, streaming add-ons, home security, renters insurance, a storage unit.
- **Phone and tech:** the phone plan itself, device protection, cloud storage, extra phone lines nobody uses.
- **Life:** the gym, apps, meal kits, warranty plans, car insurance, bank account fees.

**Write the renewal date next to anything annual.** Cancelling the day after an annual renewal usually means you've already paid for the year. Cancelling the week before usually means you haven't.

## Step 2: Keep, cut, downgrade or negotiate (15 minutes)

Now go down the list once. Every line gets exactly one of four decisions.

- **Keep:** you used it in the last thirty days, and you'd buy it again today at this price.
- **Cut:** you haven't used it in thirty days, or you'd forgotten it existed. Cancel it this week.
- **Downgrade:** you use it, but not all of it. Look for a cheaper tier, an ad-supported plan, fewer lines, or a smaller storage plan.
- **Negotiate:** you need it, but the price is negotiable. This is usually internet, phone, insurance, card interest rates and fees. Chapter {{better-price}} has the scripts.

Three questions settle most decisions:

**"If I didn't have this, would I sign up today at this price?"** If the answer is no, it's a cut or a downgrade.

**"Do I have two of these?"** Two music apps. Three video services. Two cloud storage plans. Keep one. For streaming, consider rotating: subscribe for the month you're watching something, then cancel and move to the next one.

**"What's the cost per use?"** A $40-a-month gym you visit twice a month is $20 a visit. A $10 app you open every day is about 33 cents a day. Cost per use is a fairer test than price.

### When annual billing is worth it

Many services charge less if you pay yearly, often around two months free. Only switch something to annual billing if you marked it *keep* and you've used it steadily for at least three months. Switching something you barely use to annual billing locks in the waste for a whole year.

### A worked example

Here's what one person's audit might look like. (The prices are illustrative, not quotes from any company.)

| Charge | Billed | Per year | Decision | Saves per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Second video streaming service | $15.49 a month | $185.88 | Cut | $185.88 |
| Cloud storage, 2 TB | $9.99 a month | $119.88 | Downgrade to 200 GB ($2.99) | $84.00 |
| Phone plan | $75 a month | $900.00 | Negotiate to $55 | $240.00 |
| Meditation app | $69.99 a year | $69.99 | Keep | $0 |
| **Total freed up** | | | | **$509.88** |

Four lines, about half an hour of work, and over $500 a year. Most people's lists are longer than four lines.

## Step 3: Cancel cleanly, so it stays cancelled (15 minutes)

The goal isn't just to cancel. It's to never see the charge again, and to have proof if you do.

1. **Cancel where you signed up.** App store subscriptions have to be cancelled in the app store. Deleting the app never cancels a subscription. Retailer add-ons are cancelled in your account with the retailer.
2. **Look for "pause" or a cheaper plan first**, for anything you marked *downgrade*. Many services offer one in the cancellation flow.
3. **Screenshot the confirmation**, whether it's a page or an email, and note the date. If the charge shows up again, that screenshot is your evidence.
4. **Check next month's statement.** Put a two-minute reminder in your calendar for one month from today: "Did the cancelled charges stop?"
5. **If a company keeps charging after you cancelled**, contact them first with your screenshot and ask for a refund. If that doesn't work, dispute the charge with your card issuer or bank, and explain that you cancelled on a specific date. Card issuers have their own dispute rules and time limits, so act within a month or two of the statement.

### The thirty-second habit for free trials

When you start any free trial, immediately put a calendar reminder two days before it converts: "Cancel this, or keep it?" Some services let you cancel the same day you sign up and still use the trial until it ends, which is the safest option. Check the terms first.

### When you can't find how to cancel

- Search for "[company name] cancel subscription" and use the company's own help page. Be careful of look-alike sites that ask for card details.
- Use the company's chat or phone line, and say: *"I'd like to cancel my subscription today. Please confirm by email."* You don't have to give a reason, and you don't have to accept the first offer to stay.
- Some banks and card apps let you see and block recurring merchants. Use this as a backup, not instead of cancelling: blocking a payment doesn't end a contract you still owe on.

### Careful with contracts

Phone, internet and gym contracts can carry early termination fees, and a financed phone can have a remaining balance. Check before you cancel. Sometimes downgrading, or waiting until the contract ends, is cheaper than cancelling now.

## Step 4: Give the freed-up money a job

Money you stop spending on subscriptions has a habit of quietly disappearing into everything else. Decide where it goes today, while the number is fresh.

- Add it to a goal at your next monthly reset, so it moves automatically on payday.
- Or point it at your highest-interest debt as an extra monthly payment.

Then put a calendar reminder in for six months from now: "Bill audit." Subscriptions creep back. A twice-yearly audit takes less time each time, because the list is shorter.

# Ask for a Better Price {#better-price}
Some bills you can't cut: you need internet, a phone, insurance. But the price of many of them is more negotiable than it looks. Companies often have better offers for customers who ask, especially customers who sound ready to leave.

This chapter gives you word-for-word scripts. You don't need to be a good negotiator to use them. You need to be polite, specific, and willing to say "I'd like to cancel, then."

## Before you call (two minutes)

For each call, have three things ready:

- **Your current price**, what's included, and roughly when you signed up.
- **One or two real competitor offers** for similar service in your area, with the price. Five minutes of searching is enough.
- **Your account number** and the security answer they'll ask for.

## Internet or TV

> "Hi, I've been a customer for [X years], and my bill has gone up to [$current]. I'm looking at [competitor] for [$offer] a month for similar speed. I'd rather stay. What can you do on my price?"

If they say no:

> "Could you transfer me to the retention or loyalty team? I'm thinking about cancelling."

If they offer something:

> "Thanks. Is that the best you can do? Does it change my contract or add any fees? How long does that price last?"

## Phone plan

> "I'd like to review my plan. I use about [X GB] of data a month. Is there a cheaper plan that covers that, or any current promotions for existing customers? I'm comparing with [carrier or prepaid option] at [$offer]."

Then ask:

> "Are there any lines, insurance or add-ons on my account that I'm still paying for?"

Check your bill for device protection and extra lines before you call. Many people find they're paying for a line on a phone that's been in a drawer for two years. Prepaid plans and smaller carriers that use the big networks are often much cheaper for the same coverage, and they're worth comparing.

## Car or renters insurance

> "I'm reviewing my policy before renewal. Can you check that I'm getting every discount I qualify for? For example: bundling, paperless or autopay, safe driver, low mileage, and good student if that applies."

> "What would my premium be with a [higher deductible amount]?"

A higher deductible lowers your premium, but it means you pay more yourself if you make a claim. Only choose a deductible you could pay from your emergency fund tomorrow.

Then get two or three quotes from other insurers for the same coverage, and compare like for like. Insurance prices vary a lot between companies for the same person, and loyalty is rarely rewarded. Before you drop or reduce any insurance, make sure you still have the coverage you need, including anything your lender or state requires.

## Credit card interest rate

> "Hi, I've had this card for [X years] and I pay on time. My APR is [X%]. I'm working on paying the balance down, and I've had offers from other cards. Could you lower my interest rate?"

Then stop talking and let them answer. If they say no:

> "Okay, thank you. Is there a hardship or payment plan program, or any promotional rate available on my account?"

Hardship programs can come with conditions, like a frozen card, so ask what they are before you agree. And if the answer is still no, say thank you and try again in a few months, particularly once your credit score improves. It doesn't cost anything to ask, and asking doesn't affect your credit.

## Late fees and annual fees

For a late fee:

> "I missed my payment by [X days], which isn't usual for me. I've paid it now. Would you be able to waive the late fee as a courtesy?"

For an annual fee:

> "I'm deciding whether to keep this card because of the annual fee. Is there a retention offer, or can I switch to a no-fee version of the card without closing the account?"

Switching to a no-fee card from the same issuer usually keeps the account's history, which can matter for your credit score. Closing the account doesn't.

## Bank account fees

> "I was charged a [monthly maintenance or overdraft] fee of [$X] on [date]. Could you refund it? And what do I need to do to avoid this fee in future?"

If fees keep happening, many banks and credit unions offer checking accounts with no monthly fee at all. Moving accounts is a small hassle once, and it can save you every month after.

## Gyms and memberships

> "I'd like to cancel my membership. Please tell me exactly what I need to do, any fee involved, and send me written confirmation."

If you'd rather keep it:

> "Do you have an off-peak or lower-tier membership?"

## What makes these calls work

- **Be pleasant to the person on the phone.** They didn't set the price, and they're far more likely to help someone who's nice to them.
- **Silence is fine.** After you ask, stop talking and let them look.
- **Ask "Is that the best you can do?" once.** Then decide.
- **If the answer is no, call back another day.** A different person may have different offers available.
- **Get it in writing.** Ask for an email confirming the new price and how long it lasts.

## Keep a call log

For every call, write down the date, who you spoke to, the old price, the new price, and how long the new price lasts. That last part matters: many discounts are promotional and end after twelve months. Put a reminder in your calendar for a few weeks before the promo ends, and call again then.

None of this works every time. Prices and policies vary by company and change often, and some calls will end with a polite no. But a few calls a year, each taking ten or fifteen minutes, are often the highest-paid work you'll do all year.

# Debt: Put a Date on It {#debt}
Debt feels endless when it has no finish line. You pay every month, the balance barely moves, and there's no sense of when it will be over. This chapter gives it a finish line: a month and a year, worked out from your real balances, rates and payments. Then it shows you how to pull that date closer.

## List every debt

You need four numbers for each debt. Log in to each account, or grab the latest statement, and write them down.

1. **Balance:** what you owe today. For credit cards, use the current balance, not the last statement balance.
2. **APR:** the annual interest rate. On card statements it's usually in a box labeled "Interest charge calculation." Use the purchase APR.
3. **Minimum payment:** the minimum monthly payment. Card minimums shrink as the balance does; use today's.
4. **Any promotion:** a 0% or deferred-interest offer, and the date it ends.

Include credit cards and store cards, buy-now-pay-later plans, personal loans, car loans, student loans, medical payment plans, and money owed to family if there's a set payment.

Leave your mortgage out unless you specifically want to plan it. Its long term and usually lower rate would push your debt-free date out by decades and hide your progress on everything else.

**Watch for one warning sign.** If a debt's minimum payment doesn't cover the monthly interest, the balance can't shrink on minimums alone. To check, multiply the balance by the APR and divide by twelve. If the result is more than the minimum payment, that debt needs attention first, through a lower rate (Chapter {{lower-rate}}) or a bigger payment.

## The one rule every method shares

Every good debt payoff method works the same way:

> Pay the minimum on every debt. Put every extra dollar on one target debt. When the target is paid off, its payment rolls onto the next target.

The methods only differ in which debt is the target.

## Avalanche or snowball?

**The avalanche method** targets the debt with the highest interest rate first. It's mathematically the cheapest, because you're always attacking the most expensive debt. It suits you if you're motivated by the numbers and can wait a while for the first debt to disappear.

**The snowball method** targets the smallest balance first. It costs a bit more in interest, but you clear whole debts sooner, which means fewer bills and a real sense of progress. It suits you if quick wins keep you going.

**A custom order** is fine, too. You might want to clear a 0% promotion before it ends, or pay back a family member first because it matters to you.

### How to choose in ten seconds

Have you started a debt plan before and given up? Choose snowball. Otherwise, choose avalanche. You can switch at any time; nothing is locked in.

## Jordan's plan

Jordan (fictional) has five debts:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store card | $900 | 29.99% | $35 |
| Credit card | $4,200 | 24.9% | $105 |
| Personal loan | $1,800 | 11% | $75 |
| Car loan | $6,500 | 7.9% | $210 |
| Student loan | $14,000 | 5.5% | $180 |
| **Total** | **$27,400** | | **$605** |

Jordan can find $200 a month extra. Here's what each approach does, using a simple month-by-month calculation:

| Plan | Debt-free in | Total interest | First debt gone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimums only, no rollover | 97 months | $9,804 | Personal loan, month 28 |
| Avalanche, $200 extra | 39 months | $3,804 | Store card, month 5 |
| Snowball, $200 extra | 39 months | $3,985 | Store card, month 5 |

For Jordan, the avalanche saves about $181 compared with the snowball, and both finish in the same month. The snowball clears the personal loan sooner, in month 10, so Jordan has one less bill to think about much earlier. Either plan saves around $6,000 compared with paying only the minimums, and either one finishes almost five years sooner.

The method matters far less than having one and sticking to it.

## The rollover is the whole trick

When Jordan's store card is paid off in month five, its $35 minimum doesn't go back into spending. It joins the $200 extra and goes to the next target. When the next debt is paid off, its minimum joins too. By the last debt, Jordan is paying the original $605 plus the $200 extra, $805 a month, into a single loan.

That's why the debt-free date moves so much faster than paying minimums. Every payoff makes the next one quicker.

## Find your extra payment

The extra payment is the lever that moves your date. Here's what different amounts would do for Jordan, using the avalanche method:

| Extra per month | Debt-free in | Total interest | Interest saved vs $0 extra |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0 (minimums, with rollover) | 57 months | $7,071 | — |
| $100 | 46 months | $4,836 | $2,235 |
| $200 | 39 months | $3,804 | $3,267 |
| $400 | 31 months | $2,777 | $4,294 |

Notice two things. First, even with no extra money at all, simply rolling paid-off minimums forward (57 months) beats paying each debt separately (97 months). Second, the first $100 does the most work. Every extra dollar helps, so start with what you can, even if it's $25.

Where the extra can come from:

- **Recurring charges you cut.** The bill audit in Chapter {{bill-audit}} usually finds something.
- **A spending ceiling.** The weekly number makes leftovers visible, and leftovers can go to debt at the monthly reset.
- **Lower interest rates.** A lower APR means more of the same payment goes to the balance. Chapter {{lower-rate}} covers this.
- **Windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses, gifts and side income. A simple rule many people like: half to debt, half to you.
- **Paused extra investing, but not the match.** If your employer matches retirement contributions, keep contributing enough to get the full match. That match is usually worth more than paying down most debts faster.

## Build a small cushion first

If you have no savings at all, the next car repair goes straight back on a card, and the plan goes backwards. Many people set aside a starter emergency fund, often $500 to $1,000, before putting extra money toward debt. Keep it in a separate savings account so it isn't spent by accident.

## Maya's debts

To see the same ideas with different numbers, here's Maya, from Part Two. She has $505 of minimums and $150 extra.

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit card | $2,400 | 24.9% | $60 |
| Personal loan | $1,200 | 11.0% | $55 |
| Car loan | $6,500 | 7.9% | $210 |
| Student loan | $14,000 | 5.5% | $180 |
| **Total** | **$24,100** | | **$505** |

| Plan | First debt gone | Debt-free in | Total interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimums only ($505, with rollover) | Month 25 | 58 months | $5,017 |
| Snowball ($655 a month) | Month 7 | 42 months | $3,128 |
| Avalanche ($655 a month) | Month 14 | 42 months | $2,992 |

The extra $150 matters far more than the method: it cuts about 16 months and roughly $1,900 to $2,000 of interest either way. The avalanche saves Maya about $136 over the snowball, but the snowball gives her a win seven months earlier. Both are good choices.

A note on these figures: they come from a simple monthly calculation, with interest of APR ÷ 12 on each balance each month. Real lenders calculate interest daily and may apply payments differently, so your statements won't match to the dollar. Use them for planning, not as a payoff quote.

## Automate it, and stay with it

The plan only works if the payments happen every month without you needing to be strong. Set it up once:

1. **Autopay the minimum on every debt.** A late payment costs a fee, can trigger a higher penalty interest rate, and hurts your credit. Autopay removes that risk.
2. **Schedule the extra payment for the day after payday**, as a recurring payment to your current target debt.
3. **When a debt hits zero, move that payment to the next target the same day.** This is the rollover. Don't let it leak back into spending.
4. **Once a month**, at your reset, add up your balances and write the total down.

### Staying motivated

- **Watch the date, not the balance.** Your debt-free date is the number to celebrate. Every extra payment pulls it closer.
- **Mark each payoff.** When a debt is gone, do something small and free, or cheap, to celebrate it, and tell someone.
- **Expect a bad month.** A slip isn't failure. Pay the minimums, skip the extra that month, and restart next payday.

### Should you close cards as you pay them off?

Usually not right away. Closing a card removes its credit limit, which can raise your credit utilization (Chapter {{credit}}), and closes an account that may be helping your credit history. If a card has an annual fee, or you can't trust yourself with it, closing it is reasonable. Otherwise, many people keep paid-off cards open with a single small recurring charge on autopay. If temptation is the problem, take the card out of your wallet and delete it from your saved payment details.

## When you're done

On the day your last debt is paid, you'll have a few hundred dollars a month that you're already used to living without. Keep paying it, to yourself: first to a full emergency fund, then to investing. It's the easiest saving you'll ever do, because you won't miss money you've spent years not having.

# Lower the Rate, and When the Plan Isn't Enough {#lower-rate}
There are three ways to pay less interest on the same debt. Try them in this order: the first costs nothing, and the other two have fees and need reasonably good credit.

## 1. Ask (five minutes per card)

Use the script in Chapter {{better-price}}. Call the number on the back of the card, say how long you've been a customer, that you pay on time, that you're paying the balance down, and ask for a lower rate. It doesn't always work, but it costs nothing and doesn't affect your credit.

## 2. A balance transfer card

A balance transfer moves a card balance to a new card that has a low or 0% promotional rate for a set time, usually in exchange for a fee, commonly 3% to 5% of the amount moved.

Here's an example. Say you have a $3,000 balance at 24.9% and pay $200 a month. Staying put costs about $631 in interest by the time it's paid off. Moving it to a card with a 15-month 0% promotion and a 3% fee costs about $92, which is the fee.

That's a big difference, but a balance transfer is only worth it if all three of these are true:

- **The fee is less than the interest you'd save.** Do the sum before you apply.
- **You can pay off most or all of the balance before the promotion ends.** Divide the balance by the number of promotional months to see the monthly payment you'd need. Whatever is left when the promotion ends goes back to a normal rate, which can be high.
- **You stop using both cards for new spending.** Moving the debt, then filling the old card up again, is how people end up with twice the debt.

## 3. A consolidation loan

A consolidation loan is a fixed-rate personal loan that pays off several debts at once, leaving you with one payment and a fixed end date. It helps when the loan's APR, including any fees, is well below the average rate of the debts it replaces.

It doesn't always help. In Jordan's example from the last chapter, much of the debt is already at 5.5% to 7.9%. A 12.5% loan with a 4% fee would cost more than Jordan's current plan. Run your own numbers before you apply, and be wary of any loan that only looks good because it stretches your payments over a longer time.

## Federal student loans are different

Federal student loans come with protections that private loans don't have, like income-driven repayment plans, deferment and forbearance. Refinancing or consolidating them into a private loan gives up those protections permanently. Federal repayment rules changed in 2025 and 2026, so check StudentAid.gov or your loan servicer for the options that apply to you now.

## When the plan isn't enough

Sometimes the honest answer is that a payoff plan, on its own, won't fix it. If the numbers say your debt-free date is decades away, or the minimum payments alone are more than you can pay after rent and food, you need a different tool. That's not a personal failure. There are good tools, and many people use them.

### Signs it's time for outside help

- You're using credit cards to pay for essentials, or to pay other debts.
- You've missed payments, or a debt has gone to collections.
- Minimum payments take more than you can afford after housing and food.
- You're being sued, or your wages are being garnished.

### Your options, from least to most drastic

**Hardship programs.** Ask each lender directly. Many offer a temporary lower rate or payment for people going through a rough patch. There may be conditions, like a frozen card, so get the terms in writing.

**Non-profit credit counseling.** A free or low-cost session with a counselor who reviews your whole situation: income, debts, budget, options. In the US, look for an agency that's a member of the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC). The words "non-profit" in a company's name aren't proof on their own, so check its accreditation.

**A debt management plan.** Arranged by a credit counseling agency, it combines your card payments into one monthly payment to the agency, often with reduced interest rates that the agency negotiates, usually over three to five years. There's typically a small monthly fee, and the cards in the plan are usually closed.

**Debt settlement.** A company negotiates with your creditors to accept less than you owe, while you stop paying them and save into an account instead. It can work for some people, but it damages your credit, carries fees, can lead to lawsuits while you're not paying, and forgiven debt may count as taxable income. Under Federal Trade Commission rules, settlement companies can't charge fees before they've actually settled a debt.

**Bankruptcy.** A legal process that can discharge or restructure debts. It has a serious and long-lasting effect on your credit, and it's not a decision to make alone. Get a consultation with a bankruptcy attorney; many offer a free first consultation.

### Red flags

Be very wary of anyone who:

- charges large fees upfront,
- promises to make your debts disappear,
- tells you to stop talking to your creditors, or
- pressures you to sign today.

Before you sign anything, check complaints with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at consumerfinance.gov and with your state attorney general.

## A last word on debt

Debt carries a lot of shame for a lot of people, and shame makes it harder to deal with, because it makes you look away. The people who get out of debt aren't the ones who never made a mistake. They're the ones who looked at the numbers, made a plan, and kept going through the bad months. A list, a date and an automatic payment are enough to start.
