# Living on the Weekly Number {#living}
Setting a weekly number takes an evening. Living on it takes a few weeks of practice. The first month usually has a week where the money runs out on Thursday, a week where it's barely touched, and a week where something unexpected happens. That's normal. This chapter is about the everyday side of the system: how to make the number go further without feeling deprived, and how to handle the moments that tend to blow it.

## Know your fixed weekly costs

Inside your weekly number, some spending is nearly fixed: groceries and gas, mostly. The rest is flexible: eating out, coffee, fun, the little things.

It helps to know roughly what the fixed part costs, because it tells you how much freedom you really have. In Maya's case, $184 a week covers about $85 of groceries and $35 of gas, which leaves around $64 for everything else. If she knows that number, she knows that a $45 dinner out on Saturday uses most of the week's fun money, and she can decide whether that's how she wants to spend it.

You don't need to track this every week. Just look at a typical week once, early on, and write down the two numbers: what the essentials cost, and what's left over.

## Groceries: the biggest lever

Groceries are usually the largest flexible cost, which is why they live inside the weekly number rather than as a separate bill. Small habits here make a noticeable difference without changing what you eat very much.

- **Shop with a list, and shop once a week if you can.** Every extra trip to the store is a chance for unplanned purchases.
- **Plan a few dinners, not every meal.** Deciding three or four dinners in advance is enough to cut the "nothing in the fridge, let's order in" evenings, without turning your week into a spreadsheet.
- **Cook once, eat twice.** Make a double batch of one meal a week and have it for lunch or freeze half.
- **Look at the price per unit**, usually on the shelf label. The bigger package isn't always cheaper per ounce, and store brands are often much cheaper for the same thing.
- **Check what you already have** before you shop. A surprising amount of grocery money goes on food that's thrown away.

None of these are rules. They're levers. Use the ones that suit you.

## Eating out and coffee

The weekly number doesn't ban eating out, and you shouldn't try to. A budget that removes everything enjoyable is a budget you'll abandon. The point is to choose.

A few approaches people find helpful:

- **Decide in advance.** If Friday dinner with friends matters to you, plan for it at the start of the week, and let the rest of the week be cheaper.
- **Swap, don't stop.** Coffee out three times a week instead of five. Lunch out on Fridays instead of every day. You keep the pleasure, at a lower cost.
- **Notice the defaults.** Delivery fees, service charges and tips can add a third or more to the price of a takeout meal. Picking up the order yourself often costs noticeably less.

## The 48-hour rule

For any non-essential purchase above a set amount, say $50, wait 48 hours before buying. Put it in a wish list or a note on your phone, and come back to it in two days.

Often, you'll still want it, and you'll buy it from your weekly number with a clear conscience. Just as often, you'll find the urge has passed. The rule doesn't stop you buying anything. It just separates the wanting from the buying, which is where most impulse spending lives.

Online shopping makes this easier to apply than it sounds: add the item to your cart and close the tab. If you find you're still thinking about it in two days, it's probably worth having.

## Make spending slightly harder

Most stores and apps are designed to make spending as easy as possible. You can push back a little:

- **Remove saved cards** from shopping sites and apps you use often. Having to type your card number in adds a few seconds of thought.
- **Unsubscribe from marketing emails.** Fewer "48 hours only" emails means fewer purchases you didn't plan.
- **Turn off one-click buying** where you can.
- **Delete the shopping app** you open when you're bored. You can still shop on the website; you just won't be reminded to.

These are small things, but they work for the same reason the four-line plan does: they reduce the number of decisions you have to make.

## Social spending

Some of the hardest moments for a weekly number are social. A friend suggests an expensive dinner. A colleague is collecting for a gift. A group trip comes up. Saying no can feel awkward, and saying yes every time can break the week.

A few things help:

- **Suggest an alternative instead of just saying no.** "I'm keeping my spending down this month. Could we do a picnic, or cook at mine?" Most people are relieved; plenty of your friends are probably trying to spend less too.
- **Plan the predictable ones.** Birthdays, weddings and the holiday season aren't surprises, so they belong in your gifts and holidays fund, not in the weekly number.
- **Know your number before you go.** If you know you have $40 left for the week, you can enjoy the evening and order accordingly, without doing math at the table.

You don't owe anyone an explanation of your budget. "That's not in my plan this month" is a complete sentence.

## When something unexpected comes up mid-week

Sooner or later, something will cost money in the middle of a week that wasn't planned: a parking ticket, a last-minute birthday you forgot, a broken phone screen. Work through it in this order:

1. **Is there a fund for it?** Car costs come from the car fund; gifts from the gifts fund. Move the money at your next check-in.
2. **If not, can it come from this week's number?** If it's small, just absorb it, and have a cheaper week.
3. **If it's larger,** take it from the emergency fund if it's genuinely unexpected and necessary, and add a refill to your next monthly reset.
4. **At the reset, ask whether it's likely to happen again.** If yes, it probably deserves a sinking fund of its own.

What you want to avoid is the quiet fourth option: putting it on a credit card and hoping. That's how a single surprise turns into a balance that lasts for months.

## Rollover or sweep?

When you finish a week with money left over, you have two choices. You can **roll it over**, adding it to next week's number, or **sweep it**, moving it to a goal at the end of the month.

Both are fine. Rolling over gives you flexibility for a bigger week ahead, like a birthday dinner, and it can make the system feel generous. Sweeping turns small wins into real progress: $15 a week left over is $780 a year toward a goal.

A good default is to roll leftovers forward through the month and sweep whatever is left at the monthly reset. That way you have flexibility week to week, and nothing drifts for longer than a month.

## Rewards, not punishment

The weekly number works best when it feels like freedom rather than restriction. Inside that number, you don't have to justify anything to anyone, including yourself. Spend it on what you enjoy most. If you love books, buy books, and skip the things you don't care about.

Over a few months, many people find their spending shifts on its own, away from things they barely notice and toward things they really value. That's the system working: not making you spend less on everything, but making it easy to spend on purpose.
