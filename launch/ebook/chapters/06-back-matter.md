# Glossary {.unnumbered}

**401(k) and 403(b).** Retirement savings plans offered through an employer. You contribute through payroll, and many employers match part of what you put in.

**Annual percentage rate (APR).** The yearly interest rate on a debt. A card with a 24% APR charges roughly 2% a month on the balance.

**Avalanche method.** Paying off debts in order of highest interest rate first, while paying minimums on the rest.

**Balance transfer.** Moving a credit card balance to a different card, usually one with a low or 0% promotional rate, often for a fee.

**Credit bureau.** A company that collects information about how you use credit and sells reports to lenders. The three nationwide bureaus in the US are Equifax, Experian and TransUnion.

**Credit freeze.** A free block that stops most lenders from seeing your credit report, which prevents most new accounts from being opened in your name.

**Credit utilization.** How much of your available credit card limits you're using, as a percentage.

**Dollar-cost averaging.** Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule, regardless of what the market is doing.

**Emergency fund.** Savings set aside for unexpected costs, like a job loss or medical bill. Often three to six months of essential costs.

**Employer match.** Money your employer adds to your retirement plan when you contribute, up to a limit.

**Expense ratio.** The yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage of the money you have in it.

**Flexible spending account (FSA).** A workplace account for certain health or dependent-care costs, paid for with pre-tax money. Unused money is often lost at the end of the plan year.

**Furnisher.** A company, like a lender or collector, that reports information about you to the credit bureaus.

**Health savings account (HSA).** A tax-advantaged account for medical costs, available with a high-deductible health plan. The money rolls over and stays yours.

**Index fund.** A fund that owns all the companies in a market index, rather than paying a manager to choose. Usually low-cost.

**IRA (individual retirement account).** A retirement account you open yourself. It comes in Roth and Traditional versions, which differ in when you pay tax.

**Monthly pool.** In this book, what's left of your take-home pay after bills, funds and goals. It becomes your weekly number.

**Net worth.** Everything you have minus everything you owe.

**Sinking fund.** Money set aside a little at a time for a cost that's predictable but not monthly, like car repairs or holiday gifts.

**Snowball method.** Paying off debts in order of smallest balance first, while paying minimums on the rest.

**Take-home pay.** What lands in your account after tax and deductions.

**Target-date fund.** A single fund that holds a mix of investments and becomes more cautious automatically as its target year approaches.

**Weekly number.** In this book, the amount you can spend each week on everything that isn't a bill, fund or goal.

**Zero-based budgeting.** Giving every dollar of income a job before the month starts.

# Where to Find Official Information {.unnumbered}

These are the official sources mentioned in the book. Type the addresses yourself, and be careful of look-alike sites.

- **AnnualCreditReport.com:** free credit reports from all three bureaus.
- **consumerfinance.gov:** the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, with guides on credit, debt and banking, and a place to submit complaints.
- **consumer.ftc.gov:** the Federal Trade Commission's consumer advice, including sample dispute letters.
- **IdentityTheft.gov:** the FTC's identity theft reporting and recovery site.
- **IRS.gov:** current retirement account limits and tax rules, including Publication 590-A and 590-B on IRAs.
- **StudentAid.gov:** federal student loan repayment options.
- **nfcc.org:** the National Foundation for Credit Counseling, to find a member non-profit credit counseling agency.
