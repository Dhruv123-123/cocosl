# When Life Doesn't Fit the Plan {#life}
The four lines stay the same in every situation. What changes is how you set them up. This chapter covers the most common variations, and the pitfalls that catch almost everyone at least once.

## Irregular or freelance income

If your income goes up and down, the plan still works, with one change: you pay yourself a steady "salary" from a holding account.

1. **Build the plan on your lowest normal month** from the last six. Ignore true outliers, but be honest about what a lean month looks like.
2. **All income goes into a holding account first.** This can be a separate savings account. On the first of each month, "pay" yourself the baseline amount into bills checking, like a salary.
3. **Anything above the baseline stays in the holding account** until the monthly reset. Then split it: first, top up a buffer of one to two months of baseline income in the holding account; after that, send the extra to goals.
4. **If you're self-employed, set aside money for taxes from every payment before anything else.** Nobody withholds tax for you, and a tax bill you didn't plan for can undo a year of good habits. A tax professional can tell you the right percentage for your situation, and whether you should be paying estimated taxes during the year.

With a buffer in place, a bad month stops being a crisis. Your salary still arrives on the first; it just comes out of the buffer.

## Couples

There are as many ways for couples to handle money as there are couples. One setup works well for most:

- **Shared bills, separate weekly numbers.** Both partners contribute to a joint account that pays shared bills, shared funds and shared goals. If your incomes are different, many couples contribute in proportion to income rather than splitting fifty-fifty. Then each person gets their own weekly number, in their own account, with no questions asked.
- **Do the monthly reset together.** It's the one time you look at the whole picture. Make it pleasant: coffee, a nice spot, a fixed end time.
- **Do the weekly check-in separately or together**, whichever you'll actually keep doing.

Separate weekly numbers remove the most common money argument, which is about small spending. If your partner wants to spend their weekly number on concert tickets and you want to spend yours on books, neither of you has to justify it.

## A tight month, or a tight season

Sometimes the numbers just don't work for a while: a job loss, reduced hours, a medical bill, a new baby.

- **Pause goals before you cut groceries to unrealistic levels.** Extra debt payments and savings contributions can wait a month or two. Food can't. Keep paying all minimums, though, because a missed payment adds fees, can raise your interest rate and hurts your credit.
- **Look at bills first.** Call providers about hardship programs, payment-date changes or cheaper plans. Many utilities, lenders and even some landlords have options they don't advertise.
- **If minimums alone are more than you can pay, contact your lenders before you miss a payment.** It's easier to arrange something in advance than to fix it afterwards. And consider a non-profit credit counselor, which Chapter {{lower-rate}} explains.

## Comfortable incomes, and very tight ones

If your bills are low and your income is comfortable, you can eventually stop logging the weekly number at all, once you trust it. Keep the monthly reset, though, and automate your goals generously. The risk at higher incomes isn't running out of money; it's spending more each year without noticing, so that a raise disappears.

If money is very tight, the weekly check-in becomes more useful, not less. Some people find two five-minute check-ins a week, say Wednesday and Sunday, work better than one longer one. You catch problems sooner.

**If you like cash**, withdraw your weekly number in cash on transfer day. Physical cash makes the remaining amount impossible to ignore, and many people find they spend less when they can see the notes leaving their wallet.

## The pitfalls, and how to fix them

Almost every budget that fails hits one of these. None of them mean you're bad with money.

**The weekly number was too optimistic.** You planned $150 a week but always spend $190. That's not a character flaw; it's information. Raise the weekly number at the next reset, and lower a goal or a fund to pay for it. A realistic plan you follow beats an ambitious one you don't.

**You forgot the non-monthly costs.** The budget breaks every month because of "one unusual expense." Go back to Chapter {{sinking-funds}} and build sinking funds.

**Goals came out of what was left.** If savings happen at the end of the month, they usually don't happen. Move the transfer to payday.

**You divided by four.** If money runs out a few days before the end of every month, check your math. Use × 12 ÷ 52.

**The plan never changed.** Your rent went up, a debt was paid off, and you got a raise, but the transfers are the same as a year ago. That's what the monthly reset is for.

**You stopped after a bad week.** Everyone has bad weeks. Next week's number is a bit lower; that's the whole consequence. Keep going.

**It was too complicated.** If you've added categories, sub-accounts and color-coded spreadsheets until the check-in takes an hour, strip it back. Four lines, one number, twenty minutes.
