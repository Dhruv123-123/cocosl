# Saving for Big Goals {#big-goals}

Once the emergency fund is under way, most people have something bigger in mind: a down payment on a home, a car bought with cash instead of a loan, a wedding, a year of school, a long trip. These goals sit between sinking funds and investing. They're too big and too far away for a sinking fund to feel natural, and usually too soon, or too important, to put at the mercy of the stock market.

This chapter is about how to plan them, where to keep the money, and how to keep going when the goal is years away.

## Give the goal three numbers

A vague goal like "save for a house" is hard to act on. A goal with three numbers is a plan:

1. **How much.** Research a realistic figure. For a home, that's the down payment plus closing costs plus some money left over for moving and the first repairs. For a car, the price you'd actually pay, including tax and registration.
2. **By when.** A month and a year.
3. **How much per month.** Which you can now work out:

> monthly amount = (target − what you already have) ÷ months until the date

Say you want $18,000 for a down payment in four years and you've saved $2,000. That's $16,000 over 48 months, or about $334 a month.

If that number doesn't fit in your plan, you have three dials: save more each month, move the date later, or lower the target. Turning any one of them makes the goal reachable. What doesn't work is keeping all three fixed and hoping.

## Where to keep it

The rule of thumb from Chapter {{investing}} applies here: money you'll need within about five years doesn't belong in stocks. A 20% drop the year before you buy a house can push the purchase back by years.

For most big goals, that means:

- **A high-yield savings account**, ideally a separate one or a named bucket, so the goal has its own balance you can watch grow.
- **Certificates of deposit (CDs)** for part of the money, if you know the date well and want a fixed rate. Match the CD's term to when you'll need the money, because withdrawing early usually costs a penalty.
- **US Treasury bills**, for people comfortable opening an account to buy them. They're backed by the US government and their interest is exempt from state income tax.

For goals more than five to ten years away, some people invest part of the money, usually in a cautious mix, and move it to savings as the date gets closer. That's a reasonable approach, but it takes more care, and it's worth talking to a fee-only planner if the sums are large.

## Automate it like everything else

A big goal is just a goal line in your plan with a bigger number. Schedule the transfer for payday, watch the balance grow at your monthly reset, and raise the amount when a debt is paid off or your income goes up. Windfalls, like tax refunds and bonuses, can bring the date forward a lot.

## Savings challenges

Some people find a plain monthly transfer motivating. Others find it dull, and do better with a game. Savings challenges turn saving into something you can see and tick off, and they work especially well for building a starter emergency fund, a holiday fund or a first big goal.

**The 52-week challenge.** Save $1 in week one, $2 in week two, and so on, up to $52 in week fifty-two. The full year adds up to $1,378. A popular variation runs it in reverse, starting with $52 in January when motivation is high and ending with $1 in December, when money is tight around the holidays. The total is the same.

**The 100 envelope challenge.** Number 100 envelopes from 1 to 100. Each day, or each few days, pick one at random and put that many dollars in it. All 100 envelopes add up to $5,050. It's a big target, so many people do it over a year or more, or adapt it, for example by using 50 envelopes for a total of $1,275.

**The no-spend challenge.** For a set period, often a week or a month, spend nothing beyond essentials: no eating out, no shopping, no new subscriptions. Groceries, bills and transport still happen. Then move what you would have spent to a goal. Even one no-spend week a month can show you how much of your weekly number goes on things you don't miss.

**A savings tracker you color in.** A chart of boxes or a picture divided into sections, each worth an amount. Every time you save that amount, you color one in. It sounds simple, and it is, but seeing progress on the fridge every day is a real motivator for many people.

### Making a challenge work

- **Run it alongside your plan, not instead of it.** A challenge works best as the way you fill a goal line, with the money moving to a savings account each week rather than sitting in envelopes at home.
- **Pick a challenge that fits your income.** If the weeks near $52 would be a struggle, try the reverse version, or halve the amounts.
- **Expect to miss a week.** Catch up when you can, or just carry on. The habit is the point.
- **Decide in advance what the money is for.** "Save $1,378" is less motivating than "save for the trip in August."

## When the goal is years away

Long goals have a motivation problem. In month three, the target is still a long way off, and it's tempting to borrow from it for something nearer.

A few things help:

- **Celebrate milestones.** 10%, 25%, half. Mark them, and tell someone.
- **Track the date, not just the balance.** If you're ahead of plan, the date moves closer, and that's motivating.
- **Keep the goal money out of sight.** A separate account at a different bank from your checking account adds just enough friction to stop casual withdrawals.
- **Revisit the plan once a year.** Prices change, and so do goals. A goal that no longer matters to you can be closed and the money redirected, on purpose, at a monthly reset.

Big goals are where the system in this book pays off most. They're reached the same way as everything else: a clear number, an automatic transfer, and a lot of ordinary months.
