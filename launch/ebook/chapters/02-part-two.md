# Part Two: Run It {.part .unnumbered}

# Your Weekly Number {#weekly-number}
You now have three of the numbers you need: take-home pay, bills and funds. This chapter adds the fourth, your goals, and turns all of them into the one number you'll actually watch.

## Choose your goals, and pay them first

Goals are the money you're paying to your future self. A sensible order for most people looks like this, though your situation may change it:

1. **A starter emergency fund of about one month of bills.** It's what stops a flat tire from turning into new credit card debt. If one month feels out of reach, start smaller: even $500 to $1,000 covers a lot of small emergencies.
2. **Extra payments on high-interest debt.** Anything around 8% APR or more, which includes almost every credit card. Chapter {{debt}} compares two ways to do this.
3. **Grow the emergency fund toward three to six months of essential costs.** More if your income is irregular or your job feels shaky.
4. **Long-term investing**, starting with any employer retirement match you'd otherwise leave on the table. (Chapter {{investing}} explains why the match comes even before paying off card debt.)

You don't need to finish one step to the last dollar before starting the next. Many people put most of their goal money toward one step and a little toward the next.

**Pick an amount you can keep up every month.** Consistency beats ambition here. A $150 goal you hit every month for a year is $1,800. A $600 goal you abandon in February is $600 and a bad feeling. You can always raise it at a monthly reset.

## The calculation

Here's the whole thing:

> monthly pool = take-home pay − bills − funds − goals
>
> weekly number = monthly pool × 12 ÷ 52, rounded down

The *monthly pool* is what's left for everyday spending after everything else has been paid. The weekly number is that pool spread evenly across the weeks of the year.

## Why × 12 ÷ 52, and not ÷ 4

This is the most important detail in the chapter, so it's worth a moment.

It's tempting to divide the monthly pool by four. A month is about four weeks, right? But it isn't. A month is about 4.33 weeks. Dividing by four quietly hands you an extra week of spending money you don't have, and you'll feel it in every month that has a fifth payday or a fifth weekend: the money runs out a few days before the month does.

Multiplying by 12 and dividing by 52 gives you the true weekly share of your yearly pool. Rounding down leaves a few dollars of buffer in your checking account, which is the kind of mistake you want to make.

## Is the weekly number livable?

The weekly number has to cover groceries, gas and everything fun. Look at it honestly.

If it's comfortable, great. If it's tight but workable, also fine; the next chapters will help you loosen it.

If it isn't realistic, don't pretend. A plan you abandon in week two saves nothing. Lower a goal for now. Raise it again once a bill goes down or a debt is paid off. The plan should be something you can actually live inside, because the whole point is that you'll keep doing it.

**If the pool is negative**, you've planned to spend more than you earn. That happens, and it's useful to know. Trim goals first, then funds, then look hard at bills. If you can't get the pool above zero with all goals paused, that's a sign to go straight to Chapters {{bill-audit}}, {{better-price}} and {{lower-rate}}, which cover cutting bills and getting help with debt.

## Maya's month

Here's the full plan for Maya, who you met in the last chapter. She takes home $3,500 a month, has a car loan, a student loan and two smaller debts she'd like gone.

**Line 1: Bills**

| Bill | Per month |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,250 |
| Renters and car insurance | $140 |
| Utilities | $120 |
| Internet | $55 |
| Phone | $45 |
| Subscriptions (three) | $35 |
| Car loan minimum | $210 |
| Student loan minimum | $180 |
| Credit card minimum | $60 |
| Personal loan minimum | $55 |
| **Bills total** | **$2,150** |

**Line 2: Funds** total $200 a month, as you saw in Chapter {{sinking-funds}}.

**Line 3: Goals**

| Goal | Per month |
|---|---|
| Emergency fund | $200 |
| Extra debt payment | $150 |
| **Goals total** | **$350** |

**Line 4: The weekly number**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Take-home pay | $3,500.00 |
| Minus bills, funds and goals ($2,150 + $200 + $350) | −$2,700.00 |
| Monthly pool | $800.00 |
| × 12 ÷ 52 | $184.62 |
| **Maya's weekly number (rounded down)** | **$184** |

That $184 covers groceries (about $85 a week for her), gas (about $35), and leaves roughly $64 for eating out, coffee, fun and odds and ends. Rounding down from $184.62 leaves about $2.67 a month extra in checking as a small buffer.

If she gets to the end of a week with money left over, she can roll it into next week or sweep it to savings at the monthly reset. If she overspends, next week is a little tighter.

Notice what's not here: there's no "dining out" category, no "entertainment" limit, no "household" line to police. Maya decides those trade-offs inside $184, week by week. That's the freedom the system gives back to you in exchange for paying everything else first.

## Your plan on one page

Before you move on, write your plan out in one place:

- Take-home pay: $\_\_\_\_\_
- Bills: $\_\_\_\_\_
- Funds: $\_\_\_\_\_
- Goals: $\_\_\_\_\_
- Monthly pool: $\_\_\_\_\_
- Weekly number: $\_\_\_\_\_

That's your budget. The rest of Part Two is about making it run by itself.

# Put It on Autopilot {#autopilot}
The fewer decisions you make by hand, the less willpower the budget needs. Two or three bank accounts and a few scheduled transfers do most of the work. You set this up once, in about half an hour, and then it just happens.

## The simple account setup

You need, at most, three accounts:

1. **Bills checking.** Your paycheck lands here. Every bill is paid from here on autopay.
2. **Savings.** A high-yield savings account that holds your sinking funds and your emergency fund. If your bank lets you create named buckets, make one for each fund and one for the emergency fund.
3. **Weekly spending** (optional, but powerful). A second checking account with its own debit card. Your weekly number moves here every week, and this is the card you use for everyday spending.

That third account is the secret weapon. If your weekly spending has its own account, *your balance is your remaining weekly number.* You don't have to calculate anything at the checkout. You open the app, and it says $61. That's what's left for the week.

Many banks and credit unions let you open a second checking account in a few minutes, often with no monthly fee. If yours charges a fee, it's worth looking for one that doesn't; Chapter {{better-price}} has a script for asking a bank to drop a fee.

**If you prefer a credit card for rewards**, that's fine, but treat it like a debit card: pay it off in full every week during your check-in, from the weekly spending account. If you ever find yourself not paying it off in full, go back to the debit card. The rewards are never worth the interest.

## Maya's payday flow

Maya is paid monthly, on the first. Here's what happens without her touching anything:

1. **On the 1st**, $3,500 lands in bills checking.
2. **Also on the 1st**, an automatic transfer moves $400 to savings: $200 for her funds and $200 for the emergency fund. A scheduled payment sends her $150 extra debt payment straight to her credit card.
3. **Every Friday**, an automatic transfer moves $184 to the weekly spending account.
4. **Through the month**, her bills, $2,150 in total, come out of bills checking on autopay.

That's the whole system. Maya doesn't move money by hand. She doesn't decide whether to save this month. The decisions were made once, at the monthly reset, and the bank carries them out.

## Five-Friday months

A few times a year, a month has five Fridays. Because the weekly number was calculated with × 12 ÷ 52, the year evens out, but the cash timing doesn't: in a five-Friday month, five transfers leave bills checking instead of four.

The fix is a small buffer. Keep an amount roughly equal to one weekly transfer, or a little more, sitting in bills checking all the time. Maya keeps $300. The fifth transfer never bounces, and in four-Friday months the buffer quietly refills itself.

## If you're paid every two weeks

Split each monthly transfer in half and schedule it on each payday. For example, if your monthly savings transfer is $400, send $200 on each payday.

Twice a year you'll get a "third paycheck" month, because 26 paychecks don't divide evenly into 12 months. These months are the easiest money you'll ever save, because your plan was built on two paychecks a month and you don't need the third one to live. Don't let it vanish into spending. Decide at the monthly reset before it arrives where it goes: a goal, a fund, a debt, or a one-time treat you've planned for. A common choice is to send most of it to a goal and keep a small part for something fun, so it feels like a reward and not a punishment.

## If you're paid weekly or twice a month

The same principle applies: line up the transfers with your paydays, so money moves out to savings and goals as soon as it arrives. The weekly spending transfer can go on payday if you're paid weekly, or on a fixed day each week if not.

## Timing matters

Schedule savings and goal transfers for the day of, or the day after, payday. Money that leaves first doesn't get spent. Money that waits until the end of the month usually isn't there.

Schedule your bills for shortly after payday, too, where you can. Most companies let you choose or change your due date. If your rent is due on the first and you're paid on the 15th, it's worth asking whether other bills can move closer to the 15th, so your bills checking account doesn't spend half the month nearly empty.

## Alerts: let your accounts warn you

While you're setting up transfers, take ten minutes to turn on three kinds of alerts in your bank and card apps:

- **A low-balance alert** on bills checking, set to your buffer amount. If the balance drops below it, something unexpected happened.
- **A large-transaction alert** on every card, set to something like $100. You'll spot fraud and surprise charges the same day.
- **A payment-due alert** on every card and loan, a few days before the due date. Autopay should handle it, but the alert catches autopay failures.

They're free, and your accounts start warning you instead of surprising you.

# The 20-Minute Week and the Monthly Reset {#routine}
The system runs on two habits: a twenty-minute check-in every week, and a twenty-minute reset once a month. This chapter is the one to come back to. Once your accounts are set up, it's the only part you'll actually do.

## The weekly check-in

Same day, same time, every week. Sunday evening and Friday lunch both work well. Put it in your calendar as a recurring event, and set a twenty-minute timer when you start. The timer matters: it keeps the check-in small enough that you'll keep doing it.

### Minutes 0 to 5: Look

Open your bank and card apps. Note the balance in your weekly spending account, or add up this week's purchases if you don't use a separate account. If you used a credit card for weekly spending, pay it off now, in full.

### Minutes 5 to 10: Log

Write down one number: how much you spent from the weekly pot this week. That's it. You don't need to categorize every purchase. If one purchase surprised you, jot a note next to it, but don't turn this into a full audit.

Keep the log somewhere simple, like a note on your phone, a page in a notebook, or one row per week in a spreadsheet. Over a few months, it becomes a very useful record of whether your weekly number is realistic.

### Minutes 10 to 15: Check

Run through four questions:

- Did every bill due this week go out, at the amount you expected?
- Did the payday transfers to savings and goals happen?
- Are there any charges you don't recognize? If so, dispute them now, not in three months.
- Did you spend from a sinking fund? If so, move the money from savings to cover it, and update the fund's balance.

### Minutes 15 to 20: Decide one thing

Pick one small move based on what you just saw. Only one. Examples:

- **Under budget?** Roll the leftover into next week, or move it to a goal.
- **Over budget?** Next week's number is lower by the amount you overspent. Plan one cheap week, a "make-up week," and move on.
- **Big week coming**, like a birthday or a trip? Decide now which fund covers it.
- **Saw a subscription you don't use?** Cancel it before you close the app.

Then close the apps. You're done until next week.

### If you miss a week

You will, at some point. Don't try to reconstruct the missed week receipt by receipt. Add up what you spent across both weeks, compare it with two weeks of your weekly number, and carry on. The habit matters far more than perfect records. People who give up on budgets usually give up after a missed week, out of guilt. Skip the guilt and keep the habit.

## The monthly reset

Once a month, a few days before payday, spend twenty minutes setting next month's plan. This is the only time the plan changes. It has four parts.

### Minutes 0 to 5: Review

Look at your weekly log. Were you consistently under, over, or roughly on target?

Two or more over-budget weeks in a row usually means the weekly number is unrealistic, not that you're bad at this. That's useful information. Next month's plan should fix it, by raising the weekly number and lowering something else, rather than asking you to try harder.

### Minutes 5 to 10: Adjust

- Is any bill changing next month? A rent increase, a promotional rate ending, a debt you've paid off? Update your bills.
- Are there known one-off costs next month, like a wedding or an annual renewal? Make sure the right fund has enough, or add a new fund.

### Minutes 10 to 15: Assign

- **Leftover weekly money from this month:** roll it forward, or send it to a goal. Decide; don't let it drift.
- **A debt you paid off:** its old minimum payment rolls onto the next debt. This is what makes payoff speed up, and Chapter {{debt}} explains it.
- **A raise or extra income:** decide its split now, before you get used to it. A common choice is half to goals and half to the weekly number. You get to enjoy some of it, and your future self gets the rest.

### Minutes 15 to 20: Confirm

Check that next month's pool is zero or more, and note the new weekly number. If it changed, update the automatic transfer in your bank app before you finish. A plan that says $190 while the bank still sends $184 isn't a plan.

### Your monthly reset checklist

- Weekly check-ins reviewed and leftover money assigned
- Bills, funds and goals updated for next month
- Pool is $0 or more, and the new weekly number is noted
- Automatic transfers match the plan
- Sinking fund and debt balances updated from your statements

## The three-month question

Every third reset, ask one bigger question: *which single bill could I lower?*

Get one new insurance quote. Check one phone plan. Ask one lender about a lower rate. You don't need to do all of them; just one, every three months. One phone call that saves $40 a month adds up to $480 a year, with no ongoing effort at all. Chapters {{bill-audit}} and {{better-price}} have the scripts.

## What twenty minutes buys you

It's worth noticing how little time this is. Twenty minutes a week plus twenty minutes a month is about nineteen hours a year. In exchange, you always know where your money is, your bills and savings run themselves, and a bad week never becomes a bad month. Most people spend more time than that looking for a parking space.
