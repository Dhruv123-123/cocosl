# Your Emergency Fund {#emergency-fund}
Almost every chapter of this book has mentioned the emergency fund: it comes first in the goals, it protects your debt plan, and it's the first check before investing. This chapter brings it all together: how big it should be, where to keep it, how to build it, and when to use it.

## What it's for

An emergency fund is money set aside for things you can't predict and hope never happen: losing your job, a medical bill, an urgent car or home repair, an emergency trip to see family. It isn't for things you can predict. Those belong in sinking funds (Chapter {{sinking-funds}}).

Its real job is to keep a bad week from turning into a bad year. Without it, a $900 repair goes on a credit card at 25% interest, the debt plan stalls, and the stress of it makes everything else harder. With it, the same repair is an inconvenience. You pay it, you refill the fund, and you carry on.

## How big?

Build it in two stages.

**Stage one: a starter fund.** Aim for about one month of bills, or $500 to $1,000 if that feels more reachable. This covers most small emergencies, and it's enough to stop new card debt while you pay down the old.

**Stage two: the full fund.** Once high-interest debt is paid off, grow the fund toward three to six months of *essential* costs. Essential means what you'd need to keep going if your income stopped: housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments. It doesn't include eating out, travel or extra savings, which you'd stop in an emergency.

Where in that range you aim depends on your situation. Lean toward the higher end if:

- your income is irregular, or you're self-employed,
- you're the only earner in your household,
- your job is in an industry with frequent layoffs, or finding a new one would take a while,
- you have dependents, or
- you have health conditions or an older car or home that could need expensive repairs.

Lean toward the lower end if you have two stable incomes, low fixed costs, and good insurance.

## Where to keep it

The emergency fund has three requirements: it has to be **safe**, it has to be **available within a day or two**, and it should be **separate from your everyday money** so you don't spend it by accident.

That points to a high-yield savings account at a bank insured by the FDIC, or a credit union insured by the NCUA. Deposits are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category, which covers almost everyone's emergency fund many times over. Online banks often pay noticeably more interest than the big branch banks, so it's worth comparing a few.

What the emergency fund should *not* be in:

- **Investments.** The stock market can fall 20% or more in a year, and job losses tend to happen in the same years the market falls. You don't want to be forced to sell at the bottom.
- **Your checking account.** It's too easy to spend, and it usually earns little or nothing.
- **Cash at home**, apart from a small amount for power cuts or emergencies. It earns nothing and can be lost or stolen.

Some people keep part of a large emergency fund in certificates of deposit (CDs) or US Treasury bills to earn a little more, with only part of it in instant-access savings. That's reasonable once the fund is big, but keep at least a month or two of expenses where you can reach it immediately.

## How to build it

The same way everything else in this book works: automatically, on payday, before you see the money.

1. **Set a monthly emergency-fund goal** in your plan. Maya's is $200 a month.
2. **Schedule the transfer** for payday, to a savings account or bucket labeled "Emergency fund."
3. **Add windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses, gifts and "third paychecks" can build the fund much faster than monthly transfers alone.
4. **Add leftovers.** Weekly money you sweep at the monthly reset can go here until the starter fund is full.

### How long will it take?

Simple division tells you:

> months to target = amount still needed ÷ monthly contribution

At $200 a month, a $1,000 starter fund takes five months. A full fund of $9,000 would take about three and a half years at the same rate, which is why most people build the starter fund first, pay off high-interest debt, and then point the old debt payments at the emergency fund. Once a few hundred dollars of debt payments are freed up, the full fund grows much faster.

## When to use it

Before you use the emergency fund, ask three questions:

1. **Is it unexpected?** If you could have seen it coming, it should have a sinking fund. (If it doesn't yet, use the emergency fund this time and create the sinking fund at the next reset.)
2. **Is it necessary?** A car repair you need to get to work is. A great deal on a new TV isn't.
3. **Is it urgent?** If it can wait a month, it can probably be planned for at the next reset instead.

If the answer to all three is yes, use the fund without guilt. That's what it's for. Then add a refill to your next monthly reset, even a small one.

## If you lose your income

If the worst happens and your income stops, the emergency fund buys you time. A few steps to make it last:

- **Switch the plan to essentials only.** Pause goals and non-essential funds. Keep paying minimums on every debt.
- **Apply for unemployment benefits right away** if you're eligible. There's often a waiting period, and delays are common.
- **Call lenders and providers early** about hardship options, before you miss a payment.
- **Review your health coverage**, because losing a job often means losing workplace insurance. Look at your options, including any continuation coverage and marketplace plans, which often have special enrollment periods after a job loss.
- **Recalculate how many months you have**, based on essential costs only. Seeing a clear number is easier to live with than vague worry.

## A note on "but I have nothing saved"

Many people have no emergency savings at all, and building one can feel impossible when every dollar already has somewhere to go. If that's you, start smaller than you think you should. $10 a week is $520 in a year. Once the habit and the automatic transfer exist, raising the amount later is easy. The hardest part is the first transfer, not the size of it.
