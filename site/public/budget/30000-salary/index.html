How to budget on a $30,000 salary

$30,000 a year is $2,500 a month before tax and about $2,190 after federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare. Here's a plan for that money, how much rent it supports, and how much your state changes it.

A $30,000 budget, line by line

This is the four-line plan from how to make a budget you'll keep, filled in with $2,190 a month. The percentages are starting points for this income, not rules:

LineShare of take-homePer monthWhat goes in it
Bills60%$1,314Rent, utilities, phone, insurance, groceries baseline, minimum debt payments
Funds8%$175Monthly amounts for irregular costs: car repairs, gifts, renewals (sinking funds)
Goals10%$219Emergency fund, extra debt payments, retirement. Moved on payday.
Weekly number22%$111 a weekEating out, fun, shopping, top-up groceries. When it's gone, the week's done.

On $30,000 there's very little slack, so the order you pay things in matters more than the percentages. Cover rent, utilities, food and minimum payments first. The goals line is small on purpose: $219 a month still builds a $3,943 emergency fund in about 18 months, and that fund is what stops one car repair turning into card debt. If bills take more than 60% of take-home, the fix is usually housing (a roommate, or a cheaper area) rather than cutting coffee.

For comparison, the 50/30/20 rule would give $1,095 for needs, $657 for wants and $438 for savings.

How much rent fits on $30,000

The 30%-of-gross rule says up to $750 a month. Measured against take-home pay, 35% is about $767. Here's how that compares with fair market rents in the 25 largest US cities:

None of the 25 largest US cities has a fair-market one-bedroom under $750. The cheapest is El Paso at $969. With a roommate splitting a two-bedroom, these also fit: El Paso ($573 each), Oklahoma City ($622 each), San Antonio ($720 each), Houston ($748 each).

Emergency fund target

Three months of the bills line is $3,943; six months is $7,885. Putting the whole goals line toward it, the three-month version takes about 18 months. Keep it in a high-yield savings account so it earns something while it waits. More in how to build an emergency fund.

What a 401(k) costs you at this salary

Contributing 5% ($1,500 a year) lowers your take-home by only about $1,320 a year, $51 a paycheck, because traditional 401(k) money goes in before federal income tax. If your employer matches 50% of the first 6%, contributing 6% costs you about $1,590 a year in take-home and adds $900 of employer money. See how a 401(k) match works.

Your tax bracket on $30,000

Your taxable income (salary minus the $16,100 standard deduction) is $13,900, which puts your top dollars in the 12% federal bracket. Your average federal rate is only 4.7%, because each bracket only applies to the dollars inside it. You could earn $36,500 more before any dollar is taxed at 22%, and even then only the dollars above that line are. A raise never lowers your take-home pay.

Common questions

How much is $30,000 a year after taxes?

About $26,285 a year, or $2,190 a month, for a single filer in a state with no income tax in 2026, after $1,420 of federal income tax and $2,295 of Social Security and Medicare. In California it's about $2,172 a month; in New York City about $2,043.

How much is $30,000 a year per hour?

About $14 an hour before tax, based on 40 hours a week for 52 weeks (2,080 hours). After federal tax and FICA it's about $13 an hour.

How much rent can I afford on $30,000?

The common 30%-of-gross rule gives $750 a month. Measured against take-home pay, a 35% cap gives about $767 with no state income tax. Either way, rent plus your other fixed bills should stay near 60% of take-home for the plan on this page to work.

How much should I save on $30,000?

A starting point is 10% of take-home pay, about $219 a month, split between an emergency fund of $3,943 to $7,885 (three to six months of bills) and retirement. If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, contribute enough to get the full match first.
