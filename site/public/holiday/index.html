Your holiday budget in four numbers

Every January a lot of people open a card statement and wonder how December happened. It's rarely one big purchase; it's forty small ones. A plan takes about 30 minutes:

  1. One total. What you can spend from money you have now or will earn this month: your checking balance, minus bills through month-end and a small cushion, plus anything already set aside. Not next month's paycheck, and not a card balance you'll pay off "eventually".
  2. Four categories. Gifts; travel (door to door, including parking, rides and the pet sitter); food and hosting; and extras, the one people forget: outfits, decorations, tips, the white-elephant gift. If they add up to more than the total, shrink the biggest first.
  3. A per-person gift number. Divide the gift total by the number of names. $300 for 12 people is $25 each (an example, not a target). Some people get more and some less, but you decide that on purpose instead of at the register.
  4. Next year's number. In early January, add up what you actually spent and divide by 12. That's the monthly transfer into a holiday sinking fund, so next November the money is already there.

Track it in your phone's notes app: subtract every purchase from its category. When a category hits zero, you stop or move money from another category deliberately.

Black Friday traps

Deals can be real. Stores are also designed to make you decide fast. Five checks slow it down:

  • Was it on your list before the sale? If not, it sits in the cart for 24 hours. Countdown timers and "only 2 left" messages create urgency; a night's sleep removes it.
  • Is it really cheaper? Check the price history: some "sale" prices are what the item cost in October.
  • Cost per use. Divide the price by the times you'll realistically use it. In a made-up example, a $40 gadget used twice costs $20 a use; a $90 kitchen appliance used 100 times costs 90 cents. The cheap thing you never use is the expensive one.
  • Pay with a credit card you clear in full, not debit, when shopping online. Federal law caps your liability for unauthorized credit card charges at $50, and the money stays the bank's while you dispute it. With debit, it leaves your account right away. Turn on transaction alerts either way.
  • Does it fit the category? If not, pick something else, or move money on purpose.

The store card at checkout. "20% off if you open a card today" often comes with deferred interest: if any of the promotional balance is left when the promo ends, interest is charged all the way back to the purchase date, and store card rates are often near 30%. Opening one also means a hard inquiry. Unless you'd pay it off on the first statement, the discount isn't worth it.

Buy now, pay later: four rules

Pay-in-4 plans can cost nothing, if you follow four rules:

  1. One plan at a time. Several plans auto-pulling from a thin checking account is how a $50 payment becomes a $200 week.
  2. Know the late fee and where payments come from. An empty account can mean a late fee from the lender and an overdraft fee from your bank.
  3. Treat it as credit. It is a loan, and some providers report payments to the credit bureaus.
  4. Only for things you could pay for today. It's a payment schedule, not extra money.

If you're already juggling several plans, list each one with its next payment date and amount, and don't start another until they're done.

The year-end checklist

Ten moves, about an hour in total. The free printable above has room to tick them off.

  1. Spend your health FSA balance on things you'd buy anyway: glasses, contacts, prescriptions, many over-the-counter medicines.
  2. Set next year's 401(k) percentage, at least to the full match. Got a raise? Add 1%. (How a match works.)
  3. Run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator and give payroll a new W-4 if you're heading for a big refund or a bill.
  4. Check your credit reports and consider a freeze: both free. (How to freeze your credit.)
  5. Cancel subscriptions you didn't use this fall, including holiday free trials about to start charging. (Find them in 10 minutes.)
  6. Use work perks that reset in January: learning stipends, wellness or phone allowances.
  7. Write down your net worth as a baseline for next December.
  8. List next year's known big costs, each with its month and amount.
  9. Turn them into sinking funds: $600 of travel in June, starting in January, is $100 a month.
  10. Pick three goals with a number, a date and an automatic transfer, and set the transfers before January 1.

Common questions

Should I use buy now, pay later for holiday gifts?

Only for something you could pay for today, one plan at a time, with the payment dates in your calendar. Stacked plans pulling from a thin checking account are where late fees and overdrafts come from.

Is the store card discount worth it?

Usually not. Many store cards use deferred interest, so a balance left at the end of the promo is charged interest back to the purchase date, at rates often near 30%. Only take it if you'd pay the whole thing on the first statement.

How much should I spend on holiday gifts?

Whatever fits in money you have this month. Set a total first, then divide by the number of people for a per-person number. A smaller holiday you can pay for beats a bigger one that follows you into March.

What has to be done before December 31?

Spending a health FSA (unless your plan has a grace period or carryover), using perks that reset, and any extra 401(k) money for this year, which has to come out of this year's paychecks. IRA and HSA contributions for the year can generally wait until the April tax deadline.
