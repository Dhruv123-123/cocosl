Best high-yield savings accounts: how to compare them (and 5 to start with)

What actually matters in a high-yield savings account (insurance, fees, rate history, transfers), a side-by-side table of five well-known online options, and how to switch in 15 minutes.

A high-yield savings account (HYSA) is an ordinary, insured savings account that pays a much higher interest rate than the national average, usually because the bank is online-only and has lower costs. It's the usual home for an emergency fund and sinking funds.

We don't list exact rates in the table on purpose. HYSA rates are variable: banks change them whenever they like, often after Federal Reserve decisions. Any rate printed here would be wrong within weeks. Check each bank's current APY on its site, then use the criteria below to pick.

What to compare (in this order)

  1. Insurance. The account must be at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union. Using an app? Check who actually holds the money.
  2. Fees and minimums. No monthly fee, no minimum balance to avoid fees, no minimum to earn the advertised rate (or one you'll comfortably meet).
  3. The rate, and its history. A competitive APY today matters less than a bank that keeps its rate competitive. Some banks advertise a teaser rate for new money only.
  4. Conditions on the rate. Some top rates require a direct deposit, a linked checking account, or a balance tier.
  5. Transfers. Free transfers to your main checking account, ideally arriving within one to two business days.
  6. Extras you'll actually use. Savings "buckets" for sinking funds, a linked checking account, ATM access if you need cash.

Which one fits you

  • You want one app for checking and savings and you can set up direct deposit: look at SoFi first, and confirm what currently counts as a qualifying deposit.
  • You want savings buckets for sinking funds: Ally's buckets do the tracking for you.
  • You just want a simple, separate account that you'll rarely touch: Marcus or CIT, after checking which CIT product carries the rate you saw.
  • You like having a branch or café nearby: Capital One.

Also worth checking: your local credit union. Some pay competitive rates, and an NCUA-insured account at a credit union you already use can be the easiest option.

How to switch in 15 minutes

  1. Open the new account online (ID and Social Security number ready).
  2. Link your current checking account and make one small test transfer.
  3. Move the balance.
  4. Update your automatic payday transfer to point at the new account.
  5. Leave the old account open for a month to catch any stray transfers, then close it.

What a better rate is actually worth

On a $5,000 emergency fund, the difference between a 0.5% APY and a 4% APY is roughly $175 a year (hypothetical rates for illustration). Worth 15 minutes, but not worth chasing a slightly higher rate every month. Pick a good account and leave it.

Common questions

Is a high-yield savings account safe?

If it's at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union and you're within the coverage limit ($250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category), your deposits are protected if the institution fails.

Can the rate go down after I open it?

Yes. HYSA rates are variable. That's normal; a good bank keeps its rate competitive over time.

Is interest on savings taxed?

Yes, in the US interest is generally taxable income. Banks typically send a 1099-INT if you earn $10 or more in a year.

HYSA or money market account?

They're similar. A money market deposit account at a bank is also FDIC-insured and sometimes includes checks or a debit card. A money market fund at a brokerage is an investment and isn't FDIC-insured.
