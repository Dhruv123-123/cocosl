Balance transfer vs personal loan: which is cheaper for paying off card debt?

A 0% balance transfer card or a fixed-rate personal loan? How each works, the fees, a worked $6,000 example, and when neither is the right move.

If you're paying 20% or more on credit card debt, moving it somewhere cheaper can save hundreds or thousands of dollars. The two most common ways are a balance transfer card (a new card with a 0% or low promotional rate) and a personal loan (a fixed-rate loan you use to pay off the cards). Both can work. Which one wins depends mostly on one question: can you pay the balance off before the 0% period ends?

Short answer:

  • You can clear the debt within the promotional period (often 12 to 21 months): a balance transfer is usually cheapest.
  • You need longer, or want a fixed payment and a fixed end date: a personal loan.
  • You can't qualify for either at a good rate: call your card issuers and a nonprofit credit counselor first.

A worked example: $6,000 of card debt

Quinn (a fictional example) owes $6,000 on a card at 24.99% APR. Two offers, with hypothetical terms for illustration:

  • Balance transfer: 0% for 18 months, 3% transfer fee, then 26.99%.
  • Personal loan: 36 months at 12% APR, no origination fee.

If Quinn can pay about $345 a month: the balance transfer moves $6,180 (including the $180 fee) to the new card. At $344 a month, it's cleared in 18 months, just as the promotion ends. Total cost: $180.

If Quinn can only pay $250 a month: after 18 months, $1,680 is still on the card when the rate jumps to 26.99%. It takes about eight more months to clear, adding about $162 of interest. Total cost: about $342, still cheap, but only because Quinn keeps paying $250 without adding new purchases.

With the personal loan: a fixed payment of about $199 a month for 36 months. Total interest: about $1,174. Higher than either balance transfer scenario, but with a lower payment, a fixed end date and no promotional cliff to watch.

All three moves beat staying put. The balance transfer wins on cost if Quinn can keep up larger payments; the loan wins on predictability if money is tight.

If the loan had a 5% origination fee, Quinn would borrow about $6,316 to net $6,000, the payment would rise to about $210 and the total cost to about $1,552. Always compare the APR, which includes the fee, not just the interest rate.

Traps to avoid

  • New purchases on the balance transfer card. On many cards, new purchases are charged the regular APR and payments may go to the 0% balance first. Use a different card, or none, for spending.
  • Missing a payment. A late payment can end the promotional rate on some cards. Set up autopay for at least the minimum.
  • Paying the minimum on the transfer. The minimum is designed to leave a balance at the end. Divide the balance by the number of promotional months and pay that instead.
  • Choosing the longest loan term for the lowest payment. A 5-year loan costs much more interest than a 3-year loan at the same rate. (Debt consolidation, with the maths.)
  • Running the old cards back up. Moving debt doesn't remove the habit that built it. Keep the old cards open for your credit history, but out of your wallet.

If neither works

If you can't qualify for a good rate, or your budget can't cover the payments:

  • Call your card issuers and ask for a lower rate or a hardship program.
  • Talk to a nonprofit credit counselor. A debt management plan can lower your rates and combine payments without a new loan.
  • Build a payoff plan on the cards you have. Our debt payoff calculator shows how long the avalanche and snowball methods take. (Avalanche vs snowball.)

Be wary of debt settlement companies that advertise "consolidation" but ask you to stop paying your creditors. That can damage your credit and add fees.

Common questions

Does a balance transfer hurt your credit score?

Applying for a new card usually causes a small, temporary dip from the hard inquiry, and a new account lowers the average age of your accounts. Paying down the balance lowers your utilization, which often helps more over time, as long as you pay on time.

Is a personal loan or balance transfer better for credit?

Both can help if they lower your balances and you pay on time. A personal loan is installment debt, so moving card balances to it can lower your card utilization sharply. A balance transfer keeps the debt on a card, so utilization depends on the new card's limit.

Can I transfer a balance between two cards from the same bank?

Usually not. Most issuers don't allow balance transfers between their own cards, so you'll generally need a card from a different issuer.

What credit score do you need for a 0% balance transfer card?

The best offers usually go to people with good to excellent credit. Requirements vary by issuer. Checking for prequalified offers with a soft inquiry gives an idea of your chances without affecting your score.
