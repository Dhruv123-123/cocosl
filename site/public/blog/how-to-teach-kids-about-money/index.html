How to teach kids about money: simple lessons for every age

Age-by-age money lessons for young children, school-age kids and teens: saving, spending choices, allowances, first accounts and credit, with an example.

Children pick up ideas about money long before anyone sits them down to talk about it: from watching how adults pay, shop and talk about bills. You don't need to be a money expert to help. The CFPB's Money as You Grow resources put it simply: "No need to be a money expert." Small, regular conversations and a little real money to manage do most of the work.

This guide gives practical money lessons by age, ideas for allowances and first accounts, and a worked example of one family's system.

Why start early

Money skills are built gradually, like reading. Young children learn that money is exchanged for things and that choices have trade-offs. Older kids learn to plan, save and compare. Teens can practise with real accounts, paychecks and budgets while mistakes are still small and cheap. The CFPB groups its guidance into three stages, young children, school-age children to preteens, and teens to young adults, and suggests checking whether your child is reaching age-appropriate money milestones.

Money lessons by age

1. Young children (about 3 to 5)

  • Money buys things, and it runs out. Let them hand over cash at the store and get change.
  • Waiting. Practise waiting for something: a treat at the end of the week, not today.
  • Needs and wants. Sort items in a shopping trip into "need" and "want."
  • A clear jar. Seeing coins pile up makes saving real.

2. Early school age (about 6 to 9)

  • An allowance, even a small one, gives them something to manage.
  • Three jars: spend, save and give, or another split you choose.
  • Saving for a goal. A toy or a game they choose, with a picture on the jar.
  • Comparing prices. Show two versions of a product and talk about the difference.

3. Preteens (about 10 to 12)

  • A first savings account, so they see interest and statements.
  • Bigger choices. Let them make a spending decision you think is a mistake, then talk about it afterwards.
  • Advertising. Talk about how ads, influencers and in-game purchases try to get them to spend.
  • Earning. Extra paid jobs at home or in the neighbourhood link work and money.

4. Teens (about 13 to 17)

  • A real budget. Give a monthly amount that covers some of their own costs, such as lunches out, entertainment or clothes extras.
  • A debit card linked to a teen account, with alerts on.
  • Their first paycheck: read the pay stub together, including taxes. (How to read a pay stub.)
  • Credit basics: how cards and interest work, and why paying in full matters. (How credit card interest works.)
  • Scams: fake job offers, giveaways and messages asking for codes. (How to spot a scam text.)

5. Young adults (about 18 and up)

  • Building credit with a student or secured card used lightly. (Secured credit cards explained.)
  • Their own budget for college, a first job or a first apartment. (College student budget.)
  • Saving for retirement once they have earned income. (Retirement savings in your 20s.)

Allowances: a few approaches

There's no single right way. Common options:

  • A set weekly amount, unconnected to chores, to practise managing money.
  • Chores for pay, where some jobs earn money (beyond everyday family chores).
  • A mix: a base allowance, plus extra for bigger jobs.

Whatever you choose, keep it consistent, pay on time, and let them make choices with it. A common rule of thumb is to give more as they take on more of their own costs.

First accounts

A savings account in a child's name (usually a custodial or joint account with a parent) lets kids see deposits, interest and statements. Make sure it's at an FDIC-insured bank or an NCUA-insured credit union. Look for:

  • No monthly fees and no minimum balance.
  • A parent view or controls, if you want them.
  • A linked debit card for teens, with spending alerts.

Some parents also open a custodial investment account or a 529 plan for future education, which can be a way to talk about long-term investing. (How 529 plans work.)

A worked example: the Ellis family's system

The Ellis family (a fictional example) has two children, Finn, 7, and June, 14.

Finn gets $5 a week, split into three jars: $3 to spend, $1.50 to save, $0.50 to give. He wants a $24 toy. Saving only from the save jar, that's 16 weeks, which feels endless to a seven-year-old. His parents show him that if he also saves half of his spend jar, he'll get there in 8 weeks. He chooses 8 weeks, and learns he can speed up a goal by spending less.

June gets $25 a week, about $100 a month, and it now covers lunches out, entertainment and clothes extras. She opens a teen savings account and saves $10 a week toward a $400 bike: 40 weeks on her own. Her parents offer a 50% match on what she saves, which gets her there in about 27 weeks. She tracks it in a simple spreadsheet.

Everyday ways to talk about money

The CFPB's resources include conversation starters on topics like buying a car, getting a pet, moving home, paying bills and using a credit card. Other easy openings:

  • At the grocery store: "We have $80 for this trip. How should we spend it?"
  • When paying bills: show the electric bill and talk about what makes it go up.
  • When you say no: "That's not in this month's plan" teaches more than "We can't afford it."
  • On a big purchase: share how you saved for it or compared options.
  • When you make a mistake: talk about what you'd do differently.

Reading together helps too. The CFPB's Money as You Grow bookshelf suggests children's books with money lessons built in.

Common mistakes

  • Rescuing them every time. A small mistake now, like spending a whole allowance on day one, is a cheap lesson.
  • Keeping money a secret. You don't need to share every number, but silence makes money feel scary.
  • Lecturing. Short, regular conversations work better than one big talk.
  • Doing it all digitally too early. Young children understand physical money more easily than taps on a screen.

Talking about saving and interest

As children get older, you can show them how saving grows. A simple way: offer to pay "family interest" on their savings jar, such as 10 cents for every dollar they leave in it for a month. It's much higher than a bank would pay, but it makes the idea of interest concrete and rewards patience. Once they have a real savings account, look at the statement together and find the interest line.

For teens, compare a small amount saved every month from age 15 with the same amount started at 25. You can use a free compound interest calculator to show the difference. The point isn't the exact numbers, which depend on returns that can't be predicted, but the idea that time is a big advantage.

Money values, not just skills

Kids also learn what you value: giving, sharing, saving for things that matter, and not judging people by what they own. Talk about why your family spends money on some things and not others, and invite them to choose a cause for their "give" jar. Those conversations often stay with them longer than the arithmetic.

Handling "everyone else has one"

Sooner or later a child asks for something because their friends have it. It's a good moment for a money conversation rather than a flat no. Ask what they like about it, how much it costs, and how long it would take to save for from their own money. Sometimes they'll decide it isn't worth it; sometimes they'll save and buy it, and value it more.

Common questions

At what age should kids get an allowance?

Many families start around age five or six, when children can count and understand that money buys things. The right time is when your child can make simple choices with it. Start small and increase it as they take on more responsibility.

Should kids be paid for chores?

Families differ. Some keep everyday chores unpaid, as part of being in a family, and pay for extra jobs. Others link allowance to chores. Either works if the rules are clear and consistent.

When should a teen get a debit card?

Many teens get a debit card linked to a teen or joint account around 13 to 15, especially once they have their own money to manage. Set up alerts and talk about how to keep the card and its PIN safe.

How can I teach kids about money if I'm not good with it myself?

You don't need to be an expert. Learning alongside your child, being honest about what you're working on, and using free resources like the CFPB's Money as You Grow can be powerful lessons in themselves.
