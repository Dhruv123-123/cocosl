How to get a car loan: preapproval, comparing offers and avoiding markups

How to get preapproved, compare APR and total cost, avoid dealer rate markups and long terms, and handle add-ons, with a worked example of three loans.

For most people, a car is the second-biggest purchase after a home, and the loan can cost almost as much attention as the car. A difference of a couple of percentage points, or an extra two years on the loan, can add thousands of dollars. The good news is that getting a good car loan mostly comes down to doing things in the right order.

This guide explains how car loans work, how to get preapproved, how to compare offers, how dealer financing works, and a worked example of three loans for the same car.

How a car loan works

A car loan is a secured loan: the car is the collateral. If you stop paying, the lender can repossess it. You borrow a set amount, then repay it in fixed monthly payments over a set term, usually between three and seven years, with interest.

The key numbers in any offer:

  • Amount financed: what you're borrowing, after any down payment or trade-in.
  • APR: the yearly cost of the loan, including interest and certain fees. (APR vs APY explained.)
  • Term: how many months you'll pay.
  • Monthly payment and total cost: what you'll pay each month and in total.

How to get a car loan

1. Check your credit first

Your credit history has a big effect on the rate you're offered. Check your reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com and fix any errors before you apply. (How to check your credit report.)

2. Set a budget for the whole cost

Work out a monthly amount that covers the payment plus insurance, fuel, maintenance and registration. (How much car can I afford?.)

3. Get preapproved by a bank or credit union

The CFPB suggests getting a preapproved loan offer with a maximum amount and a rate before you shop. A preapproval tells you what you can afford and gives you something to compare the dealer's offer against.

4. Shop several lenders within a short window

Get quotes from two or three lenders. The CFPB notes that applying for car loans within a short period, generally around 14 to 45 days depending on the scoring model, usually counts as a single inquiry for your credit score.

5. Negotiate the car's price separately

Agree on the price of the car before you talk about financing, trade-ins or extras. Mixing them makes it hard to see what you're actually paying.

6. Ask the dealer to beat your preapproval

If the dealer offers financing, compare its APR and total cost with your preapproval. If it's better, take it. If not, use your own loan.

7. Check the paperwork before you sign

Make sure the amount, APR, term and payment match what you agreed, and that no add-ons were included that you didn't ask for. Don't take the car until the financing is final.

Dealer financing: how the markup works

When you finance through a dealer, the dealer sends your application to lenders: banks, credit unions or auto finance companies. The CFPB says most dealers contact about five lenders and present you with one loan.

The lender quotes the dealer a "buy rate", and the dealer may charge you that rate plus extra interest, which pays the dealer for arranging the loan. That's why the CFPB says getting a loan directly from a bank or credit union tends to be the cheaper option: you avoid the dealer's markup.

Dealer financing isn't always worse. Manufacturers sometimes offer promotional rates, including 0% APR, on new cars for buyers with strong credit. But a promotional rate may mean giving up a cash rebate, so compare both.

Why the loan term matters

A longer term lowers the monthly payment, which can make an expensive car look affordable. But you pay interest for longer. The CFPB's guide gives an example: a $20,000 loan at 4.75% costs about $1,498 in interest over 36 months and about $3,024 over 72 months. It also warns that "a lower monthly payment doesn't necessarily mean a lower interest rate." Some advisers suggest keeping car loans to five years or less.

Long loans also raise the risk of being "upside down": owing more than the car is worth, which is a problem if you need to sell it or it's written off.

A worked example: Abe compares three loans

Abe (a fictional example) needs to borrow $18,000 for a car. He's preapproved at a credit union at 6.5% APR, and the dealer offers its own loan at 7.5% APR over 72 months.

Credit union, 6.5%, 60 monthsabout $352about $3,130
Dealer, 7.5%, 72 monthsabout $311about $4,410

The dealer's monthly payment is the lowest, but it costs about $1,280 more in interest than the 60-month credit union loan, and Abe would pay for a year longer. Abe asks the dealer to beat 6.5%; it can't. He takes the 60-month credit union loan, which fits his budget.

Add-ons and extras

At signing, you may be offered an extended warranty or service contract, GAP insurance, credit insurance and other products. The CFPB notes they are optional, increase the amount you borrow (so you pay interest on them), and can often be bought more cheaply elsewhere, for example GAP coverage through your own car insurer. The CFPB also notes you have the right to cancel add-ons during the loan. If you want one, ask for its price separately and decide on its own merits.

If your credit isn't great

  • Start with a credit union, which may be more flexible with members.
  • Make a larger down payment to borrow less.
  • Choose a cheaper car rather than a longer loan.
  • Consider a co-signer only if both of you understand that they're fully responsible if you don't pay.
  • Avoid "buy here, pay here" lots unless you've compared other options: they can charge very high rates.
  • Refinance later. After a year of on-time payments, you may qualify for a lower rate.

Refinancing a car loan

If rates have dropped or your credit has improved, refinancing can lower your rate. Compare the new APR and total cost, check for any prepayment penalties or fees, and avoid stretching the term so far that you pay more overall.

If you're struggling to pay

Contact your lender before you miss a payment. Ask about deferring a payment or changing the due date. The CFPB has guidance for borrowers who owe more than the car is worth, are having trouble making payments, or are facing repossession, on its auto loans pages.

What to bring when you apply

Lenders usually ask for the same documents. Having them ready makes preapproval faster:

  • Proof of identity, such as a driver's licence.
  • Proof of income: recent pay stubs, or tax returns and bank statements if you're self-employed. (How to read a pay stub.)
  • Proof of address, such as a utility bill or lease.
  • Details of the car, if you've chosen one: the price, mileage and vehicle identification number (VIN).
  • Trade-in information, including any loan still owed on it.
  • Proof of insurance, or the details of the policy you'll put in place before you drive away.

New vs used car loans

Lenders often charge different rates for new and used cars, and rates can be higher for older cars or those with high mileage. Some lenders limit the age or mileage of the cars they'll finance. When you get preapproved, ask whether the rate depends on the car's age, and get a quote for the type of car you're actually considering. A slightly cheaper car with a lower rate can save more than you'd expect. (How to buy a used car.)

Common questions

What credit score do I need for a car loan?

There's no single minimum, and lenders lend to a wide range of credit scores. A higher score usually means a lower APR. Checking your reports and fixing errors before applying can make a difference.

Is it better to get financing from the dealer or a bank?

Get a quote from a bank or credit union first, then let the dealer try to beat it. The CFPB notes direct financing tends to be cheaper because you avoid dealer markups, but dealers sometimes have manufacturer promotions.

Can I pay off a car loan early?

Usually, yes, and paying early reduces the total interest. Check your loan agreement for any prepayment penalty first, and make sure extra payments go toward the principal.

What is GAP insurance and do I need it?

GAP insurance covers the difference between what you owe and what the car is worth if it's totalled or stolen. It can help if you made a small down payment or have a long loan. It's optional, and your own car insurer may offer it for less than the dealer.
