How to lower your credit card interest rate: a 10-minute call and what to say

When an issuer is likely to cut your APR, how to prepare, a word-for-word call script, what to do if they say no, and a worked example of the savings.

If you carry a balance on a credit card, the interest rate (APR) decides how much of each payment actually reduces what you owe. Card rates are high: the Federal Reserve's data has shown average rates on accounts that pay interest of over 20% in recent years. Even a few points lower can save hundreds of dollars.

One of the simplest ways to get a lower rate is also the most overlooked: ask. Issuers don't always agree, but a short, prepared phone call costs nothing and works more often than people expect. This guide covers when to call, what to say, and what to do if the answer is no.

How credit card interest adds up

Card interest is usually calculated daily on your balance and charged monthly. On a $4,000 balance at 28% APR, that's roughly $90 a month in interest at the start, which means much of a $200 payment never touches the balance. (How credit card interest works.)

Lowering the APR shifts more of every payment onto the balance, so you pay it off sooner and pay less overall.

When an issuer is most likely to say yes

Your chances are better if:

  • you've had the card for a year or more;
  • you've paid on time, every month;
  • your credit score has gone up since you opened the card (what counts as a good score);
  • you've received offers from other cards with lower rates or balance transfer deals;
  • you use the card regularly, so they'd rather keep you.

If you've recently missed payments, work on a few months of on-time payments first.

How to prepare (5 minutes)

  1. Find your current APR on your statement or in the app. Note whether you have more than one (purchase, balance transfer, cash advance).
  2. Know your balance and history: how long you've had the card, and that you've paid on time.
  3. Check your credit score in your banking app or a free service.
  4. Look up one or two alternatives: a balance transfer card or a personal loan rate you could qualify for. (Balance transfer vs personal loan.)
  5. Decide your ask: a specific number, like "below 22%," is easier to respond to than "lower."

How to ask for a lower rate

1. Call the number on the back of your card

Ask for the department that can review your interest rate. The first person may not have the authority; it's fine to ask politely for someone who does.

2. Make your case in three sentences

Say how long you've been a customer, that you pay on time, and that your rate is higher than other offers you've seen.

3. Ask for a specific rate

Name a number. Then stop talking and let them respond.

4. Ask the follow-up questions

If they agree: "Is this permanent or promotional? When does it take effect? Does it apply to my existing balance?" If they decline: "Is there anything else you can offer, like a hardship programme or a lower-rate promotion?"

5. Get it in writing

Ask them to confirm by email or letter, and check your next statement.

A script you can adapt

"Hi, I've had this card for three years and I've always paid on time. My current APR is 27.99%, and I've been getting offers from other cards at much lower rates. I'd like to keep using this card, so I'm calling to ask whether you can lower my rate to 21.99% or below."

If they say no:

"I understand. Is there any other option, such as a temporary lower rate or a hardship programme? I'm trying to pay this balance down in the next two years."

Keep it friendly and short. The person on the phone isn't the one who set your rate, and calm, polite requests tend to go further.

A worked example: Hugo's call

Hugo (a fictional example) has a $4,000 balance at 27.99% APR. Hugo has stopped using the card for new purchases and pays $200 a month.

APRMonths to pay offTotal interest
27.99% (no change)28about $1,450
24.99% (partial win)27about $1,230
21.99% (Hugo's ask)26about $1,030

Hugo calls, uses the script, and the issuer agrees to 21.99%. That ten-minute call saves about $420 in interest and gets Hugo debt-free two months sooner, at the same monthly payment. If Hugo had only got 24.99%, that would still have saved about $220.

(Fictional example. Interest simulated monthly at APR ÷ 12; your issuer's daily calculation and any new purchases will change the figures.)

If the answer is no

A "no" isn't the end. Your options:

  • Ask again in three to six months, after more on-time payments or a higher credit score.
  • Ask about a hardship programme if you're struggling. Some issuers offer temporarily lower rates or payments for people facing a job loss, illness or other difficulty.
  • Move the balance. A 0% balance transfer card can stop interest for a period, though there's usually a transfer fee of a few percent. (Best balance transfer cards.)
  • Consider a personal loan at a lower fixed rate, if you qualify, with a set payoff date.
  • Talk to a nonprofit credit counselling agency about a debt management plan, which may reduce rates on several cards.

Whatever you choose, the most important change is to stop adding new charges to the card you're paying down.

What a lower rate can and can't do

A rate cut makes your payments work harder, but it doesn't reduce the balance by itself. In Hugo's example, the payoff still takes more than two years. The biggest lever remains the size of your payment: adding even $50 a month to Hugo's $200 would cut the payoff time far more than the rate change alone. So treat a lower APR as a boost to a payoff plan, not a replacement for one.

It also helps to know which balance the new rate applies to. If you have a purchase balance and a cash advance balance at different rates, ask which one changes. Payments above the minimum generally go to the highest-rate balance first, but the minimum payment itself may go to lower-rate balances.

Things to avoid

  • Closing the card in frustration. It can lower your available credit and raise your utilization, which can hurt your score. Pay it down instead.
  • Paying for a "rate reduction" service. Anyone charging upfront fees to call your issuer for you is doing what you can do yourself for free, and some are scams.
  • Moving debt without a plan. A lower rate only helps if the balance goes down. (Avalanche vs snowball.)

What else to ask for while you're on the phone

If you've got a representative's attention, a few other requests are worth making:

  • A waiver of a recent late fee, if you've otherwise paid on time.
  • Removal of the annual fee, or a switch to a no-fee card from the same issuer, which keeps your account history.
  • A change of due date to a few days after payday, which makes on-time payments easier.

Each one is a small saving, but together they can add up to more than the rate cut itself.

Keep the momentum after the call

Write the new rate and the date in your budget, then set up autopay for a fixed amount above the minimum. Each time a statement arrives, check the APR shown matches what you were told. And put a reminder in your calendar for six months' time: if your score has improved again, it may be worth another call.

If you have more than one card

Call about the card with the highest rate and largest balance first: it has the most interest to save. Then work through the rest. Keep notes of who you spoke to, the date and what was offered.

Common questions

Does asking for a lower interest rate hurt my credit?

Usually not. Asking your current issuer to review your rate generally doesn't involve a hard credit check, though it's worth asking the representative to confirm. A balance transfer card or new loan application would involve one.

How much can I get my credit card rate lowered?

There's no set amount. Some issuers offer a few percentage points, some offer a temporary promotional rate, and some decline. A strong payment history and a better credit score improve your chances.

Can I ask for a lower rate if I've missed payments?

You can ask, but the answer is more likely to be no. If you're having difficulty, ask about a hardship programme instead, and build a few months of on-time payments before asking again.

Will the lower rate apply to my existing balance?

Ask when you call. Usually a permanent rate reduction applies to the balance going forward, but promotional offers can have conditions and end dates.
