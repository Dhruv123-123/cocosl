How to save money on car insurance without being underinsured

Shopping quotes, deductibles, discounts and when to drop collision, plus what not to cut, with a worked example of a renewal that saved hundreds.

Car insurance is one of the few bills that tends to go up every year whether or not you've done anything wrong. Insurers adjust prices for repair costs, claims in your area and many other factors, and loyalty is rarely rewarded. The upside is that it's also one of the bills with the most room to cut, often without reducing the protection that matters.

This guide covers the changes that usually save the most, the ones that are a false economy, and a worked example of a renewal.

What decides your price

Insurers look at many things, including:

  • Your car: its value, repair costs, safety record and theft risk.
  • Where it's kept: local accident, theft and weather claims.
  • How much you drive and what for (commuting, pleasure, business).
  • Your driving record: accidents and violations.
  • Your coverage: limits, deductibles and optional extras.
  • Credit-based insurance scores, in most states.
  • Age and experience, especially for young drivers.

Some of these you can't change quickly. The rest is where the savings are.

The main parts of a policy

  • Liability: pays for injuries and damage you cause to others. Almost every state requires it, with minimum limits.
  • Collision: pays to repair your car after a crash, minus your deductible.
  • Comprehensive: pays for theft, vandalism, hail, flood, fire and hitting an animal, minus your deductible.
  • Uninsured/underinsured motorist: covers you if the other driver has no or too little insurance.
  • Medical payments or personal injury protection (PIP): depending on your state.
  • Extras: rental car, roadside assistance, gap insurance.

How to lower your premium

1. Get at least three quotes every year or two

Prices for the same driver and car can vary widely between insurers. Compare quotes with the same limits and deductibles, so you're comparing like with like. An independent agent can check several insurers at once.

2. Raise your deductibles, carefully

A higher deductible on collision and comprehensive usually lowers your premium. Choose an amount you could pay from savings tomorrow, like $1,000, and make sure your emergency fund covers it. (How much emergency fund you need.)

3. Bundle policies

Buying car and renters (or home) insurance from the same company often earns a multi-policy discount. Compare the bundle total with buying separately. (Renters insurance explained.)

4. Ask for every discount you might qualify for

Common ones include a clean driving record, defensive driving courses, good student grades, low annual mileage, safety features, paying in full, paperless billing and automatic payments. Some groups, employers and alumni associations have arranged discounts too.

5. Report lower mileage

If you've started working from home or moved closer to work, tell your insurer. Some offer usage-based or pay-per-mile programmes that track your driving; they can save money for careful, low-mileage drivers, but read what data they collect.

6. Consider dropping collision and comprehensive on an older car

If your car is worth little, the yearly cost of collision and comprehensive can approach what you'd ever get back. A common rule of thumb: if those coverages cost more than about 10% of the car's value a year, or you could replace the car from savings, it may be time to drop them. Keep liability, of course.

7. Look after your credit

In most states, insurers use credit-based insurance scores. Paying bills on time and keeping card balances low can help over time. (What is a good credit score?.)

8. Check before buying your next car

Get insurance quotes on the specific models you're considering. Two similar cars can cost noticeably different amounts to insure. (How much car can I afford?.)

9. Pay annually if you can

Some insurers charge installment fees for monthly payments. Saving for the annual premium in a sinking fund avoids them. (Sinking funds, explained.)

What not to cut

  • Liability limits. State minimums are often low compared with the cost of a serious accident. If you cause more damage than your limits cover, you can be personally responsible for the rest. Raising liability limits usually costs less than people expect.
  • Uninsured motorist coverage. It protects you from other people's lack of insurance.
  • Collision on a car you couldn't replace. If losing the car would leave you without a way to work and no savings to replace it, keep collision.

A worked example: Finn's renewal

Finn (a fictional example) gets a renewal notice for $1,980 a year, up from $1,700. Finn drives a 10-year-old sedan worth about $5,500 and has liability limits of 50/100/50 with $500 deductibles on collision and comprehensive. Finn spends an hour on it:

ChangeEffect on yearly premium
Three quotes, same coverage: best is$1,720
Raise collision deductible to $1,000−$110
Move renters insurance to the same insurer (multi-policy discount)−$90
Low-mileage discount (works from home 3 days a week)−$60
Raise liability limits to 100/300/100+$85
New premium$1,545

Finn pays $435 less than the renewal price and has higher liability protection.

Finn also considered dropping collision. It costs about $420 a year on a car worth $5,500, roughly 8% of its value. Finn decided to keep it for now, because replacing the car would be hard, and to look again next year.

How to compare quotes fairly

Quotes are only useful if they're for the same thing. Before you start, write down your current coverage from your declarations page: the liability limits (shown like 50/100/50, meaning $50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident for injuries, and $50,000 for property damage), your deductibles, and any extras like rental reimbursement. Ask each insurer to quote exactly that, then ask what it would cost with higher liability limits or a higher deductible. Also note each insurer's reputation for handling claims, because a cheap policy that's hard to claim on isn't a bargain.

When to shop around

  • every year at renewal, or at least every two years;
  • after a move, a new car, marriage, or a change in commute;
  • when an accident or ticket drops off your record (often after three to five years);
  • when you turn 25, for many younger drivers.

Don't let coverage lapse when switching: start the new policy before cancelling the old one.

Should you claim for small damage?

A claim close to your deductible may not be worth making. If repairs cost $1,200 and your deductible is $1,000, you'd receive $200, and an at-fault claim could raise your premium for several years. Get a repair estimate first, then decide. For anything large, or any accident involving injuries or another driver, report it to your insurer promptly as your policy requires.

After a claim or a ticket

Your premium may rise after an at-fault accident or a moving violation. It's still worth shopping around, because insurers weigh these differently. Some offer accident forgiveness for a first incident, and a defensive driving course can sometimes reduce points or earn a discount.

Gap insurance and other extras

Optional coverages can be worth it, but check each one:

  • Gap insurance pays the difference between what you owe on a loan or lease and what the car is worth if it's totalled. It's useful in the first years of a loan with a small down payment, and pointless once you owe less than the car is worth. It's often cheaper from your insurer than from the dealer.
  • Rental reimbursement pays for a rental car while yours is repaired after a covered claim. Useful if you have no other way to get to work.
  • Roadside assistance may duplicate a membership you already have, or coverage that comes with a credit card or your car's warranty.

Remove extras you're paying for twice; keep the ones that cover a real gap.

Young drivers

Insurance is most expensive for new and young drivers. The biggest savings usually come from staying on a family policy if possible, good-student discounts, a safe and inexpensive car, and a clean record. Prices often fall noticeably with a few years of experience.

Common questions

How often should I shop for car insurance?

At least every year or two, and after any big change like a move or a new car. Prices change constantly, and staying with the same insurer doesn't always get you the best rate.

Does a higher deductible save money?

Usually, yes, because you take on more of the cost of a claim. Only raise it to an amount you could comfortably pay from savings, since you'll have to pay it after an accident.

When should I drop full coverage on an old car?

Consider it when the yearly cost of collision and comprehensive is a large share of the car's value, or when you could replace the car from savings. Keep liability coverage, which is required in almost every state.

Will getting quotes affect my credit?

Insurance quotes generally use a soft check that doesn't affect your credit score. Check with the insurer if you're unsure.
