How to choose a checking account: the fees, features and fine print that matter

What to compare when choosing a checking account (fees, minimums, overdraft rules, ATMs, deposit insurance) and a worked example of a year of fees.

A checking account is the hub of your money: your pay lands there, your bills leave from it, and your debit card draws on it. Most people open one when they're young and never look again. But the wrong account can quietly cost a few hundred dollars a year in fees, while the right one costs nothing.

This guide covers what to compare, the fine print worth reading, and how to switch without missing a payment.

What to compare

1. Monthly maintenance fees

Many accounts charge a monthly fee unless you meet a condition, like a minimum daily balance or a minimum amount of direct deposits. Check:

  • the fee itself;
  • every way to waive it;
  • whether you'd reliably meet the condition every month, not just most months.

Plenty of online banks and credit unions offer checking with no monthly fee and no minimum.

2. Overdraft rules and fees

Ask: how much is the overdraft fee, is there a daily limit, is there a grace period, and is debit-card overdraft coverage on or off by default? Some accounts simply decline transactions you can't cover and charge nothing. Others offer free transfers from linked savings. (How to avoid overdraft fees.)

3. ATM access

Look at the size of the fee-free ATM network, the fee for using other ATMs, and whether the bank refunds other banks' ATM fees. Two out-of-network withdrawals a month can easily cost more than $100 a year.

4. Deposit insurance

Make sure the account is at an FDIC-insured bank or an NCUA-insured credit union. The standard FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. Be careful with apps that aren't banks themselves: your money should be held at an insured bank in your name. (Is my savings app FDIC insured?.)

5. How fast deposits are available

Banks set their own hold policies within federal limits. If you deposit checks, ask how soon the money is available. Many accounts make direct deposit available on payday, and some a day or two early.

6. Everyday features

  • A good mobile app with alerts (low balance, large purchases, deposits).
  • Mobile check deposit.
  • Free bill pay and transfers (Zelle or similar).
  • Easy links to a savings account, ideally a high-yield one. (Best high-yield savings accounts.)
  • Branch or phone support, if that matters to you.

7. Other fees

Read the fee schedule for paper statements, cashier's checks, wire transfers, foreign transactions, stop payments and account closure. Most people only hit one or two of these, but it's worth knowing which.

How to switch accounts

1. Open the new account first

Keep the old one open while you move everything.

2. Move your direct deposit

Give your employer the new account details, or use your payroll portal. Some people split pay between both accounts for one cycle.

3. Move automatic payments

List every autopay and bill from the last three months of statements (rent, phone, insurance, subscriptions, loan payments) and update each one.

4. Leave a small balance in the old account

Keep enough to cover anything you missed for a month or two.

5. Close the old account

Once a full cycle passes with no activity, withdraw the balance and close it in writing. Ask for confirmation that it's closed with a zero balance.

Second-chance accounts

If you've been turned down for an account because of past overdrafts or closed accounts (often recorded by a reporting company such as ChexSystems), look for second-chance or "checkless" accounts at banks and credit unions. They usually have fewer features and sometimes a small monthly fee, but they give you a way back into mainstream banking. After a year or so of good history, ask about moving to a standard account. You can also request your report from the reporting company and dispute errors.

Questions to ask before you open an account

Bank websites highlight the best features. These questions get at the fine print. Ask them in a branch, over the phone or in chat, and note the answers:

  1. What is the monthly fee, and every way to avoid it? Get the exact balance or deposit amount, and whether it's a daily minimum or a monthly average.
  2. What happens if a debit card purchase would overdraw the account? Declined with no fee, covered for a fee, or covered from linked savings?
  3. What does an overdraft or returned payment cost, and how many can be charged per day?
  4. How many ATMs are fee-free, and do you refund other banks' ATM fees? If so, up to how much a month?
  5. When are direct deposits and mobile check deposits available to spend?
  6. Is there a minimum opening deposit or a fee for closing the account early? Some banks charge if you close within a few months.
  7. Is the account FDIC or NCUA insured? If it's an app, which bank holds the money?

Write the answers next to each other for two or three accounts. Usually one clearly fits your habits better.

Match the account to your habits

The cheapest account on paper isn't always the cheapest for you. Think about how you actually use your money:

  • You're paid in cash or tips: a bank or credit union with nearby branches or cash-deposit ATMs matters more than a slick app.
  • Your balance runs low before payday: prioritise no overdraft fees and low-balance alerts over interest or rewards.
  • You travel or move a lot: a large ATM network or ATM fee refunds can save the most.
  • You keep a large balance: an interest-bearing account or a linked high-yield savings account makes sense, as long as fees don't eat the interest.
  • You share bills with a partner: look for easy joint accounts and transfers. (How to split finances as a couple.)

Keep checking and savings separate

Even the best checking account works better alongside a separate savings account. Money you can see in checking tends to get spent. Keeping your emergency fund in a different account, ideally one that earns interest and isn't linked to your debit card, makes it easier to leave alone, while still letting you move it within a day or two when you need it.

Common questions

How many checking accounts should I have?

One is enough for most people. Some couples keep a joint account for shared bills plus individual accounts. Pair your checking account with a separate savings account for your emergency fund.

Does opening a checking account affect my credit score?

Usually not. Banks may check a consumer reporting company that tracks banking history, which isn't a credit report inquiry. Some banks do a soft credit check, which doesn't affect your score. Ask if you're unsure.

What's the minimum I need to open a checking account?

It varies. Many online banks and credit unions have no minimum opening deposit; some banks require $25 to $100. A credit union may also ask for a small deposit into a membership savings share.

Is an online bank safe?

An online bank that's FDIC insured has the same deposit insurance as a bank with branches. Check the bank's name on the FDIC's BankFind tool, and make sure an app that isn't a bank holds your money at an insured partner bank.
