A sinking fund for car repairs: how much to save each month (with a real tally)

Turn tires, brakes and surprise repairs into one monthly number: a car repair sinking fund with a real tally and what happens when a repair comes early.

Ask anyone who has owned an older car what their worst money month was, and there's a good chance the answer involves a mechanic. A $900 brake job or a $1,200 repair arrives, it goes on a credit card, and three months of interest later it has cost $1,000 or more.

The repair wasn't really a surprise, though. Cars need tires, brakes, batteries and the odd unexpected fix. You don't know which month, but you know it's coming. That's exactly what a sinking fund is for: a small monthly amount set aside for a cost you know will arrive, so the money is already there when it does. (Sinking funds, explained.)

This guide shows how to work out your own car's number, with a worked example, and what to do when the big repair comes before the fund is full.

Emergency fund or car fund?

They do different jobs.

  • An emergency fund is for things you can't predict: a job loss, a medical bill, an urgent trip. (Build a starter one.)
  • A car repair fund is for things you can predict in total but not in timing: tires every few years, brakes, a battery, and a yearly allowance for the repair you can't name yet.

Keeping them separate matters. If car repairs come out of the emergency fund, it's half-empty the day a real emergency arrives. And seeing a separate "car" balance makes it much easier to say yes to the mechanic without panic, or no to an upsell you don't need.

What does a car cost to keep running?

AAA's yearly driving-cost study puts maintenance, repair and tires at about 11 cents a mile for the average new vehicle. At 12,000 miles a year, that's roughly $1,300 a year, or about $110 a month.

Two caveats. AAA's figure is an average across new cars over their first five years, so an older car with more miles usually costs more in repairs, and a brand-new car under warranty usually costs less in its first couple of years. And the average hides the lumpiness: you won't spend $110 every month. You'll spend $80 in March and $900 in August.

That's why it's worth doing your own tally. It takes about 30 minutes and gives you a number you trust.

How to set up a car repair fund

1. List the regular maintenance and its interval

Check your owner's manual (or the manufacturer's website) for oil change, tire rotation and filter intervals. Write down each job, how often it happens, and roughly what your local shop charges. Your past receipts or bank statements are the quickest source for prices.

2. Add the wear items with their lifespan

Tires, brake pads and rotors, the battery, wiper blades. For each, estimate the cost and how many years it lasts at your mileage. Divide cost by years to get a yearly figure.

3. Add an allowance for the repair you can't name

Something will break that isn't on the list: a sensor, a water pump, a suspension part. For a car under warranty, this can be small. For an older car past 100,000 miles, $500 to $1,000 a year is a sensible starting allowance, and your own repair history is the best guide.

4. Divide the yearly total by 12

That's your monthly transfer. Round it up to a number that's easy to remember.

5. Automate it into a separate savings account

Set the transfer for the day after payday, into a high-yield savings account you name "Car". It earns interest while it waits, and the name makes it harder to raid for something else. (How to open one.)

A worked example: Sam's 2015 Corolla

Sam (a fictional example) drives a 2015 Toyota Corolla with 105,000 miles, about 12,000 miles a year. These are rounded, typical figures for illustration, not quotes.

Tires (set of four, fitted)$720every 4 years$180
Brake pads and rotors$450every 3 years$150
Battery$220every 4 years$55
Wipers, filters, small parts$90yearly$90
Unknown repairs allowance$700yearly$700
Total$1,335

$1,335 ÷ 12 = $111.25 a month. Sam rounds it to $115.

The cross-check: AAA's 11.04 cents a mile at 12,000 miles is $1,325 a year for the average new car. Sam's tally lands right next to it, with most of the difference being the larger unknown-repairs allowance that a 105,000-mile car deserves.

Notice what's not in the table: insurance, registration and fuel. Those are real car costs too, but insurance and registration are known bills with due dates (they can have their own sinking fund or go in your monthly bills), and fuel is a weekly spending cost. Keep the repair fund for repairs, or it becomes impossible to tell whether it's on track.

What happens when the big repair comes early

Sam starts the fund with a $500 deposit from a tax refund, then adds $115 a month.

  • After 6 months, the fund holds $500 + 6 × $115 = $1,190.
  • In month 7, the mechanic finds worn brakes and a failing wheel bearing: $1,100.
  • Month 7's transfer brings the fund to $1,305. Sam pays the $1,100 in full, leaving $205.

No card, no interest, no week of stress. The fund then rebuilds at $115 a month.

Now the other version: the same $1,100 repair in month 2, when the fund holds only $730. Sam pays $730 from the fund and $370 from the emergency fund, then pauses other savings goals until the emergency fund is topped back up. It's not perfect, but $370 is a far smaller problem than $1,100 on a card at 25%.

That's the real job of a sinking fund. It doesn't need to be full to help: every month it runs, the next repair gets smaller.

Starting when money is tight

If $115 a month isn't possible yet, start with what is: $40 a month is still $480 a year that won't go on a card. Then raise the transfer when something else ends, like a subscription you cancel or a debt you pay off. The order matters more than the amount: set up the automatic transfer first, at whatever size works, because a fund that exists at $40 a month beats a perfect plan that never starts.

How to save money on car repairs (without skipping them)

The fund makes repairs affordable. These habits make them smaller:

  • Do the cheap maintenance on time. Oil changes and tire rotations are cheap insurance against engine and tire replacements.
  • Get a second quote for any repair over a few hundred dollars. Ask for the old parts back and an itemized estimate.
  • Ask which jobs are urgent and which can wait a few months. Then let the fund catch up.
  • Don't let "while we're in there" upsells take the fund on a single visit. A fluid flush that isn't in your manual's schedule can usually wait.
  • Check for recalls on your car's VIN. Recall repairs are free.

When the fund keeps running dry

If big repairs keep outrunning the fund, that's information. Add up what the car has cost in repairs over the last two years and compare it with what a replacement would cost a month. At some point, a reliable used car with a payment can be cheaper than a free car that's in the shop every quarter. The fund makes that comparison honest, because you can see the real yearly cost.

And when you do replace it, the car fund keeps going. The next car needs tires too, and if you're buying, the same habit builds the down payment.

Common questions

How much should I save for car repairs each month?

Add up your car's yearly maintenance and wear items, plus an allowance for unexpected repairs, and divide by 12. As a rough check, AAA puts maintenance, repair and tires at about 11 cents a mile for an average new car, so around $100 to $125 a month at 12,000 miles. Older, high-mileage cars usually need more.

Should car repairs come out of my emergency fund?

Ideally not. Repairs are predictable in total, so a separate car fund keeps your emergency fund whole for things you can't predict. If a repair arrives before the car fund is ready, use the emergency fund for the gap and refill it first.

Where should I keep a car repair fund?

In a separate high-yield savings account, named for its purpose. It earns interest, it's FDIC-insured at an insured bank, and it's one step removed from your checking account.

Do I need a car fund if my car is new?

A smaller one. Warranty covers most repairs early on, but tires, brakes and maintenance aren't free. Starting the habit while the costs are low means the fund is already healthy when the warranty ends.
