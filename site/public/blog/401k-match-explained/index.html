The raise you're ignoring: how a 401(k) match works

A common 401(k) match is 50% of what you contribute, up to 6% of pay. Here's how matches work, how vesting works, and how to check you're getting all of it.

There's a good chance part of your pay is sitting unclaimed. Many employers match contributions to a workplace retirement plan like a 401(k) or 403(b): you put money in, they add more. If you're not contributing enough to get the full match, you're turning down money your employer has already offered you.

How a match works

Every plan has its own formula, written in its summary plan description. A common one:

50% of what you contribute, up to 6% of your pay.

That means if you contribute 6% of your salary, your employer adds 3%. Contribute 4%, they add 2%. Contribute 10%, they still add 3% (the match stops at 6%).

Example with numbers

On a $45,000 salary with that formula:

You contributeYou put in per yearEmployer addsTotal
0%$0$0$0
3%$1,350$675$2,025
6%$2,700$1,350$4,050
10%$4,500$1,350$5,850

At 6%, the match is an instant 50% on the money you put in, before any investment growth. Few things in personal finance come close.

Other common formulas: 100% of the first 3% to 4%, or 100% of the first 3% plus 50% of the next 2%. Some employers add a fixed contribution whether you contribute or not.

It costs less than it looks

Traditional (pre-tax) 401(k) contributions come out before income tax, so contributing $100 per paycheck reduces your take-home pay by less than $100. Roth 401(k) contributions are after-tax, so they reduce take-home pay dollar for dollar, but the money comes out tax-free later. Many plans offer both; matching dollars may go in as pre-tax by default.

Vesting: when the match is really yours

Your own contributions are always 100% yours. The employer's match may vest over time: for example, 20% per year over five years, or all at once after three years. Leave before you're vested and you can lose some or all of the unvested match.

Find your plan's vesting schedule in the plan documents or your account dashboard. It doesn't change the advice (still take the match), but it's useful to know before you change jobs.

How to check you're getting the full match (10 minutes)

  1. Log in to your plan's website (your HR portal or benefits page will link it).
  2. Find the match formula in the plan summary, or ask HR: "What's the match formula, and is there a vesting schedule?"
  3. Check your contribution rate. Is it at least the percentage needed for the full match?
  4. Check your investments. New contributions sometimes sit in a default cash or money market fund. Many plans default to a target-date fund, which is a reasonable one-fund choice; just make sure the money is actually invested.
  5. If you can't afford the full match yet, start with what you can and set an automatic increase of 1% a year, if your plan offers it.

Match first, then the rest

A common order for people starting out:

  1. Minimum payments on all debts, and a starter emergency fund.
  2. Enough in the 401(k) to get the full match.
  3. Pay down high-interest debt (credit cards). See avalanche vs snowball.
  4. Build the full emergency fund.
  5. More retirement saving: an IRA, or more in the 401(k).

Common questions

What's the 401(k) contribution limit?

For 2026, the IRS limit on employee contributions is $24,500 (higher with catch-up contributions from age 50). The match doesn't count toward that employee limit. Check the IRS site for the current year.

My employer doesn't offer a match. Should I still contribute?

It can still be worth it for the tax benefits, but without a match, an IRA (often with lower fees and more fund choices) may be a better first stop. Compare fees.

I'm a contractor or freelancer. Is there an equivalent?

Self-employed people can use a SEP IRA or a solo 401(k). There's no employer match, but contribution limits can be generous.

What happens to my 401(k) if I leave?

Your vested balance stays yours. You can usually leave it, roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Avoid cashing out, which typically triggers tax and a 10% penalty if you're under 59½.
