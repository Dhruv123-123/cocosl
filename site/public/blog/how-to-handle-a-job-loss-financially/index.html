How to handle a job loss financially: the first week, the first month, and after

What to do in the first days after losing a job: unemployment benefits, health coverage, a bare-bones budget, bills, your 401(k), and a worked example.

Losing a job is a shock, emotionally and financially. The days after are often a blur, but a few early decisions make a big difference to how long your money lasts: applying for benefits quickly, sorting out health coverage, and cutting your budget to the essentials before savings run down.

This guide sets out what to do in the first few days, the first month, and beyond, with a worked example of one household's plan.

In the first few days

You don't need to solve everything at once. Focus on four things.

What to do after losing your job

1. Apply for unemployment benefits straight away

Unemployment insurance is a joint federal-state programme, and the Department of Labor says each state runs its own. You generally qualify if you lost your job through no fault of your own and earned enough during a recent "base period." The DOL says to file in the state where you worked, and to contact the programme soon after losing your job. You can usually apply online, by phone or in person. The first payment typically arrives two to three weeks after you file, so the sooner you apply, the better.

2. Sort out health coverage

Healthcare.gov describes the main options:

  • Marketplace coverage: you may qualify for savings based on your income and household size, not your employment status. Losing job-based coverage usually lets you enrol outside open enrolment, for a limited time.
  • COBRA: a federal law that lets you keep your workplace coverage temporarily, but you pay the full premium plus a small administrative fee.
  • Medicaid: free or low-cost coverage for some people with low incomes. The Marketplace application also checks whether you qualify.

Compare the costs before choosing. COBRA keeps the same doctors and plan but is often much more expensive than a Marketplace plan with savings. (Health insurance, explained.)

3. Get your final pay and paperwork

Check your final paycheck, any unused vacation pay your state or employer requires, severance terms, and the date benefits end. Keep your separation letter and pay stubs: you'll need them for unemployment. (How to read a pay stub.)

4. Switch to a bare-bones budget

List your essential costs: housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, phone and minimum debt payments. Pause everything else for now. The goal is to stretch your savings and benefits as far as possible. (How to budget on a low income.)

5. Call your lenders and providers early

Before you miss a payment, contact your landlord, utilities, card issuers and loan servicers. Ask about hardship programmes, payment plans or deferrals. Many have options, but only if you ask before you fall behind.

6. Leave your 401(k) alone

Cashing out a retirement account usually means income tax and often a 10% early withdrawal penalty. If you need to move it, roll it into an IRA or a new employer's plan. Treat it as a last resort, not a first step.

A worked example: Dee's runway

Dee (a fictional example) is laid off. Dee's take-home pay was $3,400 a month, and Dee has $4,000 in savings.

Essential costs:

Rent$1,250
Food$420
Minimum debt payments$200
Transport$180
Utilities$160
Insurance (renters and car)$95
Phone$45
Total$2,350

Dee pauses the gym and streaming ($75), moves to a cheaper phone plan ($20 less) and trims groceries by $70, bringing essentials to about $2,185.

Dee applies for unemployment on day one. The estimated benefit (it varies by state and past earnings) is about $1,600 a month, starting after about three weeks.

  • Monthly gap: $2,185 − $1,600 = $585
  • Runway: $4,000 ÷ $585 ≈ 6.8 months

For health coverage, Dee compares COBRA (the full premium) with a Marketplace plan that's cheaper because Dee's income for the year is now lower, and picks the Marketplace plan.

In the first month

  • Know your runway. Divide savings by your monthly gap after benefits. That number guides every decision.
  • Look for help with essentials. The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps eligible households with energy bills, and food banks and local programmes can free up cash for rent.
  • Handle debt carefully. Pay at least the minimums on everything to protect your credit. If you can't, prioritise housing, utilities, food and transport, then talk to lenders. (How to stop living paycheck to paycheck.)
  • Avoid high-cost borrowing. Payday loans can turn a short gap into a long problem. (Payday loans explained.)
  • Check student loan options. Federal student loans have options for borrowers with reduced income; contact your servicer. (How to pay off student loans faster.)

Taxes on unemployment benefits

Unemployment benefits are generally taxable income on your federal return, and in many states. Many states let you have tax withheld from your payments; doing so can save you a surprise bill when you file. If you take on freelance work while job hunting, set aside money for tax on that too. (Estimated tax payments explained.)

Finding work

Your state's unemployment programme often requires you to look for work and report your job search each week. CareerOneStop, sponsored by the Department of Labor, has job search tools, training information and links to local job centres. Use the time to update your résumé, reach out to your network and consider short-term or freelance work, which may reduce but not end your benefits, depending on your state's rules.

When you're working again

Rebuild in this order:

  1. Restart the emergency fund, aiming first to replace what you used. (How to build an emergency fund.)
  2. Catch up on anything you deferred, such as payments you paused.
  3. Restart retirement contributions, at least enough for any employer match.
  4. Bring back the extras slowly, now that you know what you can live without. (Lifestyle creep.)

Talk to the people who depend on you

If you share money with a partner or family, tell them early and agree on the bare-bones budget together. Decide which costs are paused, who will contact which lenders, and how often you'll check in, for example once a week on the same day. Children don't need the details, but an honest, calm explanation helps them understand why some things are on hold.

It's also normal for a job loss to affect your mood and confidence. Short routines, such as a set time each day for the job search and a set time off, can help. If stress is affecting your sleep or health, talk to a doctor or a counsellor; many communities have free or low-cost options.

Severance and final pay checklist

Before you sign anything your employer gives you, check:

  • Severance amount and timing: a lump sum or continued pay, and when it arrives.
  • What you're agreeing to: some agreements include a release of legal claims. You can usually take time to read it and ask questions.
  • Health coverage end date, and whether the employer pays any of the COBRA premium.
  • Unused vacation pay, bonuses or commissions owed.
  • Retirement accounts: your vesting status and options for your 401(k).
  • Other benefits: life insurance, a health savings account (an HSA stays yours), and any flexible spending account deadlines. (HSA vs FSA.)

If you have a side income

If you earn money from freelance or gig work while receiving unemployment, report it to your state as required, usually weekly. Your benefit may be reduced for that week, but not reporting it can lead to having to repay benefits and penalties. Keep a simple log of hours and earnings for each week you claim. Set aside part of that income for tax too, since unemployment benefits and side income are both usually taxable.

Common questions

How long does unemployment last?

It depends on your state. Each state sets how many weeks of benefits you can receive and the amount, which is usually based on your past earnings. Check your state's unemployment programme for its current rules.

Can I get unemployment if I quit or was fired?

Generally, unemployment is for people who lose their job through no fault of their own. Some people who quit for good cause, or were fired for reasons other than misconduct, may still qualify. Each state decides, so apply and let the state review your case.

Should I use my emergency fund or a credit card?

Your emergency fund is for exactly this. Using savings avoids interest charges. Keep a card for true emergencies, but try not to fund everyday spending with credit while you're out of work.

What should I stop paying first if money runs out?

Protect the essentials first: housing, utilities, food, transport for job hunting and insurance. Then contact lenders about unsecured debts, like credit cards, before missing payments, since many offer hardship plans.
