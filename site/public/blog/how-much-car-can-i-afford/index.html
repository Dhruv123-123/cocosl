How much car can I afford? The 20/4/10 rule, worked out on a real salary

How to work out a car budget from your income, what the 20/4/10 rule means, and a worked example comparing a 72-month loan with a smarter plan.

Car dealers usually ask one question: "What monthly payment are you comfortable with?" It's the wrong question, because almost any car can be made to fit almost any monthly payment by stretching the loan. A $450 payment for 72 months is a very different purchase from a $450 payment for 48.

The better question is: what's the most the whole car should cost me, every month, all in? This guide shows how to work that out from your income, explains the popular 20/4/10 rule, and walks through a worked example comparing the "dealer" approach with a plan that saves thousands.

The 20/4/10 rule

The most common rule of thumb for buying a car with a loan:

  • 20% down. Put at least a fifth of the price down in cash or trade-in value.
  • 4 years. Finance for no more than 48 months.
  • 10% of income. Keep total monthly car costs (payment, insurance, fuel) at or below 10% of your gross monthly income.

Each part does a specific job. The down payment means you don't owe more than the car is worth the day you drive it away. The four-year limit keeps interest down and gets you out of the payment while the car still has years of life left. The 10% cap keeps the car from crowding out everything else in your budget.

It's a guideline, not a law. Some people use 15% if they drive a lot or have no other big costs; others use a lower figure if they have student loans or high rent. But it's a solid starting point, and it's much stricter than what a lender will approve.

Why the lender's number is too high

A lender looks at whether you can make the payment, not whether you should. Approval is based on your income, credit and existing debts, and it's common to be approved for far more car than your budget can comfortably carry. Longer loan terms (72 or even 84 months) make bigger loans look affordable by shrinking the payment.

The problem with long loans isn't only the extra interest. Cars lose value fastest in their first few years, and on a long loan with little down, you can owe more than the car is worth for a long time. That's called negative equity, and it becomes a real problem if the car is written off in an accident or you need to sell it: you have to pay off the difference. Many people roll that balance into the next car loan, which makes the next one even more expensive.

Work out your car budget

1. Start from gross monthly income

Divide your annual salary by 12. This is what the 10% part of the rule uses.

2. Multiply by 10% to get your all-in monthly limit

That's the most your payment, insurance and fuel together should cost.

3. Subtract insurance and fuel

Get an actual insurance quote for the type of car you're considering (a quote takes minutes, and rates vary a lot between models). Estimate fuel from the miles you drive and the car's fuel economy. What's left is your maximum monthly payment.

4. Turn that payment into a loan amount

Use a loan calculator with a 48-month term and the interest rate you're likely to get. Your bank or credit union can pre-approve you, which tells you your real rate before you visit a dealer.

5. Add your down payment to get your maximum price

Loan amount plus down payment is the most you should pay for the car, including taxes and fees. Remember to keep money aside for registration and the first year's maintenance.

A worked example: Lee's car budget

Lee (a fictional example) earns $62,000 a year, which is about $5,167 a month before tax. Ten percent of that is about $517 a month for all car costs.

Lee has saved $5,000 and has fallen for a $25,000 car. Here are three ways it could go.

Plan 1: the dealer's offer. No money down, a 72-month loan at 8%.

ItemMonthly
Loan payment ($25,000, 72 months, 8%)$438
Insurance (quote)$150
Fuel$120
Total$708

That's about 13.7% of gross income, well above 10%. Over six years, Lee would pay about $6,560 in interest. And with nothing down, Lee would likely owe more than the car is worth for the first couple of years.

Plan 2: same car, 20/4/10 terms. $5,000 down, $20,000 financed for 48 months at 7% (shorter loans often get a slightly lower rate).

ItemMonthly
Loan payment ($20,000, 48 months, 7%)$479
Insurance$150
Fuel$120
Total$749

Better terms, but a higher monthly cost, about 14.5% of income. This is the useful lesson: the 20/4/10 rule doesn't just change the loan, it tells Lee the car is too expensive for this income.

Plan 3: a cheaper car on 20/4/10 terms. Lee looks at a three-year-old model for $16,000, puts $3,200 (20%) down and finances $12,800 for 48 months at 7%.

ItemMonthly
Loan payment ($12,800, 48 months, 7%)$307
Insurance (lower for this model)$130
Fuel$110
Total$547

About 10.6% of income: close to the line, and Lee is comfortable with that. The total interest is about $1,913, around $4,650 less than the dealer's plan. Lee keeps $1,800 of savings for taxes, registration and a car repair fund. (A car repair fund, worked out.) And in four years, the payment ends while the car still has plenty of life, leaving $307 a month free for other goals.

New, used, or leasing?

New cars come with a full warranty and the latest safety features, but lose value fastest in the first few years. Used cars two to four years old often cost much less while still having a lot of life left; get a pre-purchase inspection from an independent mechanic and check the vehicle history report before buying. The FTC's used-car guidance lists what dealers must disclose, including the Buyers Guide sticker that says whether there's a warranty.

Leasing gives you a lower payment for a newer car, but you're paying for the car's use, not buying it. Mileage limits, wear charges and fees at the end can add up, and you're always making a payment. It can make sense for people who want a new car every few years and drive a predictable number of miles. For most people trying to keep car costs low, buying a reliable used car and keeping it for many years is cheaper.

How to keep the price where you planned

  • Get pre-approved first. A rate from your bank or credit union gives you a benchmark. If the dealer offers a lower rate, take it; if not, use your own.
  • Negotiate the price, not the payment. Ask for the "out-the-door" price including taxes and fees. Decide your maximum before you walk in.
  • Say no to extras you didn't plan for. Extended warranties, paint protection and add-on packages are often added to the loan. Each one is optional, and some can be bought later or elsewhere for less.
  • Check your credit before you shop. A higher score can mean a much lower rate. If there's an error on your report, fix it first. (How to dispute a credit report error.)
  • Don't roll old debt into a new loan. If you owe more on your current car than it's worth, adding that to a new loan makes the new car cost far more than its price.

Costs people forget

Beyond the payment, insurance and fuel, budget for:

  • Maintenance and repairs: oil changes, tires, brakes, and the unexpected. A monthly amount in a sinking fund keeps these from landing on a credit card.
  • Registration, inspection and taxes: annual costs that vary a lot by state.
  • Parking and tolls, if they apply where you live or work.
  • Depreciation: not a bill, but the reason a cheaper car you keep longer usually costs less overall.

Common questions

What is the 20/4/10 rule for buying a car?

Put at least 20% down, finance for no more than four years, and keep total car costs (payment, insurance and fuel) at or below 10% of your gross monthly income. It's a rule of thumb that keeps a car from taking over your budget.

Is a 72-month car loan a bad idea?

It often costs much more in interest and leaves you owing more than the car is worth for longer. A lower payment from a longer loan usually means you're buying more car than fits your budget. If you take one, paying extra each month helps.

How much should I put down on a car?

About 20% is a common target, because it helps keep your loan smaller than the car's value. If you can't, choose a cheaper car or a shorter loan rather than financing the whole price over many years.

Should I include insurance when budgeting for a car?

Yes. Insurance can vary by hundreds of dollars a year between models, so get a quote for the specific car before you buy and include it in your monthly car budget along with fuel and maintenance.
