How to choose a budgeting app: start with what's going wrong

Name your money problem first, match it to the app built for it, then run a two-week trial that shows whether it's working.

Most people pick a budgeting app from a feature list, then stop opening it a month later. A better way is to start from what's going wrong with your money right now, and pick the app built for that problem.

Before you pay for any app

  1. Know your weekly number. Take-home pay, minus bills, minus savings, divided by weeks. That one number does most of the work of any budget. How to work it out.
  2. Use the trial honestly. Link every account and do one 10-minute check-in a week. If you're not opening it by week two, cancel.
  3. Check the data trade-off. Linking accounts means sharing data with another company. Read the privacy policy, and prefer apps that don't make money selling it.
  4. Put the renewal date in your calendar. Annual plans renew automatically.

When an app is the wrong tool

If you have a few accounts and under about 25 transactions a week, a free spreadsheet often works better, because typing the numbers makes you notice them. Our comparison: budgeting app vs spreadsheet vs notebook.

Common questions

How do I know if a budgeting app is working?

If you're opening it at least once a week and you can say your weekly spending number without looking, it's working. If not by week two, switch or cancel.

What is the best free budgeting app?

Rocket Money's free tier is good for spending and subscriptions; Empower's free dashboard is good for net worth. For a full budget at no cost, a spreadsheet is hard to beat.

Are budgeting apps safe?

Reputable apps use read-only connections and encryption, but you're still trusting another company with your data. Use a unique password and two-factor login.

Is it worth paying for a budgeting app?

If it saves you more than it costs, which for many people means avoiding one overdraft or one forgotten subscription, yes. If you won't use it, no.
