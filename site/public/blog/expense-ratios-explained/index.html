The fee that can cost you $18,000: expense ratios explained

Two funds can own the same stocks and leave you with very different money. How expense ratios work, a 30-year example, and how to check any fund's fee in a minute.

November 7, 2026 · 5 min read · Reviewed by a human editor

Two funds can own the exact same stocks and leave you with very different amounts of money. The difference is usually one small number: the expense ratio.

What an expense ratio is

The expense ratio is the yearly fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage of what you have invested. You never get a bill. It's taken out of the fund's assets, so it simply shows up as slightly lower returns.

  • 0.03% means $3 a year for every $10,000 invested.
  • 1% means $100 a year for every $10,000 invested.

That sounds small either way. The problem is that the fee is charged every year, on a growing balance, for decades.

A 30-year example

Invest $10,000 once and leave it for 30 years. As a hypothetical, assume the investments return 7% a year before fees (real markets don't move in a straight line, and returns aren't guaranteed).

Expense ratioApproximate balance after 30 years
0.03%about $75,000
0.50%about $66,000
1.00%about $57,000

Same investments, same market. The 1% fund leaves you roughly $18,000 less than the 0.03% fund, on a single $10,000 deposit. With monthly contributions, the gap is bigger.

Calculation: $10,000 × (1 + 0.07 − fee)^30, a simple approximation. Try your own numbers in the SEC's Investor.gov mutual fund analyzer.

What's a "good" expense ratio?

For a broad stock or bond index fund, very low fees are common: a few hundredths of a percent at many large providers. Target-date index funds are often somewhat higher but still low. Actively managed funds frequently charge much more, and most don't beat a comparable index over long periods after fees.

The fee isn't the only thing that matters, but among funds that hold nearly the same things, it's often the most reliable predictor of which one leaves you with more.

How to check any fund's fee in a minute

  1. Search the fund's name or ticker plus "expense ratio".
  2. Or open the fund's page on your brokerage and look for "expense ratio" or "net expense ratio" on the summary or fact sheet.
  3. Check for other costs too: sales loads (a fee to buy or sell), account maintenance fees, or an adviser fee charged on top of fund fees.

In a workplace 401(k), fund options and fees are listed in the plan's fee disclosure. If your plan only offers expensive funds, still take the employer match, then consider an IRA for extra savings.

Common questions

Is the expense ratio charged on top of my investment?

No. It's deducted from the fund's assets, so it reduces your returns rather than appearing as a separate charge.

Are ETFs cheaper than mutual funds?

Often, but not always. Many index mutual funds are just as cheap as their ETF equivalents. Compare the actual expense ratios.

Do I pay the expense ratio if the fund loses money?

Yes. It's charged regardless of performance.

What's the difference between gross and net expense ratio?

Net is what you actually pay after any fee waivers; gross is before them. Waivers can expire, so it's worth a glance at both.
