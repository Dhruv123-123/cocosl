How to negotiate a raise: the evidence, the number, and the words

How to research your market pay, build a short case, pick the right number and timing, and what to say, with a worked example and a script.

Asking for a raise is uncomfortable for almost everyone. But your salary is the biggest number in your budget, and it compounds: every future raise, bonus and retirement match is calculated from it. A raise you negotiate this year is still paying you in ten years.

This guide covers the four things that make a raise conversation work: evidence of what the job is worth, a record of what you've done, a specific number, and the right moment. Then there's a script and a worked example.

Why it's worth the discomfort

Say you earn $50,000 and negotiate a 6% raise to $53,000. If you'd otherwise get the same percentage raises in later years, the $3,000 difference grows with each one. Over five years, at 3% a year, that's roughly $16,000 more in total, before any 401(k) match on top. And if you change jobs later, your new offer is often based on your current pay.

You don't have to be pushy to get this. You just need to make it easy for your manager to say yes and to justify it to whoever approves it.

Step 1: find out what the job is worth

Your manager will almost certainly have to make a case to someone else. Market data is the most useful thing you can give them.

  • Public pay data. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes pay by occupation and area (its Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics), and its Occupational Outlook Handbook gives typical pay for hundreds of jobs.
  • Job postings. Many states and cities now require pay ranges in job ads. Collect five to ten postings for roles like yours, in your area or remote, at a similar level.
  • People. Ask peers in similar roles, former colleagues, or recruiters what they see. Many people will share a range even if they won't share an exact number.

Write down a range, not one number. Your goal is to know where you sit within it.

Step 2: write down what you've done

Raises are easier to approve when they're tied to results. Write a short list (three to five items) of things you've done since your last pay change, with numbers where possible:

  • Results: "Cut the monthly report time from two days to half a day."
  • Scope: "Took over the vendor accounts when Sam left; that's now 30% of my week."
  • Skills: "Completed the SQL certification and now run all the team's queries."
  • Feedback: "My last review said I exceed expectations in client work."

If you've taken on work above your current title, say so. That's the strongest argument for a bigger raise or a promotion.

Step 3: choose your number

Pick a specific number, near the top of your market range if your results support it. A specific number ($61,000) sounds researched; a round one ($60,000) sounds like a guess. Asking a little above what you'd accept leaves room to meet in the middle.

Also decide in advance:

  • your walk-away point, the number below which you'd start looking elsewhere;
  • alternatives you'd value if the budget is fixed: a review in six months, a title change, extra time off, remote days, a training budget, or a one-off bonus.

Step 4: pick your moment

Timing matters as much as wording:

  • Before budgets are set. Raises are often decided weeks before review season. Ask your manager when budgets are planned and raise it then.
  • After a win. Right after a project goes well, your results are fresh in everyone's mind.
  • When your role changes. Taking on new responsibilities is a natural time to discuss pay.
  • Not in the middle of a crisis, or the week of layoffs.

Ask for a meeting specifically about your pay and role, rather than raising it at the end of an unrelated catch-up.

How to ask for a raise

1. Book the meeting

"I'd like 20 minutes to talk about my role and compensation. Could we find a time next week?"

2. Open with the work

Start with what you've done and what you want to keep doing. It frames the conversation around value, not need.

3. Give your number and your evidence

State your number, then your reasons in a sentence each: results, scope, and market data.

4. Stop talking

After you give the number, pause. Let your manager respond. The silence is uncomfortable, but filling it often means talking yourself down.

5. Respond to the answer

If it's a yes, ask when it takes effect and get it in writing. If it's "not now", ask what you'd need to show, and when you can revisit it. If it's a lower number, negotiate once, using your alternatives.

6. Follow up in writing

Send a short email the same day summarising what was agreed, including any review date.

A script you can adapt

"Thanks for making time. Over the last year I've taken on the vendor accounts, cut our reporting time by more than half, and finished the SQL certification. I'd like to keep growing in the role.

I've looked at pay for similar analyst roles in our area, and the range I'm seeing is about $58,000 to $64,000. Given what I've delivered, I'd like to ask for an increase to $61,000.

What do you think?"

Then stop and listen.

A worked example: Felix asks for a raise

Felix (a fictional example) is a junior data analyst who has been in the role for 18 months on $52,000. Felix's research found public pay data and job postings for similar roles locally between about $58,000 and $64,000.

Felix's preparation:

EvidenceDetail
ResultAutomated the weekly sales report, saving the team about a day a week
ScopeTook over vendor reporting after a colleague left
SkillCompleted a SQL certification
MarketRange of $58,000 to $64,000 for similar roles

The ask: $61,000, about a 17% increase. Felix's walk-away point is $57,000, and the alternatives Felix would accept are a six-month review and a training budget.

What happened: The manager said the budget allowed $57,000 now. Felix thanked them and said, "I appreciate that. Given the vendor work I've taken on, could we get to $58,500, with a review in six months to look at the rest?" The manager agreed to $58,500, a review in six months and a $1,000 training budget, and confirmed it by email.

The result: a $6,500 raise (12.5%). If Felix's future raises are about 3% a year either way, that $6,500 gap grows to roughly $34,500 extra over five years, before any retirement match.

If the answer is no

A "no" today isn't the end of the conversation:

  • Ask what would change it. "What would you need to see from me to get there by the next review?" Write the answer down and agree on a date.
  • Ask for something else. A title change, a training budget, or extra flexibility may be easier to approve than salary.
  • Check the market. If you're well below the range and your employer can't move, a job search may be the faster route to fair pay. Many people get their biggest raises by changing jobs.

When the raise comes through

Decide what happens to the extra money before it reaches your account. Otherwise it tends to vanish into spending. One option is to split it: some to a goal (an emergency fund, debt payoff or a higher 401(k) contribution) and some to enjoy. (The 401(k) match, explained.)

Check your first new paycheck to make sure the raise was applied correctly, and update your budget and your W-4 if needed.

Common questions

How much of a raise should I ask for?

Base it on market data for your role and location and on your results, not on a fixed percentage. If you're paid near the market rate, a few percent may be realistic; if you're well below it, or your responsibilities have grown, a larger increase can be justified.

Is it OK to mention a job offer from another company?

It can work, but only if you'd actually be willing to take the offer. Using it as a bluff can damage trust if your employer calls it. Many people find market data and results are enough on their own.

Can I negotiate a raise in my first year?

Yes, especially if your responsibilities have grown quickly or you've learned you were hired below market. Many employers prefer to wait for the normal review cycle, so ask when that is and prepare for it.

What if my manager says there's no budget?

Ask when the budget will be set and whether you can be considered then. In the meantime, ask about non-salary options such as a title change, extra time off or training, and agree on a review date in writing.
