Is your savings app actually insured? How to check in 5 minutes

Many savings apps aren't banks. FDIC insurance covers bank failure, not the app company failing. How to find the partner bank, check it in BankFind, and pick a safer account.

That savings app paying a nice rate might not be a bank. That isn't automatically bad, but it changes what "FDIC insured" means for you.

What FDIC insurance covers

The FDIC insures deposits at member banks up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, if the bank fails. Credit unions have equivalent coverage through the NCUA.

Two important limits:

  1. It covers deposits (checking, savings, money market deposit accounts, CDs). It doesn't cover investments like stocks, bonds, mutual funds or crypto, even if you bought them through a bank.
  2. It protects you against the failure of an insured bank. It doesn't protect you if a non-bank company in the middle fails.

How fintech apps hold your money

Many popular money apps are technology companies, not banks. They partner with one or more banks that actually hold the deposits, often in pooled accounts. This is sometimes called "pass-through" insurance: if the records are kept properly, your share of the pooled account is insured as if you held it at the bank directly.

The risk is in the middle. If the app (or a company between the app and the bank) fails or its records are wrong, it can take time to work out who owns what, and FDIC insurance doesn't cover that kind of failure. In 2024, the collapse of the fintech middleman Synapse left customers of several apps unable to access their money for months, and some found shortfalls.

How to check your app in 5 minutes

  1. Find the partner bank. Look in the app's disclosures, terms or website footer for wording like "Banking services provided by [Bank name], Member FDIC."
  2. Look it up in FDIC BankFind. Confirm it's an active, FDIC-insured bank. For credit unions, use the NCUA's credit union locator.
  3. Check what product you're in. Is it a deposit account, or are you actually in an investment (like a money market fund) or a "round-up" investing feature? Only deposits are FDIC-insured.
  4. Look for a "sweep" to several banks. Some apps spread money across multiple banks to offer more than $250,000 of coverage. Check which banks, and whether you already have money at any of them (coverage is per bank, combined).
  5. Test a transfer out. Can you move money to your main bank easily and quickly? You want access within a day or two.

A safer default for emergency money

For your emergency fund and sinking funds, prefer:

  • An account held directly at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union, in your name.
  • No monthly fees and no minimum balance traps.
  • Easy, free transfers to your checking account.
  • A competitive rate: high-yield savings accounts often pay well above the FDIC's published national average savings rate.

A high rate is nice. Knowing exactly who holds your money is better.

Red flags

  • The app can't or won't tell you the name of its partner bank.
  • The FDIC logo is used on a product that isn't a deposit (like an investment or crypto account).
  • Withdrawals take an unusually long time or have unexpected fees.
  • Rates far above what insured banks are paying, with vague explanations.

The FDIC treats misrepresenting deposit insurance as a violation, and you can report suspected misuse to the FDIC.

Common questions

Is my money safe in a fintech app?

Often yes, if the partner bank is insured and records are kept properly. But your protection depends on more parties than with a bank account, so check the details and avoid keeping your only emergency money there.

Does FDIC insurance cover fraud or hacked accounts?

No. FDIC insurance is about bank failure. Unauthorised transfers are covered by other rules (like Regulation E for electronic transfers), so report problems to your bank or app quickly.

What about money over $250,000?

Coverage is per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Spreading money across banks or ownership categories (e.g. individual and joint accounts) can increase coverage. The FDIC's EDIE estimator can calculate it.
