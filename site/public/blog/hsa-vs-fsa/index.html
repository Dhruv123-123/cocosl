HSA vs FSA: which one to choose, with the tax savings worked out

How health savings accounts and flexible spending accounts differ, the 2026 limits, and a worked example of what each saves on $2,000 of medical costs.

Open enrollment hands you a form with two acronyms on it and very little explanation. A **health savings account (HSA)** and a **flexible spending account (FSA)** both let you pay for medical costs with money that hasn't been taxed, which can knock 20% to 30% off what you spend on prescriptions, co-pays and dental work. But they work very differently, and choosing the wrong one, or putting too much into an FSA, can cost you money.

This guide explains both, gives the 2026 limits, and runs a worked example of what each one saves.

The short version

  • An HSA is your account. It only comes with a high-deductible health plan, the money rolls over forever, it moves with you when you change jobs, and it can be invested.
  • An FSA is an employer benefit. It works with most health plans, the full year's amount is available on day one, but unspent money is generally lost at the end of the plan year (with some limited exceptions).

If you have a choice, the decision usually comes down to which health plan fits you better. The account follows from the plan.

How an HSA works

An HSA is a savings account for medical costs that you can only contribute to if you're covered by a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) and have no other disqualifying coverage. For 2026, a plan counts as an HDHP if its deductible is at least $1,700 for self-only coverage or $3,400 for family coverage (and it meets the out-of-pocket limits).

The 2026 HSA contribution limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. If you're 55 or older, you can add an extra $1,000. Contributions from your employer count toward the limit.

What makes the HSA unusual is its tax treatment, often called a "triple tax advantage":

  1. Money goes in before tax. Contributions through payroll avoid federal income tax and, usually, Social Security and Medicare tax. Contributions you make yourself are deductible on your return.
  2. It grows tax-free. Interest and investment growth inside the account aren't taxed.
  3. It comes out tax-free when you spend it on qualified medical expenses.

The money is yours. It rolls over every year with no limit, stays with you if you leave your job, and once your balance is above a minimum many HSA providers let you invest it. After 65, you can withdraw for non-medical costs too, paying ordinary income tax but no penalty, much like a traditional IRA. Before 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed and usually carry a 20% penalty.

How an FSA works

A health FSA is an account your employer sets up. You choose an amount at open enrollment, and it's taken from your paychecks in equal pieces through the year, before tax. For 2026, the most you can put in through salary reduction is $3,400.

Two features define it:

  • The full year is available on day one. If you elect $1,200 and need a $900 dental crown in January, you can pay for it from the FSA before you've contributed most of the money.
  • Use it or lose it. Money left at the end of the plan year is generally forfeited. Employers can soften this with one of two options, or neither: a carryover of up to $680 into the next year (for 2026 plan years), or a grace period of up to two and a half months to spend the remaining balance. Check which one your plan has.

FSAs usually work with any of your employer's health plans, including traditional PPOs. If you leave your job mid-year, unspent FSA money generally stays with the employer, unless you continue coverage through COBRA.

There's also a limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision costs, which some employers offer alongside an HSA, and a dependent care FSA, which is a different account for childcare costs and isn't covered here.

A worked example: Dana chooses a plan

Dana (a fictional example) earns $55,000 a year, is single, and expects about $2,000 a year in medical costs: regular prescriptions, a few doctor visits and a dental filling. Dana's employer offers two plans:

  • A PPO with a $1,000 deductible, plus a health FSA.
  • An HDHP with a $2,000 deductible, plus an HSA. The HDHP premium is $60 a month less than the PPO, which is $720 a year.

Dana's insurer's cost estimator shows what Dana would actually pay for those $2,000 of expected costs under each plan. Under the PPO, co-pays and part of the $1,000 deductible add up to about $1,300 out of pocket. Under the HDHP, everything is below the $2,000 deductible, so Dana pays the full $2,000.

Next, the tax saving. Dana is in the 12% federal bracket, pays 7.65% Social Security and Medicare tax, and (for this example) 5% state income tax. That's about 24.65% saved on every dollar run through either account.

The HDHP comes out about $190 a year ahead in an expected year, before counting any money the employer adds to the HSA (many do, which widens the gap). It also has two advantages the table doesn't show: any HSA money Dana doesn't spend stays in the account, and the FSA's $1,300 election is at risk if Dana's costs come in lower.

In a bad year, the picture changes. If Dana has a hospital visit, the HDHP's higher deductible and out-of-pocket maximum mean Dana could pay several hundred dollars more than under the PPO, even after the premium savings. Dana checks both plans' out-of-pocket maximums, decides the HDHP makes sense, and makes building a $2,000 HSA balance (enough to cover the deductible) the first savings goal of the year.

Your numbers will be different. The comparison that matters is the one Dana ran: premiums, plus your expected out-of-pocket costs, minus the tax saving and any employer contribution, then a check on the bad year, and whether you could cover that bad year from savings.

How much to put in an FSA

Because unused money is lost, the right FSA election is what you're confident you'll spend, not what you might spend. A good way to estimate:

  1. Add up predictable costs: regular prescriptions, glasses or contacts, planned dental work, therapy co-pays.
  2. Check last year's statements or your insurer's claims history for a realistic figure.
  3. Elect that amount, or slightly less. If your plan has a carryover, you can elect up to that amount above your estimate with little risk.

Losing $100 of a $1,000 FSA still leaves you ahead if the tax saving was about $250. But electing $3,000 "just in case" and spending $1,200 is a real loss.

How much to put in an HSA

With an HSA there's no risk of losing the money, so the question is cash flow. A sensible order:

  1. At least enough to get any employer match or contribution. Some employers add money only if you contribute.
  2. Enough to cover your deductible, built up over time. This is what makes a high-deductible plan safe.
  3. Up to the limit, if you can afford it, especially if you can pay smaller medical costs from your regular budget and leave the HSA to grow. Some people treat it as an extra retirement account.

If you pay a medical cost out of pocket while you have an HSA, keep the receipt. Current rules let you reimburse yourself later for qualified expenses incurred after the HSA was set up, with no deadline, as long as you have records.

What counts as a qualified medical expense?

Both accounts cover the same broad category: costs to diagnose, treat or prevent illness. Common examples include doctor and dentist visits, prescriptions, glasses and contact lenses, many over-the-counter medicines, menstrual care products, physical therapy and mental health care. Gym memberships, cosmetic procedures and general wellness items usually don't qualify. IRS Publication 502 has the full list, and your plan administrator can confirm specific items.

Many accounts come with a debit card. Keep receipts anyway: the administrator may ask for proof that a purchase qualified.

Common mistakes

  • Electing the maximum FSA by default. Elect what you'll use, not the limit.
  • Choosing an HDHP with no savings for the deductible. The plan only works if you can pay the deductible without a credit card.
  • Contributing to an HSA without an eligible plan. If you're on a spouse's PPO or enrolled in Medicare, you generally can't contribute, even if you have an HSA from before.
  • Forgetting the FSA deadline. Put the plan-year end date and any grace period in your calendar, with a reminder a month before.
  • Losing the receipts. Both accounts can ask for them, and HSA reimbursements years later depend on them.

Common questions

Can you have both an HSA and an FSA?

Not a general-purpose health FSA, because it counts as other coverage that makes you ineligible for an HSA. But you can have an HSA alongside a limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision costs, if your employer offers one, and alongside a dependent care FSA.

What happens to my HSA if I change jobs?

It stays yours. You keep the balance and can keep spending it on medical costs. You can only keep contributing if you're still covered by an HSA-eligible plan, and you can move the account to another HSA provider if you prefer.

Is an HSA worth it if I'm healthy?

Often, yes. Lower premiums plus tax-free savings that roll over make a high-deductible plan good value for many healthy people, as long as you can cover the deductible from savings if something does happen.

Can I change my FSA amount during the year?

Usually only after a qualifying life event, such as marriage, a new child or a change in employment. Otherwise, the amount you choose at open enrollment is fixed for the plan year, which is why estimating carefully matters.
