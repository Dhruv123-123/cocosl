Health insurance explained: premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance and out-of-pocket max

What premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance and the out-of-pocket maximum mean, how they add up, and a worked example comparing two plans.

Health insurance has its own vocabulary, and it decides how much you pay when you get sick. Premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance and out-of-pocket maximum are five words that together determine your real cost, and most people choose a plan by looking at only one of them: the monthly price.

This guide explains each term in plain English, shows how they work together in a real year, and walks through a worked example of choosing between two plans.

The five numbers that decide your cost

Premium

What you pay to have the insurance, usually monthly, whether or not you use it. With workplace coverage, your share comes out of your paycheck, often before tax. (How to read a pay stub.)

Deductible

How much you pay for covered care each year before the plan starts sharing the cost. A $1,500 deductible means you pay the first $1,500 of covered bills (with some exceptions below), then the plan helps.

Copay

A fixed amount for a specific service, like $25 for a doctor visit or $10 for a generic prescription. On some plans copays apply before the deductible; on others, after.

Coinsurance

Your percentage share of the cost after you've met the deductible. With 20% coinsurance, you pay 20% of a covered bill and the plan pays 80%.

Out-of-pocket maximum

The most you'll pay for covered, in-network care in a year. Once your deductible, copays and coinsurance reach this amount, the plan pays 100% of covered services for the rest of the plan year. Premiums don't count toward it.

For Marketplace and most other plans, the federal limit for 2027 is $12,000 for an individual and $24,000 for a family. Many plans set their maximum well below that.

How they work together

Picture your year in three stages:

  1. Before the deductible: you pay the full negotiated price for most care (plus copays where they apply).
  2. After the deductible: you pay coinsurance or copays, and the plan pays the rest.
  3. After the out-of-pocket maximum: the plan pays 100% of covered in-network care.

Your total yearly cost is your premiums plus whatever you pay for care, up to the out-of-pocket maximum. That total, not the premium alone, is what to compare between plans.

What's free

Most plans cover certain preventive services, such as many screenings, vaccines and an annual check-up, at no cost when you use an in-network provider, even before you've met your deductible. Check your plan's list.

In-network vs out-of-network

Plans negotiate prices with a network of doctors, hospitals and pharmacies. Staying in network usually costs much less, and many plans (especially HMOs and EPOs) don't cover out-of-network care at all except in emergencies. Out-of-network costs may not count toward your out-of-pocket maximum. Always check that a doctor is in network before an appointment.

Low-deductible vs high-deductible plans

  • Low-deductible plans have higher premiums but lower costs when you get care. They suit people who expect regular care, take ongoing prescriptions, or want predictable costs.
  • High-deductible health plans (HDHPs) have lower premiums and higher deductibles. If a plan qualifies, you can contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA), which has tax advantages and can be used for medical costs now or later. (HSA vs FSA.)

Neither is better for everyone. It depends on how much care you expect and whether you could afford the deductible if something happened.

Total yearly cost in three scenarios (in-network care, simplified):

ScenarioPlan APlan B (after the $500 HSA money)
Light year: three doctor visits at $150 each$1,800 + $75 = $1,875$600 + $450 − $500 = $550
Moderate year: $5,000 of covered care$1,800 + $750 + $850 = $3,400$600 + $3,000 + $400 − $500 = $3,500
Big year: $20,000 surgery$1,800 + $4,000 = $5,800$600 + $6,000 − $500 = $6,100

In a light year, Plan B saves Gia $1,325. In a moderate or big year, Plan A costs about $100 to $300 less. Gia is healthy, has no regular prescriptions and has an emergency fund that could cover the $3,000 deductible, so Gia chooses Plan B and puts the premium savings into the HSA.

If Gia had a chronic condition, expected surgery, or no savings to cover a $3,000 bill, Plan A would be the safer choice.

How to choose a plan

  1. Estimate your care: look at last year's doctor visits, prescriptions and any planned procedures.
  2. Check your doctors and prescriptions are covered and in network.
  3. Add up the yearly cost for a light, moderate and bad year, as in Gia's table.
  4. Ask yourself the bad-year question: could you pay the out-of-pocket maximum if you had to? If not, a plan with a lower maximum may be worth the higher premium.
  5. Count any employer HSA contribution as money that lowers the high-deductible plan's cost.

Reading a medical bill

Before paying a big bill, compare it with your insurer's Explanation of Benefits (EOB), which shows what was billed, what the plan paid and what you owe. Errors are common, and you can ask for an itemised bill and question charges. Many hospitals also have financial assistance programmes.

Keeping costs down with the plan you have

  • Use in-network providers for planned care, and check before every new doctor or test.
  • Use preventive care, which is often free.
  • Ask for generics and compare pharmacies: prices for the same drug can differ.
  • Use urgent care or telehealth for non-emergencies, which is usually far cheaper than the emergency room. In a real emergency, go to the ER.
  • Use your HSA or FSA to pay for care with pre-tax money.
  • Save for your deductible in a sinking fund, so a medical bill doesn't go on a credit card. (Sinking funds, explained.)

If you're under 26

You can usually join or stay on a parent's job-based health plan until you turn 26, even if you're married, aren't claimed as a dependent, or turn down coverage from your own job. Marketplace plans have narrower rules. It's often cheaper than buying your own, but compare: your employer's plan may be a better fit, especially if your doctors are in a different area from your parent's network.

Prescription drugs

Plans group drugs into tiers, usually from cheap generics to expensive specialty drugs, each with its own copay or coinsurance. Before choosing a plan, search its drug list (the "formulary") for anything you take regularly, and compare what you'd pay on each plan over a year.

When you can change plans

You usually can't switch health plans whenever you like. With workplace coverage, you choose during open enrollment, typically once a year, or within a set window after a qualifying life event such as marriage, a new baby, losing other coverage or moving. Marketplace plans have their own yearly open enrollment period and similar special enrollment periods. So when open enrollment comes around, take 30 minutes to rerun the comparison above with your expected care for the coming year, rather than letting last year's choice roll over.

A note on surprise bills

Federal rules now protect most people from many "surprise" out-of-network bills, for example for emergency care or when an out-of-network doctor treats you at an in-network hospital. If you get a bill that looks like one of these, ask your insurer and the provider to check it before you pay.

Common questions

Do premiums count toward the deductible?

No. Premiums are what you pay to have coverage. The deductible is what you pay for care before the plan starts sharing costs. Premiums also don't count toward the out-of-pocket maximum.

What's the difference between a copay and coinsurance?

A copay is a fixed dollar amount for a service, like $25 for a visit. Coinsurance is a percentage of the cost, like 20%, usually after you've met your deductible.

What happens after I reach my out-of-pocket maximum?

For the rest of the plan year, your plan pays 100% of covered in-network services. You still pay your premiums. Out-of-network care and services the plan doesn't cover may not count.

Is a high-deductible health plan worth it?

It can be if you're generally healthy, can afford the deductible from savings, and use the HSA. If you expect a lot of care or couldn't pay the deductible, a lower-deductible plan may cost less overall.
