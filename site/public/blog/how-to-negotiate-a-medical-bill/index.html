How to negotiate a medical bill: check it, challenge it, and pay less

How to check a medical bill for errors, use your No Surprises Act rights, ask for financial assistance and set up a payment plan, with a worked example.

A medical bill can arrive weeks after a visit, for an amount that's hard to understand and harder to pay. The good news: the first number you see is often not the final one. Bills contain errors, insurers process claims wrongly, federal rules protect you from some surprise charges, and many hospitals offer discounts or payment plans to people who ask.

This guide walks through how to check a bill, the rights you have, how to ask for less, and a worked example of a $2,860 emergency room bill that ended up much smaller.

Don't pay the first bill straight away

Unless a bill is small and clearly right, it's worth a short pause before paying. Paying first and disputing later is harder, and a payment can look like you've accepted the charges. Instead:

  • Note the due date, and call the billing office if you need more time to review it. Ask that the account not be sent to collections while you do.
  • Wait for your insurer's explanation of benefits (EOB) if you have insurance. It shows what the provider charged, what the insurer paid and what you owe. The bill and the EOB should match.
  • Keep everything: the bill, the EOB, any estimates, and notes from every call with the date and the name of the person you spoke to.

How to lower a medical bill

1. Ask for an itemized bill

A summary bill shows a total. An itemized bill lists every service, test, supply and medicine with a code and a price. The CFPB suggests asking your provider for "an itemized list of services and providers" if you don't recognise a bill. Itemized bills make errors much easier to spot.

2. Check it for errors

Look for duplicate charges, services you didn't receive, the wrong number of days in a room, cancelled tests that were still billed, and your name, date of birth and insurance details. A wrong insurance ID alone can cause a claim to be denied.

3. Compare it with your insurance

If you think your insurer should have covered something, contact them for an explanation of benefits and an itemized list of payments. Ask whether the claim was processed as in-network, whether a prior authorization was missing, and how to appeal a denial.

4. Use your No Surprises Act rights

Since January 1, 2022, the No Surprises Act protects most people with health insurance from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency care, for certain out-of-network care at an in-network hospital or surgery centre, and for air ambulance services. If you got one of these bills, ask the provider and insurer to reprocess it, and file a complaint with CMS if they don't.

5. Ask about financial assistance

Many hospitals have financial assistance or "charity care" programmes that reduce or cancel bills for patients under certain income levels. The CFPB suggests checking whether payment plans, charity care or other assistance are available through your provider, your state or patient advocacy groups. Ask for the application, even if you think you earn too much: limits are often higher than people expect.

6. Ask for a lower price

If the bill is correct and you don't qualify for assistance, ask anyway. Useful questions: "Is there a discount for paying in full?", "What would an uninsured patient pay?" and "Can you match what my insurer would have paid?" Be polite, specific and persistent, and get any agreement in writing.

7. Set up a payment plan you can afford

If you still owe money, ask for an interest-free payment plan with a monthly amount that fits your budget. Many providers offer them directly. Be careful with medical credit cards offered at the front desk: some charge high deferred interest if the balance isn't paid in full by the end of a promotional period.

If you don't have insurance

If you're uninsured or choose not to use insurance, providers generally must give you a good faith estimate of the cost of your care. If you schedule care at least three business days in advance, you should get one, and you can ask for an estimate at any time. If the final bill is at least $400 more than the estimate, you may be able to dispute it through the federal dispute process. Keep the estimate: you'll need it.

A worked example: Quinn's emergency room bill

Quinn (a fictional example) has insurance and goes to an in-network hospital's emergency room after a fall. Six weeks later a bill arrives for $2,860. Quinn doesn't pay it yet, and works through the steps:

StepWhat Quinn finds or doesAmount owed
Original bill$2,860
Itemized billThe same X-ray billed twice ($340)$2,520
No Surprises ActAn out-of-network anesthesiologist billed $610 at an in-network hospital; reprocessed at in-network cost sharing, Quinn's share $150$2,060
Financial assistanceQuinn qualifies for a 50% discount based on income$1,030

The final amount is about 64% lower than the first bill, and Quinn pays it over a year without interest. It took four phone calls, one form and about two hours spread over three weeks.

What to say on the phone

Billing calls go better with a short script:

"Hi, I'm calling about account number [number]. Before I pay, I'd like an itemized bill, please. Can you also put a hold on the account so it isn't sent to collections while I review it? And can you send me your financial assistance application?"

Then, once you've reviewed it:

"I've found a duplicate charge on [date] for [service]. Can you remove it and send me a corrected bill? If I pay the remaining balance in full, is there a discount? If not, can we set up an interest-free payment plan of [amount] a month?"

Write down the name of everyone you speak to, the date and what they agreed, and ask them to confirm changes in writing.

If a bill goes to collections

If a medical bill has already been sent to a debt collector, you still have options. You can ask the collector to validate the debt, dispute it if it's wrong, and ask the original provider whether financial assistance can still apply. If you're having problems with a debt collector or a credit reporting company, you can submit a complaint to the CFPB. (How to deal with debt collectors.)

Check your credit reports too. If a medical debt is listed incorrectly, or after you've paid it, dispute it with the credit bureau. (How to dispute a credit report error.)

Medical identity theft

If a bill or EOB shows treatment you never had, it may be a mistake, or it may be medical identity theft: someone using your information to get care. Call the provider and your insurer, ask for the records, and report it at IdentityTheft.gov, which gives you a free recovery plan.

Plan for the next one

Medical costs are hard to predict, but they're not entirely random. A few habits make the next bill easier:

  • Know your plan's numbers: deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, and which hospitals and doctors are in network. (Health insurance, explained.)
  • Use an HSA or FSA if you have one, to pay with pre-tax money. (HSA vs FSA.)
  • Keep a small medical sinking fund, even $25 a month, so a copay or a prescription doesn't land on a credit card. (Sinking funds, explained.)
  • Ask for estimates before planned care, and ask whether every provider involved is in network.

Common questions

Should I put a medical bill on a credit card?

Usually not straight away. Card interest can make the bill much more expensive, and once it's on a card you lose the chance to use the provider's interest-free payment plan or financial assistance. Check the bill, ask about assistance and plans first.

How do I know if a hospital offers financial assistance?

Ask the billing office or look on the hospital's website for "financial assistance" or "charity care". Ask for the application and the income limits. Many hospitals will apply a discount even after a bill has been sent, and sometimes after it has gone to collections.

What is an explanation of benefits?

An explanation of benefits (EOB) is a statement from your insurer showing what a provider charged, what the insurer paid, and what you may owe. It isn't a bill, but you should compare it with the bill you receive from the provider.

What if my insurer denied the claim?

Ask the insurer why, in writing, and how to appeal. Common reasons include missing paperwork, a coding error or a missing prior authorization, and many can be fixed. Ask your provider's office to help, since they can often resubmit the claim with the right information.
