Debt consolidation: when it saves you money and when it doesn't (with the maths)

A loan, a balance transfer or a payoff plan? We run all three on $12,000 of card debt to show when consolidation saves money and when it costs more.

"Consolidate your debt into one low payment!" is one of the most common ads in personal finance, and one of the most misunderstood. Consolidation can genuinely save you thousands of dollars. It can also cost you more than doing nothing, with a lower monthly payment that hides a longer, more expensive loan.

The difference comes down to three numbers: the rate, the fees and the term. This guide explains the options, then runs all of them on the same $12,000 of credit card debt so you can see the real cost of each.

What debt consolidation is

Consolidation means replacing several debts with one new one, ideally at a lower interest rate. The main ways to do it:

  • A personal loan (often called a debt consolidation loan): a fixed rate, a fixed monthly payment and a fixed end date. You use it to pay off the cards.
  • A balance transfer card: a new credit card with a 0% or low promotional rate for a set period, usually with a transfer fee of 3% to 5%.
  • A home equity loan or line of credit: a lower rate, but your home secures the debt.
  • A debt management plan through a nonprofit credit counseling agency: not a new loan, but an arrangement where the agency negotiates lower rates and you make one payment to them.

What consolidation isn't: debt settlement. Some companies advertise "consolidation" but are really settlement firms that tell you to stop paying your creditors while they negotiate. The CFPB warns that these services can be risky and often charge fees up front. If a company asks for money before it has done anything, walk away.

A worked example: Riley's $12,000

Riley (a fictional example) has $12,000 spread across three cards:

CardBalanceAPR
Card A$5,20027.99%
Card B$4,30024.99%
Card C$2,50022.49%

Riley can put $450 a month toward the debt. Here are three ways to use it.

Option 1: Keep the cards, pay $450 a month with the avalanche method

Pay the minimums on everything, and put every extra dollar on the highest-rate card first. (Avalanche vs snowball.)

  • Time to debt-free: 40 months
  • Total interest: about $5,580

That's the baseline. Any consolidation has to beat it.

Option 2: A 3-year consolidation loan at 13%

Riley qualifies for a 36-month personal loan at 13% APR with a 5% origination fee. Lenders often take the fee out of the loan itself, so to end up with $12,000 to pay off the cards, Riley borrows about $12,630.

  • Monthly payment: about $426
  • Time to debt-free: 36 months
  • Total cost (interest plus fee): about $3,320

Compared with option 1, that's about $2,260 cheaper, four months faster, and $24 a month less than Riley was already paying. This is consolidation working as advertised.

Option 2b: the same loan over 5 years

The lender also offers a 60-month term at the same rate, with a payment of only about $287. It's tempting: $163 a month freed up.

  • Time to debt-free: 60 months
  • Total cost: about $5,240

That's barely cheaper than doing nothing at all, and Riley is in debt for an extra 20 months. The lower payment is the trap the CFPB warns about: a longer term can make the total cost higher even when the rate is lower. If you take a longer term for safety, pay it as if it were the shorter one.

Option 3: A balance transfer at 0% for 15 months

Riley is approved for a balance transfer card at 0% for 15 months with a 3% fee. The $12,000 becomes $12,360 on the new card (the $360 is the fee). What happens next depends entirely on the payment.

  • Paying $824 a month clears it in 15 months. Total cost: $360, just the fee. The cheapest option by far.
  • Paying the same $450 a month leaves $5,610 when the promotion ends, which then starts collecting interest at 26.99%. It takes about 15 more months to clear and costs about $1,050 in interest. Total cost: about $1,410 over 30 months. Still cheaper than options 1 and 2.

Two catches. First, approval and the credit limit aren't certain: a card with a $6,000 limit can only take half the debt. Second, the promotional rate usually applies only to the transferred balance; new purchases on that card may charge interest right away, and a payment more than 60 days late can end the promotional rate.

The comparison

OptionMonthlyMonthsTotal interest and fees
Avalanche on the cards$45040about $5,580
Loan, 3 years at 13%$42636about $3,320
Loan, 5 years at 13%$28760about $5,240

The pattern: consolidation saves money when the new rate is meaningfully lower, the fees are small, and the term is no longer than your current payoff plan. Break any of those three and the savings shrink or disappear.

Five questions before you consolidate

  1. What's the APR, including fees? Compare the loan's APR (which includes the origination fee) with your cards' rates. A 13% loan against 25% cards is a big gap; a 20% loan against 22% cards isn't worth the fee.
  2. What's the total cost, not just the payment? Multiply the payment by the number of months and subtract what you owe today. That's the real price.
  3. Is the term shorter than your current plan? If not, you're buying a smaller payment with more interest.
  4. Will you stop using the cards? Consolidation moves debt; it doesn't remove the reason it built up. Paying the cards off with a loan and then running them up again is how $12,000 becomes $24,000. Many people keep the cards open (for their credit history) but take them out of their wallet. (Why not to cancel old cards.)
  5. Is anything being put at risk? A home equity loan can carry a lower rate, but missing payments can put your home at risk. Card debt is unsecured; swapping it for a secured debt changes what you can lose.

What it does to your credit score

Applying for a loan or card means a hard inquiry, which usually lowers a score a little for a short while. Paying the cards down usually helps more, because it lowers your utilization (the share of your card limits you're using), one of the biggest scoring factors. Many people see their score rise within a few months of a consolidation, as long as the old cards stay at zero and every new payment is on time.

If you can't qualify for a good rate

If your credit score is low or your income is tight, the offers you get may not beat your cards. Two other routes:

  • Call your card companies and ask for a lower rate or a hardship program. It's free and takes 10 minutes per card.
  • A nonprofit credit counselor can review your budget and, if it fits, set up a debt management plan with lower rates. Look for an agency affiliated with a national nonprofit association, and be wary of anyone charging large fees up front.

And the plain payoff plan is always available. Our debt payoff calculator shows your own avalanche and snowball timelines in a couple of minutes, which gives you the baseline any offer has to beat.

Common questions

When does consolidating credit card debt save money?

It's a good idea when the new rate is clearly lower than your current rates, the fees are small, and the payoff term is no longer than your current plan, and when you stop adding new debt. If any of those don't hold, it can cost more than paying off the debts directly.

Does debt consolidation hurt your credit?

Applying usually causes a small, short-lived dip from the hard inquiry. Paying card balances down lowers your utilization, which often helps more. Missed payments on the new loan would hurt, as with any debt.

Is a balance transfer better than a consolidation loan?

If you can pay the balance off within the 0% period, a balance transfer is usually cheaper because you only pay the transfer fee. If you can't, a fixed-rate loan with a fixed end date can be the safer choice. Compare the total cost of each.

Should I close my credit cards after consolidating?

Usually not right away. Closing cards can shorten your credit history and raise your utilization. Keep them at zero and out of your wallet instead, unless a card has an annual fee you don't want to pay.

Is a debt consolidation company the same as a debt settlement company?

No. Consolidation replaces debts with a new loan or plan. Settlement tries to negotiate down what you owe, often after you stop paying, which can damage your credit and lead to fees. Be wary of any company that charges before it has done anything.
