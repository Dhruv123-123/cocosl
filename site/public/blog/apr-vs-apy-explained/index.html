APR vs APY: what each one means and how to compare rates

APR is the yearly cost of borrowing; APY is what savings earn with compounding. How each works, why they differ, and a worked example of both.

Rates are everywhere in personal finance: on savings accounts, credit cards, car loans and mortgages. Two abbreviations come up again and again, **APR** and **APY**, and they mean different things. Mixing them up can make a loan look cheaper or a savings account look better than it really is.

This guide explains what each one measures, why they differ, how to use them to compare offers, and a worked example of both.

The short version

  • APR (annual percentage rate) is mostly used for borrowing. It shows the yearly cost of a loan or card, and for many loans it includes certain fees as well as interest.
  • APY (annual percentage yield) is used for saving. It shows what you'll earn in a year, including the effect of compounding.

A simple way to remember it: you want a low APR on money you owe and a high APY on money you save.

What APR means

The CFPB explains that the interest rate is "the cost you will pay each year to borrow the money, expressed as a percentage rate," while the APR is "a broader measure of the cost of borrowing money than the interest rate." For a mortgage or many other loans, the APR includes the interest rate plus other charges, such as points and some fees, which is why the APR is usually higher than the interest rate.

APR on credit cards

For credit cards, the APR is the interest rate you're charged on balances you don't pay off. Many cards have several APRs: one for purchases, one for balance transfers, one for cash advances and a higher penalty APR if you pay late. The CFPB notes that credit card APRs generally fall between roughly 12% and 30%. (How credit card interest works.)

APR on loans

For personal loans, car loans and mortgages, the APR helps you compare offers that have different fees. A loan with a lower interest rate but a large origination fee can have a higher APR than a loan with a slightly higher rate and no fee.

APR on payday loans

APR also shows how expensive short-term loans can be. The CFPB says a typical two-week payday loan with a $15 fee per $100 borrowed equals an APR of almost 400%.

What APY means

Under Regulation DD (the Truth in Savings rules), APY is "a percentage rate reflecting the total amount of interest paid on an account, based on the interest rate and the frequency of compounding for a 365-day period." In plain terms: APY tells you how much a deposit earns in a year once interest earns interest.

Banks and credit unions advertise APY on savings accounts, money market accounts and CDs, so you can compare accounts that compound differently. (How to open a high-yield savings account.)

Why APR and APY differ: compounding

Compounding is interest calculated on the original amount plus any interest already added. The more often interest compounds, the more you end up with (on savings) or owe (on debt), even at the same stated rate.

  • An interest rate of 4% compounded once a year gives an APY of 4%.
  • The same 4% compounded daily gives an APY of about 4.08%.

For savings, APY includes that compounding. For credit cards, the APR you see doesn't include compounding, so if you carry a balance, the effective yearly cost can be a little higher than the APR.

A worked example: Rae compares rates

Rae (a fictional example) is looking at both sides of the same idea: where to keep savings, and what borrowing really costs.

Saving

Rae has $5,000 to put in savings.

Account A4.00% interest, compounded daily (APY about 4.08%)about $204
Account B4.05% APYabout $203
If interest were simple (no compounding)4.00%$200

At first glance "4.05%" looks better than "4.00%", but one is an interest rate and one is an APY. Compared like for like, APY to APY, Account A earns slightly more. The lesson: compare APY with APY.

Borrowing

Rae also has a $3,000 credit card balance at 24% APR and pays $150 a month. At that pace it takes about 26 months to clear and costs roughly $870 in interest.

A lender offers Rae a two-year personal loan at 13% interest with a 5% origination fee ($150 taken from the loan). The fee pushes the loan's APR to roughly 17% to 18%. That's still much lower than the card's 24%, but the APR, not the 13% headline rate, is the fair comparison. (Debt consolidation, explained.)

How to compare offers

For savings accounts and CDs

  • Compare APY, not the interest rate.
  • Check whether the APY is a promotional rate that drops after a few months.
  • Look for minimum balances or monthly fees that would cut what you earn.
  • For CDs, check the early withdrawal penalty.
  • Make sure the account is FDIC or NCUA insured. (Is my savings app FDIC insured?.)

For loans

  • Compare APR, which includes many fees, not just the interest rate.
  • Compare the total cost over the life of the loan, not only the monthly payment. A longer loan has a lower payment but often costs more overall.
  • Check whether the rate is fixed or variable. For adjustable-rate mortgages, the CFPB notes the APR doesn't reflect the maximum rate the loan could reach.

For credit cards

  • Look at the purchase APR if you might ever carry a balance, and the penalty APR if you've paid late before.
  • An introductory 0% APR is useful only if you'll pay off the balance before it ends.
  • If you always pay in full, the APR matters less than fees and rewards.

Variable vs fixed rates

A fixed rate stays the same for the life of the loan or the term of the CD. A variable rate can change, usually when a benchmark rate changes. Most credit card APRs and many savings APYs are variable, so the rate you see today may not be the rate you have in six months. Check your statements: card issuers must tell you the APR that applies, and banks show the APY you earned.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing an APY to an interest rate. They're not the same measure.
  • Judging a loan by its monthly payment. A lower payment over a longer term can cost more.
  • Chasing a teaser rate. An introductory rate that drops after three months may earn less over a year than a steady, slightly lower one.
  • Ignoring fees. A savings account that charges a monthly fee can wipe out the interest on a small balance.
  • Forgetting taxes. Interest on savings is generally taxable income, even though APY doesn't show that.

Where to find the APR and APY

You don't have to work these out yourself. Lenders and banks are required to disclose them:

  • Credit cards: the card agreement and every monthly statement show the APRs that apply to purchases, balance transfers and cash advances, and how much interest you paid this month and so far this year.
  • Loans: the loan disclosure (for mortgages, the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure) shows the APR, the finance charge and the total of payments.
  • Savings accounts and CDs: the account disclosure and your statements show the APY, and statements show the APY you actually earned for the period.

If an offer only shows a monthly payment or an "interest rate," ask for the APR (for borrowing) or the APY (for saving) before you decide. A lender or bank that won't tell you clearly is a reason to look elsewhere.

Common questions

Is a higher APR good or bad?

On borrowing, a higher APR is worse: it means the loan or card costs more each year. On savings, you look at APY instead, and a higher APY is better.

What's the difference between interest rate and APY on a savings account?

The interest rate is the base rate. APY includes how often interest is added to your account, so it's equal to or a little higher than the interest rate. Use APY to compare accounts.

Why is my credit card APR so high?

Card APRs depend on the card, the issuer and your credit history, and they're usually much higher than rates on secured loans like mortgages or car loans. Paying your statement balance in full each month means you don't pay interest on purchases at all.

Does APR include all fees?

Not always. For many loans, APR includes interest plus certain finance charges and fees, but not every cost. Read the loan's disclosure to see what's included, and compare the total amount you'll repay.
