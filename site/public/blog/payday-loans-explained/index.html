Payday loans explained: what they cost and what to do instead

How payday loans work, why a $15 fee per $100 is an APR near 400%, how rollovers trap borrowers, and cheaper options when cash is short.

When a bill is due before payday and there's no money in the account, a payday loan can look like a quick fix. It's fast, often doesn't need a credit check, and the fee sounds small. But payday loans are one of the most expensive ways to borrow, and many people end up paying fees for months on a loan they meant to repay in two weeks.

This guide explains how payday loans work, what they really cost, how rollovers trap borrowers, and what to try first when cash is short.

How a payday loan works

A payday loan is a short-term, high-cost loan, usually for a few hundred dollars. The CFPB describes it as a loan generally due on your next payday, usually two to four weeks after you take it out.

The typical process:

  1. You show proof of income and a bank account, and often give the lender a post-dated check or permission to withdraw from your account.
  2. You get the cash and agree to repay the amount borrowed plus a fee on the due date.
  3. On the due date, the lender cashes the check or withdraws the money. If there isn't enough in your account, you may face both the lender's fees and your bank's overdraft or returned-payment fees. (How to avoid overdraft fees.)

Rules vary a lot by state: some states cap fees, some limit how many loans you can take, and some don't allow payday lending at all.

What it really costs

Payday loan fees are usually quoted per $100 borrowed. The CFPB says a typical fee is $15 per $100, and that a two-week payday loan with that fee works out to an APR of almost 400%. For comparison, the CFPB notes that credit card APRs generally fall between about 12% and 30%. (APR vs APY explained.)

The fee feels small because it's for two weeks. The problem is what happens when you can't repay the whole amount on the due date.

The rollover trap

If you can't repay, many lenders let you "roll over" or "renew" the loan: you pay just the fee, and the full loan is extended for another two weeks, with another fee. Some borrowers repay and immediately borrow again. Either way, the fees keep coming while the amount owed stays the same.

The underlying issue is simple: if your budget was short by $400 this payday, it will often be short again two weeks later, now with $460 to find.

A worked example: Sol's $400 loan

Sol (a fictional example) needs $400 to cover an electric bill before payday and takes a payday loan with a $60 fee, due in two weeks: $460 in total.

On the due date, Sol can't spare $460, so Sol pays the $60 fee and rolls the loan over. The same thing happens four more times.

After 4 weeks$120$400

After ten weeks, Sol has paid $300 in fees and still owes the original $400.

The alternative path: Sol calls the electric company first and asks for a payment arrangement on the $400 bill, splitting it over the next three bills, and uses $120 from a small savings pot for this month's share. Cost: $0 in fees. Sol also checks whether the local LIHEAP office can help with energy bills.

What to do instead of a payday loan

1. Call the company you owe

Many utilities, landlords, phone companies and medical providers offer payment plans or extensions if you ask before the due date. A late fee from the original company is often much smaller than a payday loan fee.

2. Check for help with essentials

The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps eligible households with heating and cooling bills, and food banks and local assistance programmes can free up money for a bill. (How to budget on a low income.)

3. Ask your credit union about a small loan

Some credit unions and banks offer small-dollar loans with much lower costs than payday loans, sometimes to members with less-than-perfect credit. Ask what's available and how quickly it can be paid out.

4. Ask your employer

Some employers offer pay advances or hardship programmes, sometimes through payroll apps. Check any fees and how repayment works.

5. Use a credit card carefully

A credit card cash advance or purchase is expensive, but at a typical card APR it usually costs far less than a payday loan. Pay it off as fast as you can.

6. Talk to a nonprofit credit counsellor

If you're regularly short, a nonprofit credit counselling agency can review your budget and debts with you, usually free or at low cost.

If you already have a payday loan

  • Don't keep rolling it over if you can avoid it. Each rollover adds a fee.
  • Ask about an extended payment plan. Some states require lenders to offer one, letting you repay in instalments without extra fees.
  • Know your bank rights. You can generally stop a lender from taking repeated automatic withdrawals by revoking the authorization with the lender and telling your bank. Ask your bank how its stop-payment process works and what it charges.
  • Prioritise essentials. Rent, utilities, food and transport for work come before an unsecured loan. (How to stop living paycheck to paycheck.)

Building a buffer so it doesn't happen again

The best defence against payday loans is a small cushion of savings. It doesn't need to be large: even $300 to $500 covers many of the surprises that send people to payday lenders.

  • Start tiny. Set up an automatic transfer of $10 or $20 each payday into a separate account.
  • Use windfalls. Put part of a tax refund, a bonus or a third paycheck into the buffer. (What to do with your tax refund.)
  • Plan for known costs. Car registration, school supplies and annual fees are predictable; a sinking fund turns them into small monthly amounts. (Sinking funds, explained.)
  • Build credit over time, so cheaper options are open to you later. A secured credit card, used lightly and paid in full, can help you build or improve your credit history. (How to build credit from scratch.)

Signs a lender is one to avoid

Some lenders operate outside state rules, especially online. Be wary of any lender that asks for an upfront fee before giving you a loan, won't say clearly what the loan will cost, isn't licensed in your state, or pressures you to borrow more than you asked for.

Common questions

Do payday loans affect your credit score?

Many payday lenders don't report on-time payments to the major credit bureaus, so repaying usually won't help your score. But if a loan goes unpaid and is sent to collections, that can show up on your credit report and hurt your score.

Can a payday lender take money from my bank account?

If you gave the lender permission to withdraw from your account, it can try to collect on the due date. You can revoke that permission, and you can ask your bank to stop payments. You'll still owe the debt, so contact the lender about a plan.

Are payday loans legal everywhere?

No. Payday lending rules are set largely by states. Some states ban payday loans or cap their fees, others allow them with limits. Check your state's financial regulator or attorney general for local rules.

What's the cheapest way to borrow a small amount quickly?

Often it's not borrowing at all: a payment plan with the company you owe. If you do need to borrow, a small loan from a credit union or a credit card you can pay off quickly usually costs much less than a payday loan.
