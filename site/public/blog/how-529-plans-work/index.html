529 plans explained: how to save for education with tax advantages

How 529 college savings plans work, what counts as a qualified expense, the tax rules and fees, how to choose a plan, and a worked example.

Education costs are one of the biggest expenses many families plan for, and a 529 plan is one of the most common ways to save for them. It works a bit like a retirement account for education: money can grow without yearly tax, and withdrawals for qualified education costs are tax-free.

This guide explains how 529 plans work, what you can use them for, the rules and fees to know, how to choose a plan, and a worked example of starting early versus later.

What a 529 plan is

Investor.gov, the SEC's investor education site, describes a 529 plan as a tax-advantaged savings plan designed to encourage saving for certain education costs. Their legal name is "qualified tuition plans," after Section 529 of the tax code. They're sponsored by states, state agencies or educational institutions, and every state and the District of Columbia sponsors at least one.

There are two types:

  • Education savings plans, the more common type. You invest contributions in options such as mutual funds or age-based portfolios, and the value goes up or down with the investments.
  • Prepaid tuition plans, which let you pay for future tuition at participating colleges at today's prices. Investor.gov notes they usually have residency requirements.

The rest of this guide focuses on education savings plans.

What you can use the money for

Investor.gov lists the qualified expenses for education savings plans, including:

  • College and other post-secondary education: tuition and certain other costs at eligible schools.
  • Recognised post-secondary credential programmes.
  • Certain apprenticeship costs.
  • Student loan repayments: up to $10,000 in qualified education loan repayments.
  • K–12 tuition: up to $20,000 per year per beneficiary for tuition at public, private or religious elementary or secondary schools.

Rules on what counts can change, and each state decides whether it follows the federal rules for state tax purposes, so check your plan's current list before you withdraw.

The tax rules

  • Contributions aren't deductible on your federal return, but many states offer a deduction or credit for contributions, sometimes only for their own state's plan.
  • Growth isn't taxed each year while it stays in the account.
  • Qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
  • Non-qualified withdrawals: Investor.gov says the earnings portion is subject to federal and state income tax plus an additional 10% federal penalty on the earnings.

Leftover money

If the beneficiary doesn't need all the money, you can usually change the beneficiary to another family member. Investor.gov also notes that rolling leftover funds into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary is possible, within specific limits. (Roth vs traditional IRA.)

Fees

Fees reduce your returns, and Investor.gov lists several kinds: enrollment fees, annual maintenance fees, programme management fees and asset management fees. Plans sold through brokers may also charge sales loads and distribution fees. Ways to keep costs low:

  • Buy direct from the plan rather than through a broker, if you don't need advice.
  • Compare the investment options' expense ratios. (Expense ratios explained.)
  • Look for fee waivers: Investor.gov notes some plans waive fees for automatic contributions, larger balances or electronic documents.

How to choose a 529 plan

1. Check your own state's plan first

See whether your state offers a tax deduction, credit or matching grant for contributions, and whether it applies only to its own plan.

2. Compare with other states' plans

You can usually use any state's plan. Investor.gov notes that a lower-cost out-of-state plan can outweigh your own state's tax benefit.

3. Compare fees and investment options

Look at total yearly costs, and at whether the plan offers low-cost index funds and age-based portfolios.

4. Decide direct-sold or advisor-sold

Direct-sold plans avoid broker charges. Advisor-sold plans can include advice, at a higher cost.

5. Pick an investment option

Age-based (or target-enrollment) portfolios automatically shift toward more conservative investments as the beneficiary gets closer to school age. Investor.gov notes you can generally change investment options only twice a year or when you change the beneficiary.

6. Automate contributions

Set up a monthly contribution, and consider asking relatives to contribute for birthdays or holidays.

529 plans and financial aid

Investor.gov notes that savings in a 529 plan generally reduce eligibility for need-based financial aid. A parent-owned 529 is usually treated more favourably than money held in the student's own name, but rules vary by school and aid programme, so check with the school's financial aid office.

Should you save for education or retirement first?

Many planners suggest putting your own retirement first, especially getting any employer match, because students can borrow for school but you can't borrow for retirement. Once you're on track, a 529 can be a good next step. (Retirement savings in your 20s.)

How to open a 529 account

Opening an account usually takes about 20 minutes online. You'll need:

  • your own Social Security number or taxpayer ID, as the account owner;
  • the beneficiary's name, date of birth and Social Security number (for a newborn, you can often name yourself and change it later, or wait for the child's number);
  • bank details for the first contribution;
  • your investment choice, such as an age-based portfolio.

Many plans let you set up automatic monthly contributions at the same time, and some let you name a successor owner who takes over the account if something happens to you.

How to withdraw for qualified expenses

When it's time to pay, request a withdrawal from the plan, either paid directly to the school or to you or the student. Keep records that match each withdrawal to a qualified expense in the same tax year, such as tuition bills and receipts for books and required supplies. The plan sends a tax form showing the withdrawals; your records show they were for qualified costs.

Timing matters: try to take the money out in the same calendar year that you pay the expense. Coordinate with any education tax credits you claim, since the same expenses usually can't count for both a credit and a tax-free 529 withdrawal.

529 plans compared with other ways to save

A 529 isn't the only way to save for education. Some families use a regular savings account, a custodial account in the child's name, or a Roth IRA. Each has trade-offs:

Use of moneyQualified education costs (tax-free)AnythingAnything for the child's benefit; becomes theirs at adulthood
ControlThe account ownerThe account ownerPasses to the child at the age set by state law
Investment riskYes, depends on optionsNoYes, depends on investments

For money clearly earmarked for education, a 529's tax treatment is usually the main advantage. For flexible goals, a savings account may suit better.

Common questions

Can grandparents or friends contribute to a 529 plan?

Yes. Anyone can contribute to a 529 plan for a beneficiary, and many plans offer gift links. Large gifts may have gift-tax reporting rules, so check the current annual exclusion if you plan to give a lot.

What happens if my child gets a scholarship?

You can usually withdraw up to the scholarship amount without the 10% penalty, though income tax still applies to the earnings. You can also keep the money for other qualified expenses or change the beneficiary.

Can I use a 529 plan from another state?

Yes, in most cases. You can open almost any state's education savings plan, and use it for eligible schools anywhere. Compare your own state's tax benefits with other plans' fees and options.

Who controls the money in a 529 plan?

The account owner, usually a parent or grandparent, controls the account, chooses the investments and decides when to withdraw. The beneficiary is the student the money is meant for.
