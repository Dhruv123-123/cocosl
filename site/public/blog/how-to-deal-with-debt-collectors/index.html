How to deal with debt collectors: your rights, what to say, and what to send

What a debt collector must tell you, the 30-day window to dispute, calling limits, old debts, settling safely, and a worked example of a disputed debt.

A call or letter from a debt collector is stressful, especially if you don't recognise the debt or can't pay it right now. But you have more rights than most people realise, and a few simple steps put you in control: find out exactly what's claimed, check it, and decide in writing what happens next.

This guide explains what a collector must tell you, how to dispute a debt, the limits on calls and contact, what to do about old debts, and how to settle safely. It's general information, not legal advice.

Who counts as a debt collector

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) mainly covers third-party collectors: agencies collecting for someone else, companies that buy old debts, and lawyers who collect debts as a regular part of their business. A company collecting its own debt (your card issuer's in-house team, for example) may not be covered by the same rules, though state laws and other federal laws can still apply.

What a collector must tell you

When a collector first contacts you, or within five days of that first contact, it must give you validation information, usually in a letter called a validation notice. It includes:

  • the collector's name and address;
  • the name of the creditor;
  • the amount it says you owe, with a breakdown;
  • your rights, including how and by when to dispute the debt.

If you don't receive this, ask for it in writing, and get it before you pay or agree to anything.

Your 30-day window to dispute

You have 30 days from receiving the validation information to dispute the debt. If you dispute in writing within that window, the collector must stop collecting until it mails you verification of the debt. If you don't dispute within 30 days, the collector can assume the debt is valid (though you can still dispute later).

Dispute if:

  • you don't recognise the debt;
  • the amount looks wrong, or includes fees you didn't agree to;
  • you believe it was already paid, settled or discharged in bankruptcy;
  • it may be the result of identity theft.

Limits on calls and contact

Under federal rules, collectors generally:

  • can't call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your time;
  • are presumed to break the law if they call about a debt more than seven times in seven days, or within seven days after a phone conversation with you about it;
  • must stop contacting you at times or places you say are inconvenient, such as at work;
  • can't threaten violence, use obscene language, or lie about the amount you owe;
  • can't falsely claim to be police or threaten arrest;
  • can't post about your debt publicly on social media.

You can also tell a collector to stop contacting you altogether, in writing. That stops calls and letters, but it doesn't erase the debt, and the collector may still sue or report it.

How to respond to a debt collector

1. Don't panic, and don't pay on the first call

Write down the caller's name, company, phone number and the amount claimed. Say you'll respond in writing once you receive the validation notice.

2. Check it's a real collector

Scammers pose as collectors. Look up the company independently, not with the number they gave you, and never pay by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency.

3. Compare the debt with your records

Check old statements and your free credit reports. Is the creditor right? The amount? The dates? (How to dispute a credit report error.)

4. Dispute in writing within 30 days if anything is off

Send a short letter saying you dispute all or part of the debt and asking for verification and the name and address of the original creditor. Keep a copy and proof of sending.

5. Check the statute of limitations on old debts

Each state sets a time limit for lawsuits over debts. After it passes, the debt is "time-barred": a collector can't sue you over it. In some states, a payment or written acknowledgment can restart the clock, so get advice before paying anything on an old debt.

6. Decide what to do, in writing

Pay in full, negotiate a settlement or a payment plan, or (for a time-barred debt) decide not to pay. Whatever you agree, get it in writing before you pay.

Settling a debt safely

  • Get the agreement in writing before you pay: the amount, that it settles the debt in full, and how it will be reported.
  • Pay by a traceable method you control. Don't give a collector ongoing access to your bank account.
  • Keep everything: letters, emails, payment receipts.
  • Know the tax angle: forgiven debt can sometimes count as taxable income. Ask if you receive a Form 1099-C.

If you're sued

Don't ignore court papers. If you don't respond by the deadline, the court may enter a judgment against you, which can lead to wage garnishment or a bank levy. Respond, show up, and raise any defences, such as the debt not being yours or being time-barred. Legal aid organisations can help for free if you qualify.

  • Complaints: you can submit a complaint to the CFPB, and report problems to the FTC and your state attorney general.

Keep a simple paper trail

Debt collection disputes often come down to who can show what, and when. Keep one folder, paper or digital, for each debt:

  • Every letter and validation notice, with the date it arrived.
  • Copies of everything you send, plus proof of mailing. Certified mail with a return receipt costs a little more but shows the date the collector received it.
  • A call log: date, time, the collector's name and company, and what was said. If a collector breaks the contact rules, a log makes it easier to report.
  • Any settlement agreement, signed and in writing, before you pay.
  • Proof of payment, and a final letter confirming the account is resolved.

Keep the folder for several years after the debt is settled. Old debts are sometimes sold again, and a new collector may contact you about an account you've already paid.

Common questions

What should I say when a debt collector calls?

Get the collector's name, company, address and phone number, and ask them to send the validation information in writing. You don't have to discuss the debt or agree to pay on the phone.

Can a debt collector take money from my bank account?

Not on its own. A collector generally needs a court judgment before it can garnish wages or levy a bank account, unless you've given it permission, for example by authorising automatic payments. Some federal benefits have extra protections.

Will paying a collection remove it from my credit report?

Not necessarily. A paid collection may still appear on your report for up to seven years from the original delinquency, though some newer scoring models ignore paid collections. Ask how it will be reported before you pay.

How do I stop a debt collector from calling me?

Send a written request asking the collector to stop contacting you. It must then stop, except to confirm it will stop or to tell you about specific actions like a lawsuit. The debt itself still exists.
