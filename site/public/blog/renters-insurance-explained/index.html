Renters insurance explained: what it covers, what it costs, and how to buy it

What renters insurance covers (and doesn't), how to choose your coverage amount and deductible, and a worked example of a claim after a burst pipe.

If you rent, your landlord's insurance covers the building, not your things. If a fire, a burst pipe or a break-in destroys your laptop, clothes and furniture, the landlord's policy pays nothing toward them. **Renters insurance** fills that gap, and it usually costs less per month than a streaming subscription or two.

This guide explains what a standard policy covers, what it leaves out, how to pick the right amounts, and what happens when you make a claim.

The three parts of a policy

A standard renters policy has three main parts.

1. Personal property

This pays to repair or replace your belongings if they're damaged or stolen by a covered event, like fire, smoke, theft, vandalism, or water from a burst pipe. It usually covers your things away from home too, such as a bike stolen at work or a suitcase taken while travelling, often up to a limit.

2. Liability

If someone is hurt in your home, or you accidentally damage someone else's property (say your bath overflows into the apartment below), liability coverage can pay for their costs and your legal defence. Policies often include a small amount of medical payments coverage for guests' minor injuries, regardless of fault.

3. Loss of use (additional living expenses)

If your home becomes unlivable after a covered loss, this pays for extra costs above your normal spending, like a hotel and the extra cost of eating out, while it's repaired.

What it usually doesn't cover

Read your policy for the details, but standard renters policies generally exclude:

  • Floods, including rising water from storms. Flood insurance is separate, for example through the National Flood Insurance Program.
  • Earthquakes, which usually need a separate policy or add-on.
  • Pest damage, like bedbugs or rodents.
  • Your roommate's things, unless they're named on your policy. Each roommate usually needs their own.
  • High-value items above a sub-limit. Jewellery, watches, art and collectibles are often capped at a low amount (for example $1,500 for jewellery theft) unless you add extra coverage.
  • Your car, which is covered by car insurance, though items stolen from inside your car are often covered by renters insurance.
  • Wear and tear.

Replacement cost vs actual cash value

This choice makes a big difference to how much you'd actually get.

  • Actual cash value pays what your things were worth at the time, after depreciation. A four-year-old laptop might be worth a fraction of what it cost.
  • Replacement cost pays what it costs to buy a similar new item today.

Replacement cost coverage costs a little more, but it's usually worth it. With actual cash value, you might get far too little to replace what you lost. Some insurers pay the actual cash value first and the rest once you've bought the replacement and shown the receipt.

How to buy a policy

1. Estimate what your things are worth

Walk through each room and list the big items: electronics, furniture, bedding, clothes, kitchen gear, bike, instruments. Most people are surprised by the total. A simple home inventory (photos or a short video of each room) helps here and is invaluable if you ever need to claim.

2. Choose your personal property limit

Round your estimate up. Many renters choose $15,000 to $30,000. If you own high-value items, ask about extra coverage, sometimes called a "rider" or "scheduled personal property".

3. Choose a liability limit

$100,000 is a common starting point. Some landlords require a minimum in the lease. If you have significant savings or assets, a higher limit costs relatively little.

4. Choose a deductible

The deductible is what you pay toward a claim before the insurer pays the rest. A higher deductible lowers your premium, but make sure it's an amount you could pay from your emergency fund. $500 or $1,000 are common. (How much emergency fund you need.)

5. Get two or three quotes

Start with the company that insures your car: bundling often gives a discount. Compare the same coverage amounts, deductible and replacement cost setting across quotes.

6. Add your landlord as an "interested party" if asked

Some leases require proof of coverage. Adding the landlord means they're notified if the policy is cancelled; it doesn't give them any of your coverage.

A worked example: Lina's burst pipe

Lina (a fictional example) rents a one-bedroom apartment. Lina's policy has:

  • $25,000 personal property, replacement cost
  • $500 deductible
  • $100,000 liability
  • loss of use included

One winter night, a pipe bursts in the apartment above. Water comes through the ceiling and damages:

ItemCost to replace
Laptop$1,100
Couch$800
Rug$250
Clothes$450
Total$2,600

The apartment needs two nights of drying and repairs, so Lina stays in a hotel.

What Lina gets:

  • Personal property: $2,600 − $500 deductible = $2,100
  • Loss of use: the $280 hotel bill (and any extra food costs above Lina's normal grocery spending), with no deductible on this part of many policies

If Lina had chosen actual cash value instead: the insurer might have valued the depreciated items at about $1,300, leaving $800 after the deductible. That's not enough to replace a laptop, never mind a couch.

What made the claim easy: Lina had taken a two-minute video of the apartment when moving in and kept the laptop receipt in an email folder. The claim was paid within a couple of weeks.

(Figures are illustrative. Your insurer, state and policy terms decide what's paid.)

How much does it cost?

Renters insurance is one of the cheaper types of insurance, often somewhere in the range of a small monthly bill. Your price depends on:

  • where you live, including local theft and weather risk;
  • your coverage amounts and deductible;
  • replacement cost or actual cash value;
  • your claims history and, in many states, credit-based insurance scores;
  • discounts, such as bundling with car insurance, smoke detectors, or a security system.

Put the premium in your budget as a fixed bill. Paying annually is sometimes cheaper than monthly. (Sinking funds for annual bills.)

How to make a home inventory in 20 minutes

A home inventory is the single most useful thing you can do after buying a policy, and it doesn't have to be a spreadsheet.

  1. Film each room. Walk slowly, open closets and drawers, and say what things are as you go ("the TV, about three years old").
  2. Photograph serial numbers on electronics and appliances.
  3. Save receipts for big purchases in one email folder or cloud folder.
  4. Store it away from home, in cloud storage or an email to yourself, so it isn't lost in the same fire or flood.
  5. Update it once a year, or after a big purchase.

It also helps you choose your coverage amount: watching the video back is often what makes people realise their things are worth more than they guessed.

Renters insurance and roommates, partners and pets

  • Partners living together can often share one policy if both are named on it, which may be cheaper than two.
  • Pets: liability coverage may cover injuries your dog causes to others, but some policies exclude certain breeds or bite history. Ask before you buy.
  • Moving: tell your insurer when you move. Your policy is tied to your address, and the price may change.

Making a claim: what to do

  1. Make sure everyone is safe, and stop further damage if you can (move things away from water, for example).
  2. Report theft or vandalism to the police and get a report number.
  3. Tell your landlord about any building damage.
  4. Take photos and video of everything damaged before you throw anything away.
  5. Contact your insurer as soon as you can, and follow their instructions.
  6. Keep receipts for any hotel, meals or emergency purchases.

For small losses close to your deductible, it may not be worth claiming: a claim can affect future premiums.

Common questions

Is renters insurance required?

No law requires it, but many landlords make it a condition of the lease. Even when it isn't required, it's usually worth having if you couldn't easily afford to replace your things or pay for an accident in your home.

Does renters insurance cover my roommate?

Usually not, unless your roommate is named on your policy. Some insurers allow it, but it means shared claims and a shared claims history, so many roommates buy separate policies.

Does renters insurance cover flood damage?

Standard policies don't cover flooding from rising water, such as storms or overflowing rivers. Flood insurance is a separate policy. Water from a burst pipe or an overflowing appliance usually is covered.

Does renters insurance cover my laptop if it's stolen outside my home?

Often yes. Many policies cover personal property worldwide, though sometimes with a lower limit for items away from home. Check your policy's "off-premises" coverage.
