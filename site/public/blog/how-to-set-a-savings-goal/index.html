How to set a savings goal you'll actually reach

Pick one goal, put a number and a date on it, work out the monthly amount, and make it automatic. A step-by-step method with a worked example.

"Save more" is one of the most common money resolutions, and one of the least likely to happen. It isn't a lack of willpower. It's that "more" has no number, no date and no plan, so there's nothing to check against and no moment where you've succeeded.

A savings goal works when it's specific enough to act on. This guide shows how to choose one, put a number and a date on it, work out exactly what to save each month, and make it automatic, with a worked example.

Why goals help

America Saves, which runs America Saves Week each April (April 5 to 9 in 2027), builds its whole approach around the same idea: people review their finances, set a savings target and map out how to reach it. Its pledge is described as the framework that lets savers set a goal. The habit is simple, but writing a goal down and planning the steps turns a wish into something you can track.

It also matters because many households don't have much of a cushion. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey of household finances, 63% of adults said they could cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent, which means many others couldn't. A clear first goal is often the step that changes that.

How to set a savings goal

1. Pick one goal to start

List what you'd like to save for: an emergency fund, a car repair fund, a trip, a home deposit, holiday gifts. Then pick one to focus on first. If you don't have any savings yet, a small emergency fund usually comes first. (How much emergency fund do I need?.)

2. Put a number on it

Find the real cost. For an emergency fund, a first target might be $500 or $1,000, or one month of essential bills. For a trip or a purchase, look up actual prices. For known yearly costs, add up last year's bills.

3. Set a date

Choose a realistic deadline. A date makes the goal concrete and lets you work out the monthly amount. Short-term goals (under a year or two) are easier to keep going with than distant ones.

4. Work out the monthly amount

Subtract anything you've already saved, then divide by the number of months until your date. If you're paid every two weeks or twice a month, divide again to get a per-paycheck amount.

5. Find the money in your budget

Look at your take-home pay and spending to see where the amount can come from: a cancelled subscription, a lower bill, a spending category you trim, or part of a raise. The CFPB's budget worksheet is a simple way to see where your money goes. (How to make a budget you will keep.)

6. Make it automatic

Set up an automatic transfer to a separate savings account on payday. Saving first, before you can spend it, is more reliable than saving what's left at the end of the month. (How to automate your savings.)

7. Track it and celebrate milestones

Check progress once a month, and mark each quarter of the way. Seeing the balance grow is what keeps most people going.

SMART goals, simply

You may have seen the SMART framework: goals that are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. For savings, it boils down to three questions:

QuestionWeak goalStrong goal
What exactly?"Save more""Build a starter emergency fund"
How much?"A bit each month""$1,000"
By when?"Soon""By March 31"

A worked example: Gia's first goal

Gia (a fictional example) has wanted to "save more" for years. She writes down three possible goals: an emergency fund, a trip, and a new laptop. With only $150 saved, she picks a $1,000 starter emergency fund first.

StepGia's numbers
Goal$1,000 emergency fund
Already saved$150
Still needed$850
Deadline10 months away

Per paycheck (paid twice a month)$42.50, rounded to $45

To find the money, Gia cancels two subscriptions she rarely uses ($24 a month), plans meals to cut takeout ($40 a month), and sells an old phone for $120 as a one-off boost. She sets up an automatic $45 transfer each payday into a separate savings account named "Emergency fund."

With the transfers and the one-off boost, she reaches $1,000 in about eight months, two months early. Her next goal is a $1,800 fund for her car registration and insurance renewals. (Sinking funds, explained.)

Choosing between several goals

If you have more than one goal, a simple order works for many people:

  1. A starter emergency fund, so surprises don't go on a credit card.
  2. Any employer retirement match, because it's extra pay. (The 401(k) match, explained.)
  3. High-interest debt, which usually costs more than savings earn. (Debt avalanche vs snowball.)
  4. Known upcoming costs with sinking funds.
  5. Bigger goals like a home deposit or a longer-term emergency fund.

You can work on more than one goal at once by splitting your savings, for example 70% to the main goal and 30% to the next.

Where to keep the money

The right place depends on when you'll need it:

  • Within a few years: a high-yield savings account or similar, at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union. (How to open a high-yield savings account.)
  • On a fixed date: a CD can lock in a rate if you won't need the money before then. (CDs explained.)
  • Many years away: long-term goals like retirement are usually invested, accepting ups and downs. (Short-term vs long-term savings goals.)

Naming the account after the goal ("Emergency fund", "Car fund") makes it less tempting to dip into.

If you fall behind

Goals slip. A car repair or a short month can set you back. When it happens:

  • Don't stop the automatic transfer if you can avoid it; lower it instead.
  • Move the date rather than giving up on the goal.
  • Use windfalls to catch up: a tax refund, a bonus, or a third paycheck in a month. (What to do with your tax refund.)
  • Remember what the money is for. If an emergency fund gets used for an emergency, it did its job.

Make it visible

Small things help a goal stay front of mind:

  • A progress chart on the fridge or a tracker you colour in.
  • A monthly check-in on the same day each month, five minutes long.
  • A savings challenge for extra motivation. (No-spend challenge.)
  • Telling someone, a partner or friend, so you're accountable.

Goals for different stages of life

The right first goal depends on where you are:

  • Students and first jobs: a starter emergency fund, then the employer match, then a fund for moving costs or a car. (Moving out for the first time.)
  • Renting and building a career: a fuller emergency fund, sinking funds for car and insurance costs, and a home deposit if buying is a goal.
  • Families: a larger emergency fund, sinking funds for school and holiday costs, and education savings once retirement is on track. (How 529 plans work.)
  • Freelancers and side hustlers: a tax fund alongside the emergency fund, since tax isn't withheld. (Estimated tax payments explained.)

A five-minute goal worksheet

Write these down for your goal, on paper or in your phone:

  1. My goal: what the money is for.
  2. Target amount: the real cost, from prices or last year's bills.
  3. Already saved: what you have toward it today.
  4. Deadline: the date you want it by.
  5. Monthly amount: (target minus already saved) divided by months left.
  6. Where it comes from: the subscription, bill or habit that frees the money.
  7. Where it goes: the account, and the day the automatic transfer runs.

If the monthly amount is more than you can manage, move the date or lower the target, rather than skipping the plan.

Common questions

What's a realistic first savings goal?

For many people, $500 to $1,000 in a starter emergency fund is a realistic first goal. It's enough to cover many common surprises, and reachable in months rather than years with small automatic transfers.

Should I save or pay off debt first?

Many people do both: a small emergency fund first, then extra payments on high-interest debt, while getting any employer retirement match. Without a buffer, the next surprise can go straight back onto a card.

How do I stay motivated to save?

Keep the goal specific, automate the transfer so it happens without a decision, and track progress monthly. Breaking a big goal into milestones and marking each one helps too.

What is America Saves Week?

America Saves Week is an annual campaign, held each April, that encourages people to review their finances, set a savings goal and plan how to reach it. In 2027 it runs from April 5 to 9.
