Can't pay your taxes? What to do, and what each option really costs

Why you should still file on time, how IRS payment plans work, and a worked example comparing a payment plan with a credit card on a $4,200 bill.

Finding out you owe the IRS more than you have is stressful, and the instinct for many people is to put off filing until they can pay. That's the one move that reliably makes it worse.

The IRS has more options for people who can't pay than most people realise, and some are fairly cheap. This guide explains what to do, in order, how payment plans work, and a worked example comparing the real cost of an IRS payment plan with putting the bill on a credit card.

The most important rule: file on time anyway

There are two separate penalties, and one is much bigger than the other:

  • Failure to file: generally 5% of the unpaid tax for each month (or part of a month) the return is late, up to 25%.
  • Failure to pay: generally 0.5% of the unpaid tax for each month, up to 25%. While you're on an IRS payment plan, and you filed on time, it drops to 0.25% a month.

So not filing costs about ten times as much per month as filing and not paying. If you owe $4,200 and do nothing for three months, the failure-to-file penalty alone could be $630. File by the deadline, pay what you can, and set up a plan for the rest.

Interest is also charged on unpaid tax from the due date until it's paid. The rate is set every quarter (it's the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points) and compounds daily.

If you need more time to file, request an extension. It doesn't give you more time to pay, but it does avoid the failure-to-file penalty if you file by the extended deadline. (How to file a tax extension.)

What to do, in order

1. File your return by the deadline

For 2026 returns, that's April 15, 2027, or October 15 with an extension. Filing stops the biggest penalty.

2. Pay as much as you can now

Every dollar you pay by the deadline is a dollar that doesn't collect interest and penalties. Pay through IRS Direct Pay (free, from a bank account) or your IRS online account.

3. Set up a payment plan for the rest

Most people can apply online in a few minutes, without calling. See the options below.

4. Set up automatic payments

Direct debit plans usually have the lowest setup fee and make it hard to miss a payment. Missing payments can end the agreement.

5. Fix next year's withholding

A balance due usually means too little was withheld or estimated payments were too small. Adjust your W-4 or estimated payments so it doesn't happen again. (How to fill out a W-4.)

IRS payment plan options

  • Short-term payment plan: up to 180 days to pay in full, for people who owe less than $100,000 in combined tax, penalties and interest. There's no setup fee, though penalties and interest still apply until it's paid.
  • Long-term payment plan (installment agreement): monthly payments, generally for people who owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties and interest and have filed all required returns. There's a setup fee, which is lowest if you apply online and pay by direct debit, and may be waived or reduced for people with low incomes.
  • Temporarily delayed collection: if you genuinely can't pay anything right now without being unable to cover basic living expenses, the IRS can mark your account as currently not collectible. Interest and penalties continue, and the IRS may review your situation later.
  • Offer in compromise: settling for less than you owe, for people who can't realistically pay the full amount. The IRS has strict rules and a pre-qualifier tool on its website. Be very wary of companies that promise to settle tax debt "for pennies on the dollar" for a large upfront fee; many people who are told they qualify don't.

Check current fees and limits on the IRS payment plans page before applying, since they change.

A worked example: Harper's $4,200

Harper (a fictional example) picked up freelance work alongside a regular job last year and didn't set enough aside. The return shows $4,200 owed, and Harper has none of it saved. Harper files on time and compares two ways to pay over 12 months.

Option 1: an IRS long-term payment plan. Using a hypothetical IRS interest rate of 7% a year (the real rate changes quarterly) and the reduced 0.25% monthly failure-to-pay penalty:

  • Monthly payment: about $363
  • Interest over 12 months: about $161
  • Failure-to-pay penalty: about $69
  • Plus a setup fee, lowest for an online direct debit plan

Total cost: about $230 plus the setup fee.

Option 2: put it on a credit card. The IRS accepts card payments through approved processors, which charge a fee (we'll use a hypothetical 1.85%). Then the balance sits on a card at 24.99%:

  • Processing fee: about $78
  • Monthly payment to clear it in 12 months: about $407
  • Card interest over 12 months: about $601

Total cost: about $679, around three times the payment plan.

Harper chooses the payment plan, sets up direct debit for the day after payday, and changes the W-4 at the regular job to have an extra amount withheld for the freelance income this year. (Self-employment tax, explained.)

A card can make sense in one case: a 0% intro APR card that you're certain you can pay off within the promotion, where the processing fee is the only cost. Even then, compare the fee with what a short-term IRS plan would cost.

Ways to find the money first

Before committing to a year of payments, check whether you can close some of the gap quickly:

  • An emergency fund or savings that's earning less than the IRS charges. Paying the IRS from savings and rebuilding them over a few months often costs less than a year of interest and penalties.
  • Money you know is coming, like a bonus, a deposit being returned or something you're selling. A short-term plan of up to 180 days can bridge the gap with no setup fee.
  • Your budget's flexible spending for a few months, pointed at the balance. (The four-line budget.)

Avoid raiding a 401(k) or IRA for a tax bill: withdrawals are usually taxed, often with a 10% penalty if you're under 59½, which can create next year's tax problem.

What happens if you ignore it

The IRS sends a series of letters before it takes stronger action, and each one tells you what you owe and what to do. If you keep ignoring them, the IRS can eventually file a tax lien (a legal claim against your property) or levy (seize) wages or bank accounts. It can also keep future refunds to pay the balance. All of that is avoidable by responding and setting up a plan.

Also be alert to scams. The IRS generally first contacts people by mail. It doesn't demand immediate payment by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency, and it doesn't threaten arrest over the phone. If you're unsure whether a letter is real, check your balance in your IRS online account.

If you owe because of a hardship

If you can't pay because of something outside your control, like a serious illness, a natural disaster or the death of a family member, you can ask the IRS to remove penalties for reasonable cause. For people with a clean record for the past three years, there's also first-time penalty abatement, which you can request by phone or in writing. Interest generally can't be removed, but it shrinks when penalties do.

Free help is available too. Low Income Taxpayer Clinics represent people in disputes with the IRS for free or a small fee, and the Taxpayer Advocate Service, an independent office within the IRS, helps people whose problems aren't being resolved through normal channels.

Common questions

What happens if I can't pay my taxes by April 15?

File your return on time anyway, pay what you can, and set up a payment plan for the rest. You'll owe interest and a failure-to-pay penalty on the unpaid amount, but filing on time avoids the much larger failure-to-file penalty.

How do I set up a payment plan with the IRS?

Most people can apply online on IRS.gov in a few minutes. Short-term plans (up to 180 days) have no setup fee; long-term monthly plans have a fee that's lowest for online direct debit. You need to have filed all required returns.

Should I put a tax bill on a credit card?

Usually not, if you'll carry the balance. Card processors charge a fee, and card interest is typically far higher than the IRS's interest and penalties on a payment plan. A card can make sense only if you can pay it off quickly, ideally on a 0% intro offer.

Will a payment plan hurt my credit score?

An IRS payment plan isn't reported to the credit bureaus, so it doesn't appear on your credit report. A federal tax lien isn't included on credit reports either, though it's a public record that lenders can find.
