Retirement savings in your 20s: how much, where to put it, and why starting small works

Why time matters more than amount, the order to use a 401(k) match, IRA and HSA, how much to aim for, and a worked example of starting at 25 vs 35.

Retirement feels impossibly far away in your 20s, and there's always something more urgent: rent, student loans, a car, a life. But this decade has one advantage you'll never get back: time. Money invested in your 20s has decades to grow, which is why small, steady amounts now can matter as much as much larger amounts later.

This guide covers why starting early matters, the order to use the accounts available to you, how much to aim for, and a worked example comparing starting at 25 with starting at 35.

Why time matters more than amount

Investment growth compounds: returns earn returns of their own. The longer money stays invested, the more of the final balance comes from growth rather than from what you put in. That's the whole case for starting early, even with small amounts.

It works in the other direction too: investments go down as well as up, sometimes for years. Long time horizons give you more room to ride out falls, which is why retirement money is usually invested differently from your emergency fund.

Before you start: the foundations

Retirement saving works best when a few basics are in place:

  • A starter emergency fund, so a surprise bill doesn't end up on a credit card or force you to withdraw early. (How much emergency fund you need.)
  • No high-interest debt, or a plan to clear it. Card interest usually costs more than investments are likely to earn. (Avalanche vs snowball.)

The one exception: don't skip an employer match while you build those, because it's an immediate addition to your pay.

The order to save in

1. 401(k) up to the full employer match

If your employer matches contributions, put in at least enough to get the full match. It's part of your pay; leaving it unclaimed is turning down money. (The 401(k) match, explained.)

2. Clear high-interest debt

Pay off credit cards and other high-rate debt before investing more.

3. A Roth or traditional IRA

An IRA lets you invest more with tax advantages, with a wide choice of low-cost funds. For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500 (plus $1,100 catch-up if you're 50 or older). In your 20s, when your tax rate is often lower than it will be later, many people choose a Roth IRA, where you pay tax now and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. (Roth vs traditional IRA.)

4. An HSA, if you have an eligible health plan

A Health Savings Account can be used for medical costs now or saved and invested for later. (HSA vs FSA.)

5. More in the 401(k)

Back to the 401(k), up to the yearly limit if you can. For 2026, that limit is $24,500 of your own contributions.

How much to aim for

Common guidelines suggest saving about 15% of your pay for retirement over a career, including any employer match. That's a lot for many people in their 20s, so treat it as a direction, not a test:

  • Start with the match, even if it's 3% or 4%.
  • Add 1% a year, or whenever you get a raise, until you reach your target. Many 401(k) plans can do this automatically.
  • Send part of every raise to retirement before you get used to spending it. (Lifestyle creep.)

What to invest in

For most people, low-cost, broadly diversified funds are a sensible core: for example, a target-date fund for your expected retirement year, or a mix of broad stock and bond index funds. Two things to check:

  • Fees. Expense ratios come out of your returns every year. Small differences add up over decades. (Expense ratios explained.)
  • Risk. Funds with more stocks tend to rise and fall more. A longer time horizon gives more room for ups and downs, but only you can decide how much you're comfortable with.

A worked example: Pia starts at 25

Pia (a fictional example) is 25 and starts putting $200 a month into a retirement account. To show how time affects the outcome, here's what happens under a hypothetical 6% average annual return, compared with starting later. (This is an illustration, not a prediction: real returns vary year to year, can be negative, and fees, taxes and inflation are ignored.)

Start at 25$20040$96,000about $398,000

Start at 35, double the amount$40030$144,000about $402,000

Starting ten years later with the same $200 ends with about half as much. To catch up, the later starter has to put in twice as much each month, and $48,000 more in total.

Pia also gets a 3% employer match on a $50,000 salary, an extra $125 a month, so Pia's real contribution is $325 a month for the price of $200.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Cashing out a 401(k) when you change jobs. You'll usually pay income tax and often a 10% early withdrawal penalty, and lose the growth. Roll it over to your new plan or an IRA instead.
  • Waiting until you "earn more". Start with what you can, even $25 or $50 a month, and raise it later.
  • Leaving money uninvested. Money contributed to an IRA may sit in cash until you choose an investment. Check that it's actually invested.
  • Checking your balance constantly. Short-term falls are normal. Checking less often helps you stay invested.
  • Paying high fees. Compare fund expense ratios and any account fees.

If you're self-employed or have no workplace plan

You can still save for retirement. An IRA is open to anyone with earned income, and self-employed people can use accounts such as a SEP IRA or a solo 401(k), which can allow larger contributions. (Self-employment tax explained.)

Automate it so you don't have to decide each month

Retirement saving works best when it happens without a monthly decision:

  • Workplace plan: contributions come straight out of your paycheck, before you see the money. Choose a percentage rather than a dollar amount so it rises with your pay.
  • Automatic increases: many plans let you raise your rate by 1% each year on a date you choose.
  • IRA: set up a recurring transfer from checking for the day after payday, and an automatic investment into the fund you picked, so contributions don't sit in cash.
  • Check once or twice a year: review your contribution rate, your investment choice and any fees, then leave it alone.

Where to find your plan details

Your employer's benefits portal or plan provider will show the information you need to get started: whether there's a match and how it's calculated, any vesting schedule (how long you must stay before employer contributions are fully yours), the investment options and their expense ratios, and whether you were automatically enrolled and at what rate. If you were auto-enrolled at a low rate, check whether it's enough to get the full match. (How to set up your first 401(k).)

Don't forget old accounts

Many people change jobs several times in their 20s, and small 401(k) balances are easy to lose track of. Each time you leave a job, decide what to do with the account: leave it in the old plan if allowed, roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Keep a simple list of every account, its provider and your login, and update your address and beneficiaries whenever they change.

Common questions

Is there a retirement balance I should reach by 30?

There's no single right number. A common rule of thumb is about one year's salary by 30, but it depends on when you started, your income and your other goals. Starting with your employer match and increasing your rate over time matters more than hitting a specific figure.

Is it better to pay off student loans or save for retirement?

Many people do both: get the full employer match first, then split extra money between higher-interest loans and retirement. Paying off debt doesn't involve investment risk, while investing does. (How to pay off student loans faster.)

Roth IRA or 401(k) first?

Usually the 401(k) up to the match comes first, because the match is extra money. After that, many people in their 20s contribute to a Roth IRA, then return to the 401(k).

Can I take money out of a Roth IRA early?

You can generally withdraw your own Roth IRA contributions at any time without tax or penalty, but earnings withdrawn early may be taxed and penalised. It's best to treat it as long-term money.
