Your first 401(k): how to set it up in your first week at a new job

Get the full match, pick Roth or traditional, choose a fund and name a beneficiary: a 401(k) setup for your first job, with a paycheck example.

Your first real job comes with a stack of forms, and the 401(k) one is the easiest to put off. It's also the one that matters most. The choices you make in the first week usually stay in place for years, because almost nobody goes back to change them.

The good news: setting up a 401(k) properly takes about 30 minutes, and you only need to get five things right. This guide walks through each one, then shows what it does to a real paycheck with a worked example.

What a 401(k) actually is

A 401(k) is a retirement account that your employer offers and that you fund straight from your paycheck. Money goes in before it reaches your bank account, which is why it works: you never see it, so you never have to decide to save it.

Three features make it worth more than a normal savings account:

  • Tax breaks. With a traditional 401(k), contributions come out before federal income tax, so you pay less tax now. With a Roth 401(k), you pay tax now, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
  • The employer match. Many employers add money when you contribute. That's part of your pay, and you only get it if you put money in. (How a match works.)
  • A high limit. For 2026 the IRS limit on what you can contribute is $24,500, and it's usually raised each year. You won't hit it on a first salary, but it means the account can grow with you.

The trade-off is access. Money in a 401(k) is meant for retirement, and taking it out early usually means income tax plus a 10% penalty. That's why the emergency fund comes first, and why this guide never asks you to put in more than you can leave alone.

Step 1: Find out whether you're already enrolled

Many employers now enroll new hires automatically, and a federal law (SECURE 2.0) requires it for most 401(k) plans set up after December 29, 2022: they have to start you at 3% to 10% of pay and raise it by a point a year until it reaches at least 10%. Older plans, small businesses and new businesses can be different.

So before you do anything else, log in to the plan's website (HR will tell you who runs it) and check three things:

  1. Are you enrolled, and at what percentage?
  2. Which fund is your money going into?
  3. When does the first contribution come out?

If you were enrolled automatically at 3%, that's a start, not a decision. The default rate is often below what gets you the full match.

Step 2: Contribute at least enough to get the full match

Find the match formula in your benefits guide. It's usually written like "50% of the first 6% of pay" or "100% of the first 4%". Whatever the formula, the number that matters is the percentage you need to contribute to get all of it. In those two examples, that's 6% and 4%.

That's your floor. Contributing less leaves part of your pay on the table, and no investment reliably beats an instant 50% or 100% return on the money you put in.

If even the match percentage feels impossible right now, start lower and set an automatic increase (Step 5). But check your budget first: once the money goes in before you see it, most people find they adjust within a month or two. (A budget you'll keep.)

Step 3: Pick Roth or traditional

If your plan offers both, you're choosing when to pay tax:

  • Traditional: no federal income tax on the money now; you pay tax when you withdraw it in retirement.
  • Roth: you pay tax now; qualified withdrawals later are tax-free, growth included.

A useful rule of thumb for a first job: if you're in the 10% or 12% federal bracket, Roth is often the better bet, because your tax rate now is probably as low as it will be in your career. If you're already in the 22% bracket or higher, traditional's tax break now is worth more, and many people split the difference. (Roth vs traditional, in full.)

Two things don't change with this choice. The employer match generally goes into the traditional (pre-tax) side either way, and Social Security and Medicare tax still come out of your full pay. Only income tax is affected.

Step 4: Choose the investments, not just the account

This is the step people miss. Putting money in a 401(k) and investing it are two different things. Some plans park new money in a cash or "stable value" fund until you pick something, and it can sit there for years earning very little.

For most people starting out, the simplest good option is a target-date fund with the year you'd turn about 65 in its name (for example, a 2065 fund for someone born around 2000). It holds a mix of stock and bond index funds, rebalances on its own, and gets more cautious as the date gets closer. One fund, no maintenance.

Before you pick it, check the expense ratio in the fund list. A broad index fund or target-date fund in a decent plan might charge 0.05% to 0.20% a year. If the target-date funds charge 0.70% or more and the plan has an S&P 500 or total-market index fund at a fraction of that, the index fund is worth a look. (Why fees matter so much.)

Step 5: Turn on automatic increases, then name a beneficiary

Two small settings finish the job.

Automatic increase (sometimes "auto-escalation"). Set your contribution to rise by 1% each year, ideally timed with your raise. You'll barely notice it, and it's the easiest way to get from "the match" to the 10% to 15% of pay that's often suggested for long-term saving.

Beneficiary. Name who gets the account if something happens to you. It takes two minutes, it overrides your will for this account, and it's the form people most often forget.

A worked example: Maya's first paycheck

Maya (a fictional example) starts her first job on a $52,000 salary, paid every two weeks, so each paycheck is $2,000 before tax. Her employer matches 50% of what she contributes, up to 6% of pay. Her plan auto-enrolled her at 3%.

At the default 3%: Maya puts in $1,560 a year and her employer adds $780.

At 6%, enough for the full match: Maya puts in $3,120 a year ($120 a paycheck) and her employer adds $1,560. Moving from 3% to 6% gets her an extra $780 a year in match, money she was leaving behind at the default.

What it does to her take-home pay: with a traditional 401(k), the $120 comes out before federal income tax. In the 12% bracket, that saves her about $14.40 in federal tax each paycheck, so her take-home pay falls by about $105.60, not $120. (State tax, if her state has one, softens it a little more.) With a Roth 401(k), take-home falls by the full $120, and she gets the tax break later instead.

What it can grow to: if Maya kept contributing 6% with the match for 40 years, never got a raise, and earned a hypothetical 6% a year, the account would be worth about $777,000. At the default 3% with its smaller match, the same assumptions give about $388,000. Those figures aren't adjusted for inflation and real returns vary year to year, but the gap shows why the first-week choice matters: the same job, the same employer, and twice the balance.

Vesting: when the match is really yours

Your own contributions are always 100% yours. The employer's match may vest over time, which means you only keep it if you stay a certain length of time. Common schedules are "cliff" (0% until, say, 3 years, then 100%) or "graded" (20% a year until 100%). Federal rules cap how long vesting can take.

Vesting shouldn't stop you contributing, but it's worth knowing before you change jobs. If you're a month away from vesting, it can be worth thousands to wait.

What happens when you change jobs

Your 401(k) doesn't disappear when you leave. You'll usually have four options:

  1. Leave it in the old plan, if the balance is large enough for them to allow it.
  2. Roll it into your new employer's plan.
  3. Roll it into an IRA you open yourself, which often has more fund choices. (Where to open one.)
  4. Cash it out. This is the expensive one: income tax, usually a 10% penalty if you're under 59½, and the lost growth.

Ask for a direct rollover so the money goes straight from one account to the other and nothing is withheld.

Mistakes to avoid in year one

  • Contributing less than the match because the default was lower.
  • Never choosing an investment, so the money sits in cash.
  • Borrowing from the 401(k) for non-emergencies. A loan can come due quickly if you leave the job.
  • Checking the balance every week. Markets fall sometimes, and a young investor buying through a drop is buying cheaply. Check once a quarter at most.
  • Skipping the emergency fund. Without one, the first surprise bill tempts you to raid retirement money. (A $1,000 starter fund in 8 weeks.)

Common questions

Should I contribute to my 401(k) if I have student loans?

Usually yes, at least up to the match. The match is an instant return that's hard to beat. After that, compare: high-interest private loans often deserve extra payments before extra 401(k) money, while low-rate federal loans can usually be paid on schedule while you invest.

How much should I put in my 401(k) at my first job?

At least enough to get the full employer match. After that, a common long-term target is 10% to 15% of pay including the match, reached gradually with a 1% increase each year.

Is it better to do a Roth or traditional 401(k) when I'm young?

If you're in a low tax bracket (10% or 12%), Roth often makes sense because you pay tax at today's low rate. In higher brackets, traditional's tax break now is usually worth more. Splitting between the two is a reasonable middle ground.

What if my job doesn't offer a 401(k)?

Open an IRA yourself and set up an automatic monthly transfer. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500. (Roth or traditional IRA.)

Can I lose money in a 401(k)?

The account's value moves with the investments inside it, so yes, it can fall, sometimes a lot in a bad year. Over long periods, diversified stock funds have historically grown, but past results don't promise future ones. That's why short-term money belongs in savings, not in a 401(k).
