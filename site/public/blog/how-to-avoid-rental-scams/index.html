How to avoid rental scams: the warning signs and how to check a listing

How fake rental listings work, the red flags the FTC lists, how to check who owns a property, safe ways to pay, and what to do if you've been scammed.

Finding a place to rent is stressful enough without scams, but fake listings are common, especially in busy rental markets and in late summer when students and new workers are moving. Scammers copy real listings, change the contact details and lower the price, then ask for money before you've seen the place.

This guide explains how rental scams work, the warning signs, how to check a listing in about 20 minutes, and what to do if you've already paid.

How rental scams work

There are a few common versions:

  • Hijacked listings. A scammer copies photos and descriptions from a real listing (or a home that's for sale) and posts them with their own contact details and a lower rent.
  • Phantom rentals. The property doesn't exist, isn't for rent, or the address is wrong.
  • Application-fee scams. A "landlord" collects application fees from many people for a unit that's already rented or isn't theirs.
  • Identity theft. The goal is your personal information, not just money: your Social Security number, bank details or a copy of your ID.

The warning signs

The FTC lists several red flags. Any one is a reason to slow down; two or more is a reason to walk away.

  • Pressure to decide quickly. The FTC's advice: if anyone pressures you to make a decision quickly, walk away.
  • Unusual payment methods. Requests for wire transfers, gift cards or cryptocurrency. The FTC says these are the same as sending cash: once it's gone, it's very hard to get back.
  • No viewing. The "owner" is abroad, travelling or otherwise can't show you the property.
  • Pay before you see it. An application fee, deposit or first month's rent before you've seen the place or signed a lease.
  • Rent well below market. A price far under similar rentals nearby.
  • It's listed for sale. The FTC calls a home advertised for sale, rather than for rent, a big red flag.
  • Early requests for personal data. A landlord shouldn't need your Social Security number or credit score before you've agreed to rent.
  • Duplicate listings. The same address in other ads under a different name or company.

How to check a rental listing

1. Compare the rent with similar places

Look at three or four similar rentals nearby. If this one is far cheaper with no clear reason, treat it as suspicious.

2. Search the address

Search the full address with the owner's or company's name. The FTC suggests checking whether the same photos appear in other ads, especially under a different name or at a different price.

3. Check who owns the property

For private landlords, the FTC suggests checking city or county property tax records to see who owns the property, and comparing the owner's name with the ID of the person renting it to you.

4. Look up the company

For a management company, check its own website and search the name with "complaint," "review" or "scam."

5. Contact the owner yourself

Use contact details you find independently, such as the management company's official website, not details from the person who contacted you.

6. See it in person, or have someone you trust see it

Visit before paying anything, or ask a friend in the area to visit. For a long-distance move, a live video tour where they walk outside to show the street and building number is better than photos.

7. Get the lease before you pay

Read the lease, check that the landlord's name matches the owner, and pay only once you've signed.

A worked example: Yara's too-good listing

Yara (a fictional example) is moving for a new job and finds a one-bedroom apartment listed at $1,150 a month. Similar places nearby rent for $1,600 to $1,750. The "owner" says they're working abroad and can't show it, but will hold it if Yara sends a $250 application fee and a $1,150 deposit by payment app, plus her Social Security number on an application form.

Yara stops and checks:

CheckWhat she finds
RentAbout $500 below similar places
Address searchThe same photos in an older listing from a property management company, at $1,695
County tax recordsA different owner's name
Management company (number from its own website)The unit is real and available, but the "owner" has nothing to do with it

Yara reports the fake ad to the listing site and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. She loses $0. Without the checks, she'd have lost $1,400 and given her Social Security number to a stranger.

Safer ways to pay

  • Pay only after you've seen the place and signed a lease with someone you've confirmed is the owner or manager.
  • Use a traceable method such as a check, a cashier's check to the property company, or a secure payment portal run by a known management company.
  • Avoid wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency and payment apps for first payments to someone you haven't met. Payment apps are designed for people you know and may not protect you if you're scammed.
  • Get receipts for application fees, deposits and rent.

Protect your personal information

Share your Social Security number, bank details and ID only once you've confirmed the landlord and you're ready to sign. If a real landlord needs to run a credit or background check, they'll usually do it through a known screening service, often with your consent online. (How to check your credit report.)

If you've already paid a scammer

Act quickly:

  • Contact the company you used to pay: your bank, card issuer, payment app, wire service or gift card company. Tell them it was a scam and ask whether the payment can be reversed.
  • Report it to your local police, the website where the ad appeared, the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and your state attorney general.
  • If you shared personal information, go to IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan and consider a free credit freeze at all three bureaus. (What to do if your identity is stolen.)

Budgeting for a move

Scammers take advantage of people who are short on time and money. Planning ahead helps you avoid rushed decisions:

  • Save for move-in costs: the first month's rent, a security deposit, application fees, movers and utilities set-up. (Moving out for the first time.)
  • Start looking early, so a deadline doesn't push you into paying for a place you haven't seen.
  • Know what rent you can afford before you look. (How to make a budget you will keep.)

Checklist before you hand over money

Run through this list before paying any application fee, deposit or rent:

  • I've seen the property in person, or someone I trust has, or I've had a live video tour.
  • I've confirmed the owner or manager through property records or the company's official website.
  • The rent is in line with similar places nearby, or there's a clear reason it isn't.
  • I have a written lease or application terms with the landlord's or company's name.
  • I'm paying with a traceable method and I'll get a receipt.
  • Nobody is pushing me to decide today.

If you can't tick every box, wait. A real landlord with a real property can usually give you a day to check.

Students and long-distance movers

People moving from far away, including students and new graduates, are frequent targets, because they can't easily visit and are often on a deadline. If you're in that position, use your school's housing office or off-campus housing list where available, ask the new employer or a local contact for trusted landlords and agents, and plan to stay somewhere short-term for the first week or two so you can see places in person before signing.

Common questions

Is it normal to pay an application fee before seeing a rental?

It's safer not to. Real landlords and property managers usually let you see a unit, or confirm it's available, before you apply. Pay any fee only once you've confirmed who owns or manages the property, and get a receipt.

Can I rent an apartment without seeing it in person?

Many people do when moving from far away, but take extra care. Confirm the owner through property records, contact the management company through its official website, and ask for a live video tour or have someone you trust visit.

How can I tell if a landlord really owns the property?

Check the city or county property tax records for the owner's name and compare it with the ID of the person you're dealing with. For a management company, confirm the listing through the company's own website or office.

What should I do if a listing looks fake?

Don't send money or personal information. Report it to the website where it appeared and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and if you've found the real listing, let the real owner or company know.
