What to do if your identity is stolen: a step-by-step recovery plan

The signs of identity theft, the first steps to take today, how IdentityTheft.gov, credit freezes and fraud alerts work, and how to recover.

Finding out someone has used your name, Social Security number or accounts is stressful, and it can feel like there's too much to do at once. There's a clear order, though, and most of the important steps take less than an hour on the first day.

This guide covers the signs of identity theft, exactly what to do first, how the FTC's recovery tools work, and how to protect yourself afterwards.

Signs your identity may have been stolen

The FTC lists several warning signs:

  • Bills stop arriving. A missing statement can mean someone changed your billing address.
  • Charges or bills you don't recognise, for things you didn't buy.
  • Withdrawals from your bank account that you didn't make.
  • Accounts you don't recognise on your credit report.

Other signs include calls from debt collectors about debts that aren't yours, being turned down for credit unexpectedly, notices about a tax return you didn't file, or a medical bill for care you never received.

What to do if your identity is stolen

1. Contact the company where the fraud happened

Call the fraud department of the bank, card issuer or company involved. Explain that someone stole your identity, ask them to close or freeze the affected account, and ask for any documents about the fraudulent activity. Change passwords and PINs on that account.

2. Report it at IdentityTheft.gov

Go to IdentityTheft.gov, the FTC's site. The FTC says you'll get a free personal recovery plan with next steps, and pre-filled letters you can send to credit bureaus, businesses and debt collectors. If you'd rather report by phone, or in another language, call 877-438-4338.

3. Freeze your credit

Contact each of the three bureaus, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, to place a credit freeze. The FTC says that while a freeze is in place, nobody can open a new credit account in your name, and freezes are free to place and lift. (How to freeze your credit.)

4. Or place a fraud alert

A fraud alert asks businesses to verify your identity before opening a new account. You only need to contact one bureau; the FTC says it must tell the other two. An initial alert is free and lasts one year. Many people use both a freeze and an alert.

5. Check your credit reports

Get your reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com, free once a week. Look for accounts, inquiries and addresses you don't recognise. (How to check your credit report.)

6. Dispute fraudulent accounts and charges

Use the letters from your recovery plan to dispute each fraudulent account with the credit bureaus and the businesses involved. Keep copies of everything and note the dates.

7. Secure your accounts

Change passwords on your email, bank and other important accounts, starting with email (it's the key to resetting everything else). Turn on two-factor authentication and check that your recovery phone number and email are yours.

A worked example: Xan's missing statement

Xan (a fictional example) notices that a credit card statement hasn't arrived. A few days later, a letter arrives about a $2,300 laptop bought on a card Xan never opened.

Day 1 (about an hour):

  • Calls the card issuer's fraud department, reports the account as fraud and asks for it to be closed.
  • Reports the theft at IdentityTheft.gov and downloads the recovery plan and letters.
  • Places a credit freeze at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion.

Week 1:

  • Pulls all three credit reports and finds a second unknown account: a store card with a $640 balance.
  • Disputes both accounts with the bureaus and the companies, using the pre-filled letters.
  • Changes the email password, turns on two-step verification, and checks with the post office that no one has redirected Xan's mail.

Week 6:

  • Both fraudulent accounts are removed from Xan's reports. Xan pays $0 for either.

(Fictional example. How long recovery takes depends on the type of theft and how quickly companies respond.)

Special cases

Tax identity theft

If the IRS tells you a tax return has already been filed with your Social Security number, or you get a notice about income you didn't earn, report it at IdentityTheft.gov and follow the IRS instructions in the notice. Don't ignore IRS letters, but be wary of calls or texts claiming to be the IRS: the IRS usually contacts people by mail first.

Medical identity theft

If a bill or insurance statement shows care you didn't receive, contact the provider and your insurer, ask for your records, and report it at IdentityTheft.gov. Errors in medical records can affect your future care, so ask for corrections in writing.

Children's identities

Children can be targets because their credit isn't checked for years. Many parents freeze a child's credit as a precaution. Signs of a problem include pre-approved credit offers or collection calls in your child's name.

After a data breach

If a company tells you your information was in a breach, the right steps depend on what was exposed. Change the password for that account (and anywhere you reused it). If your Social Security number was exposed, a credit freeze is a strong step.

Prevent it happening again

The FTC suggests using strong passwords, turning on two-factor authentication, and not giving personal information to people who contact you by phone, email or text. A few more habits help:

  • Keep your credit frozen and lift it only when you apply for something.
  • Use a password manager so every account has its own password.
  • Turn on account alerts for large purchases, new payees and password changes.
  • Check one credit report every few months, rotating bureaus.
  • Shred documents with account numbers or your Social Security number.
  • Be careful with scam messages. Many identity thefts start with a convincing text or email. (How to spot a scam text.)

Should you pay for identity theft protection?

Paid services can monitor your credit, alert you to changes and help with recovery. Some include insurance for certain costs. But many of the most useful steps are free: credit freezes, fraud alerts, weekly credit reports and the FTC's recovery plan. If you're considering a paid service, compare what it adds to what you can do yourself. (Aura review.)

Keep a recovery log

Recovery often involves many calls and letters, and it's easy to lose track. Keep one document or notebook with:

  • every company you contacted, the date, the person's name and what they said;
  • copies of every letter you sent and received, with dates;
  • your IdentityTheft.gov report and recovery plan;
  • reference numbers for disputes, fraud claims and police reports;
  • the dates your credit freezes were placed and the PINs or logins to lift them.

If a problem comes back months later, or a debt collector contacts you about a fraudulent account, this record makes it much faster to resolve.

Watch for it coming back

After recovery, stay alert for a few months. Check your credit reports regularly, keep your freeze in place, and watch your statements for small test charges, which thieves sometimes make before larger ones. If something new appears, use your recovery log and IdentityTheft.gov report to deal with it quickly.

Common questions

How do I know if someone is using my Social Security number?

Common signs include accounts you don't recognise on your credit report, collection calls about debts that aren't yours, or IRS notices about income or tax returns you didn't file. Check your credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and report anything suspicious at IdentityTheft.gov.

Do I have to pay for charges made by an identity thief?

Generally, you're not responsible for fraudulent accounts opened in your name once you report and dispute them, and federal law limits your liability for unauthorised credit card charges. Report fraud quickly, especially for debit cards and bank accounts, where the time you take to report can affect how much you get back.

Should I file a police report?

IdentityTheft.gov will tell you whether a police report is useful in your situation. Some companies ask for one, and it can help if the thief used your identity during an arrest or for a large loan.

How long does it take to recover from identity theft?

It depends on what was stolen. Closing a single compromised card can take a day. Removing several fraudulent accounts from your credit reports can take weeks, and some cases take longer. The recovery plan from IdentityTheft.gov helps you track each step.
