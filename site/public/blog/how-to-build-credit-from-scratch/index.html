How to build credit from scratch: a 12-month plan from no score to a good one

How a credit score starts, the three safest first accounts, and a worked month-by-month plan from no credit history to a score lenders like.

Having **no credit** isn't the same as having bad credit, but it can feel like it. Without a credit history, you may be turned down for an apartment, asked for a bigger deposit on utilities, or offered a worse rate on a car loan. And it's a catch-22: you need credit to get credit.

The good news is that building credit from nothing is slow but simple. It doesn't need debt, interest or any risky products. This guide explains how a score gets started, the safest first accounts, and a worked 12-month plan.

How a credit score gets started

A credit score is calculated from your credit reports, which the three bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) build from what lenders report about your accounts. No accounts, no report; no report, no score.

The most widely used score, FICO, generally needs at least:

  • one account that has been open for six months or more, and
  • one account that has been reported to a bureau in the last six months.

So the clock starts when your first account is opened and reported. That's why the first step matters more than anything that comes after: the sooner you open one good account, the sooner you'll have a score.

What goes into the score

FICO groups the factors like this:

FactorApproximate weightWhat it means for a beginner
Payment history35%Pay every bill on time, every time
Amounts owed30%Use a small share of your credit limits
Length of credit history15%Open accounts early and keep them open
New credit10%Don't apply for lots of accounts at once
Credit mix10%Matters least; don't borrow just to add variety

Two habits cover most of it: pay on time and keep balances low. (How credit scores work.)

The safest first accounts

Secured credit card

You put down a refundable deposit (often a few hundred dollars), which usually becomes your credit limit. It works and reports like any credit card. Look for one with no annual fee that reports to all three bureaus and reviews accounts for an upgrade to an unsecured card. (Credit-builder cards compared.)

Becoming an authorized user

A parent, partner or relative with a long, clean card history adds you to their account. Many issuers report the account on your credit report too, which can give you a history immediately. You don't need to use the card at all. The risk is theirs, not yours, but their late payments or high balance could affect you, so only do this with someone who pays in full.

Credit-builder loan

A lender puts a small loan into a locked savings account; you make monthly payments, and you get the money at the end. It builds payment history, but there's usually interest or fees, so it costs a little to use. It can make sense if you can't get a secured card.

What to avoid: store cards with high rates pushed at the checkout, "credit repair" services that promise a score, and any product that charges large upfront fees.

The 12-month plan

1. Month 1: open one account

A secured card or authorized user status (or both). Don't apply for several cards in the same month; each application can add a hard inquiry.

2. Month 1: set it up so it runs itself

Put one small, regular bill on the card, like a streaming subscription or phone bill, and set autopay for the full statement balance. You pay no interest and build a perfect payment history without thinking about it.

3. Months 1 to 6: keep the reported balance low

Keep the balance under about 10% of the limit when the statement closes. On a $300 limit, that's $30 or less.

4. Month 6: check your reports and score

Get your free reports at AnnualCreditReport.com and check that the account shows up correctly. Many banks and card apps show a free score once you have one. (How to dispute a report error.)

5. Months 7 to 12: ask about an upgrade

After 6 to 12 months of on-time payments, many issuers will consider moving a secured card to an unsecured one and returning your deposit. Ask if they don't offer.

6. Month 12: consider a second card, carefully

A second no-annual-fee card can raise your total limit, which helps your utilization, but only add one if you'll treat it exactly like the first. Keep the first card open: its age helps your score.

A worked example: Nico's first year

Nico (a fictional example) is 20, has a part-time job and no credit history. An apartment application asked for a co-signer because Nico had no score.

WhenWhat Nico doesWhat it builds
Month 1Opens a secured card with a $300 deposit. Puts a $25 music subscription on it with autopay for the full balance.First account, reporting monthly
Month 1A parent with a 15-year, always-on-time card adds Nico as an authorized user. Nico never uses the card.Older account history on Nico's report
Months 2 to 6Does nothing new. The $25 is paid in full every month.Six months of on-time payments, utilization about 8%
Month 6Pulls all three free reports: both accounts appear. Nico's banking app now shows a score.A score exists
Month 10Card issuer upgrades the secured card to an unsecured one and returns the $300.Deposit back, same account age
Month 12Signs the next lease without a co-signer.

Nico never paid a cent of interest, never carried debt and spent nothing extra apart from the subscription Nico was already paying for. The score didn't come from borrowing; it came from time and on-time payments, which is the whole method.

(Score levels vary by person and scoring model, and nobody can promise a particular number. But a clean first year like Nico's is how most good scores begin.)

Building credit without a credit card

If you'd rather not have a card at all:

  • A credit-builder loan from a credit union or online lender builds payment history.
  • Rent reporting. Some landlords and services report on-time rent to one or more bureaus. Some scoring models use it; others don't. Check whether there's a fee and which bureaus receive it.
  • Authorized user status on a trusted person's card, without using the card.

If you're starting again, not starting fresh

Some people have a thin file because of a past problem, not because they're new: an old collection, a charged-off card, or years without any open accounts. The same plan works, with two additions. First, check your reports for errors and old negative items that should have dropped off; most negative information leaves after seven years. Second, expect the early score to be lower, and give the new account more time before applying for anything else. A secured card with a clean record still starts rebuilding from the first on-time payment, and recent good history counts for more than old bad history as the years pass.

Mistakes that set you back

  • A late payment. A payment 30 or more days late can stay on your reports for up to seven years. Autopay for at least the minimum is the safety net.
  • Maxing out the card. High utilization lowers scores, even if you pay in full later. Pay before the statement date if you've spent a lot.
  • Closing your first card. It shortens your credit history. If it has no annual fee, keep it open and use it lightly.
  • Applying for many cards at once. Several hard inquiries in a short time can lower a new score and make lenders wary.
  • Paying for a "credit score boost". Nothing legal can replace time and on-time payments.

Common questions

How long does it take to build credit from nothing?

FICO generally needs at least one account open for six months before it can produce a score. Building to a good score usually takes a year or more of on-time payments and low balances, and it keeps improving as your accounts age.

Can I build credit without a credit card?

Yes. Credit-builder loans, being an authorized user on someone else's card, and in some cases rent reporting can all add positive history. A secured card is often the simplest option, but it isn't the only one.

Does checking my own credit hurt my score?

No. Checking your own reports or score is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect your score. Only applications for new credit, which cause hard inquiries, can lower it slightly.

Should I carry a balance to build credit?

No. You don't need to pay interest to build credit. Paying the full statement balance every month builds the same payment history, and keeping your balance low helps your score.
