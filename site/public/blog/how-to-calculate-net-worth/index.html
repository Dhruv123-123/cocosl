How to calculate your net worth (and why a negative number is fine at 27)

Net worth is what you own minus what you owe. What to count, what to leave out, a worked example that starts below zero, and what's normal by age.

"Net worth" is one of the most-searched money terms in the country, and it's easy to see why. It's the one number that sums up your whole financial life: every account, every debt, every bit of progress or backsliding, in a single figure.

It's also simple to work out. You need about 20 minutes, your account logins and a notes app. This guide explains what to count, what to leave out, and how to read the result, with a worked example that starts below zero.

The formula

Net worth = what you own − what you owe.

That's it. What you own are your assets: cash, savings, investments, retirement accounts and things you could sell for real money. What you owe are your liabilities: student loans, car loans, credit card balances, a mortgage, money owed to family.

If you own more than you owe, your net worth is positive. If you owe more, it's negative, which is extremely common in your twenties, especially with student loans. A negative number isn't a verdict. It's a starting line.

What to count, and what to leave out

The rules that keep the number honest:

Count these assets:

  • Checking and savings balances, on the day you do the sum.
  • Investment accounts and retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, HSA) at today's balance.
  • Your car, at what it would sell for today, not what you paid. A private-sale estimate from a pricing guide is fine.
  • Your home, if you own one, at a realistic sale price.
  • Money someone genuinely owes you and will pay back.

Leave these out:

  • Everyday possessions: furniture, clothes, your laptop and phone. They'd sell for a fraction of their cost, and counting them just inflates the number.
  • Future income, a bonus you haven't received, or an inheritance you might get.
  • Employer match that hasn't vested yet, if you'd lose it by leaving. (Vesting, explained.)

Count every debt at its current balance:

  • Student loans (federal and private), car loan, credit cards (the balance today, even if you pay it in full each month), personal loans, buy-now-pay-later plans, medical bills, and any informal loans.

A simple test for any item: could I turn this into cash, or would I have to pay this, within a few months? If yes, it goes in.

Calculate yours in 20 minutes

1. List every account you have

Open your banking, investment and loan apps and write down each account's name and current balance. Your credit report lists every loan and card in your name, which catches anything you've forgotten. (Check it free.)

2. Add the value of anything big you own

Your car at its private-sale value, and a home if you have one. Be conservative: use what it would actually sell for this month.

3. Total the assets and the debts

Two columns, two totals.

4. Subtract

Assets minus debts. Write down the number and the date.

5. Put a reminder in your calendar

Same day next year (or every three months, if you like watching progress). Use the same rules each time so the numbers compare fairly.

A worked example: Jordan, 27

Jordan (a fictional example) is 27, three years into a job, with student loans and a car loan.

OwnsTodayItemOwesToday
Checking$1,850Student loans$21,800
High-yield savings$4,200Car loan$8,900
401(k)$9,600Credit card$1,350
Roth IRA$3,100
Car (private-sale value)$11,500
Total$30,250Total$32,050

Net worth: $30,250 − $32,050 = −$1,800.

That stings for about five seconds. Then it becomes useful, because the table shows exactly where the number can move: the card balance, the savings rate, the loans.

Twelve months later

Jordan keeps contributing to the 401(k), adds $250 a month to the Roth IRA, pays the card off and keeps it at zero, and sends an extra $100 a month to the car loan. The next January:

OwnsA year laterItemOwesA year later
Checking$2,000Student loans$19,300
High-yield savings$7,800Car loan$5,700
401(k)$15,400Credit card$0
Roth IRA$6,200
Car$10,000
Total$41,400Total$25,000

Net worth: $41,400 − $25,000 = $16,400. That's a swing of $18,200 in one year.

Where it came from is the interesting part. The car lost $1,500 in value, and the investment balances include some market growth that could just as easily have been a fall. But most of the change is Jordan's own choices: debt paid down ($7,050 less owed) and savings and contributions added. That's why net worth is a better scorecard than any single account: it counts paying off a card exactly the same as saving the same amount.

Three tricky cases

Health savings account (HSA). Count it as an asset. It's your money, it can be invested, and it stays yours if you change jobs. A flexible spending account (FSA) usually isn't worth counting, because unspent money can be lost at the end of the plan year.

Crypto and individual stocks. Count them at today's value, but expect the number to swing. If they're a big share of your net worth, the yearly snapshot will move with the market more than with your habits, which is worth knowing when you read the trend.

Buy now, pay later. Easy to forget, because there's no statement in the mail. Every remaining installment is a debt. Open each app and add up what's left to pay.

What's a "normal" net worth?

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances is the best public source. In its 2022 survey, the median net worth was $39,000 for families whose head was under 35, and $135,600 for ages 35 to 44 (in 2022 dollars).

Two things to keep in mind:

  • The median is the middle household, so half had less. Averages you'll see quoted online are much higher, because a small number of very wealthy households pull them up. Ignore the averages.
  • Those figures include home equity, which is a big share of net worth for homeowners. A 27-year-old renter with student loans isn't behind by having less; they're at a different stage.

The comparison that actually matters is with your own number from last year.

Net worth vs income

A high salary and a high net worth aren't the same thing. Someone earning $120,000 who spends all of it, with a new car loan and card balances, can have a lower net worth than someone earning $50,000 who saves 15% and drives a paid-off car. Income is what comes in; net worth is what you've kept. That's why the number is such a useful check on a raise: if your income went up and your net worth didn't move faster, the raise went into spending.

How to grow it (without obsessing over it)

Net worth goes up in only three ways: you owe less, you save more, or what you own grows. In practice, that means:

  1. Pay off high-interest debt first. Every dollar of card debt cleared adds a dollar to net worth and stops the interest. (Avalanche vs snowball.)
  2. Automate saving and investing so it happens without a decision each month. (Start with $100 a month.)
  3. Get the full 401(k) match. It's the fastest legal way to add money you didn't earn in salary.
  4. Be careful with things that lose value. A new car financed over six years can drag net worth down for most of that time.

And keep perspective. Net worth is a once-a-year (or once-a-quarter) check-in, not a daily score. Markets move, cars depreciate, and the number can dip in a year when you did everything right. The trend over several years is what tells you whether your plan is working.

Common questions

Is it bad to have a negative net worth?

It's common, especially in your twenties with student loans. What matters is the direction. If the number rises each year, your plan is working.

Should I include my car and my house in net worth?

Yes, at what they'd sell for today, and include the loans against them too. Leave out everyday possessions like furniture and electronics; they'd sell for very little.

Do I count my 401(k) before or after tax?

Most people count the balance as shown, which keeps it simple and consistent. Just remember that money in a traditional 401(k) will be taxed when you withdraw it, so it's worth a little less than the same balance in a Roth or a savings account.

How often is it worth updating the number?

Once a year is enough for most people, on the same date each year. Every three months works if you find the progress motivating. More often than that just tracks market noise.
