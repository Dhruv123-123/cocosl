How to lower your utility bills: the changes that save the most, including for renters

Thermostat settings, water heating, standby power, LEDs, rate plans and help programmes, ranked by effort, with a worked example of a summer bill.

Utility bills are easy to ignore because they arrive automatically and feel fixed. They aren't. A few changes to settings and habits can trim them noticeably, and most cost little or nothing. Many work for renters too.

This guide ranks the changes by effort, explains what the U.S. Department of Energy says about each, and walks through a worked example of a summer electric bill.

Start with your bills

Before changing anything, look at the last 12 months of bills (most utilities show this online):

  • Which months are highest? Summer peaks usually mean cooling; winter peaks mean heating.
  • Which utility costs most? Electricity, gas, water or internet.
  • What plan are you on? Some utilities offer time-of-use or other rate plans.

This tells you where to focus. There's little point in obsessing over lights if three-quarters of the bill is heating.

Free changes (today)

Set the thermostat back when you're out or asleep

The Department of Energy says you can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling by turning your thermostat back 7°–10°F for 8 hours a day: down in fall and winter, up in spring and summer. A programmable or smart thermostat makes it automatic. (DOE notes that heat pumps need a thermostat designed for them before using setbacks.)

Turn the water heater to 120°F

DOE recommends 120°F, which it describes as safe for most people. Many water heaters ship set higher. If you rent, ask your landlord first.

Cut standby power

Many electronics draw power even when switched off. DOE says this can add 5% to 10% to a monthly utility bill. Unplug chargers when not in use and switch off game consoles, TVs and computer setups at the wall or with a power strip.

Use appliances smartly

Run the dishwasher and washing machine with full loads, wash clothes in cold water, and air-dry when you can. Close blinds on hot afternoons and open them on sunny winter days.

Cheap changes (under $50)

  • LED bulbs. DOE says an ENERGY STAR LED uses up to 90% less energy than an old incandescent bulb and can save about $55 over its life. Replace the bulbs you use most first.
  • Advanced power strips that cut power to idle devices.
  • Weatherstripping and draft stoppers for doors and windows.
  • Low-flow showerheads, which save water and the energy to heat it.
  • A programmable thermostat, if you're allowed to install one.

Call your providers (one hour)

  • Ask about rate plans. Some utilities offer time-of-use plans with cheaper off-peak hours. They suit households that can shift laundry and dishwashing to evenings or weekends.
  • Check budget billing. It doesn't lower your annual cost, but it spreads it evenly so winter or summer spikes don't wreck your budget.
  • Negotiate internet and phone. These aren't regulated utilities, so prices are often negotiable, especially when a promotional rate ends. (Find and cancel subscriptions.)
  • Ask about assistance and discounts. Many utilities have reduced rates, payment plans or weatherisation programmes for lower-income households.

Help with energy bills

The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) is a federally funded programme that helps low-income households pay home energy bills, run through states and local agencies. If paying for heating or cooling is a struggle, it's worth checking whether you qualify. (How to budget on a low income.)

A worked example: Nell's summer bill

Nell (a fictional example) rents a one-bedroom apartment where the summer electric bill averages about $160 a month, more than half of it air conditioning. Nell makes five changes:

That's roughly 21% off the summer bill, and the $55 spent pays for itself in under two months. Over four summer months, Nell saves about $136, and the LEDs and power strip keep saving all year.

Renters: what you can and can't change

You probably can't replace windows, add insulation or swap the furnace. But renters can:

  • adjust thermostats and water heater settings (with permission where needed);
  • use LEDs, power strips, draft stoppers and window coverings;
  • choose energy-efficient habits and rate plans if the account is in your name;
  • ask the landlord to fix leaks, drafts and broken seals, which also protects their property.

When choosing a new place, ask what past tenants paid for utilities, especially in older buildings. (Moving out for the first time.)

Water bills

Fix dripping taps and running toilets quickly: small leaks waste a lot over a month. Shorter showers, full loads and low-flow fixtures cut both water and the energy to heat it. Watering a lawn early in the morning or evening loses less to evaporation.

Track the results

Note your usage (kilowatt-hours, therms or gallons), not just the dollar amount, because rates change. Compare the same month year over year to see whether your changes are working. Put the savings toward a goal, like your emergency fund, so they don't just disappear. (Sinking funds, explained.)

Heating and cooling: the biggest lever

Because heating and cooling are usually the largest part of a home energy bill, small changes there tend to matter more than anything else.

  • Change or clean filters regularly. A clogged filter makes a furnace or air conditioner work harder. Check it monthly during heavy use. Renters can usually do this themselves or ask the landlord.
  • Keep vents clear. Furniture, rugs and curtains blocking vents waste the air you're paying for.
  • Use ceiling fans to feel cooler. A fan cools people, not rooms, so turn it off when you leave. In summer it can let you set the thermostat a little higher. The DOE's renter tips also suggest switching the fan direction by season.
  • Block the sun in summer, let it in during winter. Blinds, curtains and reflective window film on south- and west-facing windows make a noticeable difference in hot afternoons.
  • Close off rooms you don't use, where your system allows it, rather than heating or cooling them all day.

A one-hour bill review

Once a year, set aside an hour to go through every recurring household bill:

  1. Energy: compare this year's usage with last year's for the same months. Ask your utility about rate plans, budget billing and assistance.
  2. Water: look for a jump that might mean a leak. Check toilets by adding a few drops of food colouring to the tank: colour in the bowl without flushing means a leak.
  3. Internet: check your speed tier against what you actually use, and whether a promotional rate is about to end.
  4. Phone: look at how much data you really use. A smaller or prepaid plan may cost less.
  5. Insurance on the home or renters policy: ask whether bundling or a higher deductible lowers the premium. (How to save money on car insurance.)

Write down what you changed and the new monthly amounts, so next year's review starts from a clear baseline.

Don't let savings become discomfort

Cutting bills shouldn't mean living in a cold or overheated home. Extreme indoor temperatures can be a health risk, especially for babies, older people and anyone with a medical condition. Make the free changes first, keep rooms at a safe temperature, and if bills are still more than you can pay, ask your utility about payment plans and assistance before falling behind.

Common questions

What uses the most electricity in a home?

Heating and cooling are usually the biggest users, followed by water heating. Appliances, electronics and lighting make up the rest. Your own bills and your utility's usage tools will show where your money goes.

Does turning the thermostat down at night really save money?

Yes. The Department of Energy says turning it back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling. Heat pump owners should use a thermostat designed for heat pumps.

Is budget billing a good idea?

It doesn't lower your total cost, but it smooths payments across the year, which makes budgeting easier. Check how your utility settles any difference at the end of the year.

Can I lower my bills if I rent?

Yes. Thermostat settings, LEDs, power strips, draft stoppers, window coverings and appliance habits all help, and you can ask your landlord to fix leaks and drafts. Rate plans may also be an option if the account is in your name.
