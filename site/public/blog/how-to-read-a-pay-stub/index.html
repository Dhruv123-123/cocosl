How to read a pay stub: every line explained, and the mistakes worth checking

What each line on a pay stub means, from gross pay and FICA to pre-tax deductions and YTD, plus a worked example and the errors worth checking.

Your pay stub (or "earnings statement") is one of the most useful money documents you get, and one of the least read. It shows exactly where your salary goes before it reaches your bank account: taxes, insurance, retirement savings and anything else. Reading it once properly helps you build a realistic budget, catch payroll mistakes, and see whether your tax withholding is on track.

This guide walks through each part of a typical stub, then a worked example with the numbers, and finally the errors worth checking every time.

The four parts of a pay stub

Layouts differ by employer and payroll provider, but most stubs have the same building blocks.

1. The header

Your name, employee ID, the pay period (the dates you're being paid for), the pay date, and sometimes your filing status and allowances from your W-4. Check the pay period first: your first paycheck sometimes covers only part of a period.

2. Earnings

  • Gross pay: everything you earned this period before anything comes out, including regular pay, overtime, bonuses, commissions and tips reported through payroll.
  • Hours and rate: for hourly workers, the hours and the rate for each type of pay.

3. Deductions

This is the long part. Deductions come in three types.

Taxes

  • Federal income tax: withheld based on your pay and your W-4.
  • Social Security (often "OASDI" or "FICA SS"): generally 6.2% of your wages, up to a yearly wage cap.
  • Medicare (often "FICA Med"): generally 1.45% of your wages, with an extra 0.9% on high earnings.
  • State and local income tax: depending on where you live and work. Some states have none.
  • Other state programmes: some states withhold for disability or paid family leave.

Pre-tax deductions (taken out before income tax is calculated)

  • 401(k) or 403(b) contributions (traditional, not Roth)
  • Health, dental and vision insurance premiums paid through a workplace plan
  • HSA or FSA contributions (HSA vs FSA)
  • Commuter benefits, in some workplaces

Post-tax deductions (taken out after tax)

  • Roth 401(k) contributions
  • Some insurance, such as certain life or disability policies
  • Wage garnishments, union dues, charitable payroll giving

4. Net pay and year-to-date

  • Net pay is what lands in your account: gross pay minus every deduction.
  • YTD (year-to-date) columns show running totals for the year. They're useful for checking your 401(k) progress and your tax withholding, and your last stub of the year should match your W-2.

Why pre-tax deductions matter

Pre-tax deductions lower the income you're taxed on, so they cost you less than their face value. If you contribute $100 to a traditional 401(k) and you're in the 12% federal bracket, your federal income tax falls by about $12, so your take-home pay falls by about $88, not $100.

Note one detail: traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your income for income tax, but Social Security and Medicare are still calculated on them. Health insurance premiums paid through a workplace "cafeteria plan" usually reduce both.

A worked example: Dev's paycheck

Dev (a fictional example) earns $57,200 a year, paid every two weeks, so each paycheck starts at $2,200 gross. Dev contributes 5% to a traditional 401(k) and pays $65 per paycheck for health insurance.

LineAmountNotes
Gross pay$2,200.00$57,200 ÷ 26 paychecks
401(k), 5% (pre-tax)−$110.00Lowers income tax, not FICA
Health insurance (pre-tax)−$65.00Lowers income tax and FICA
Social Security, 6.2%−$132.37On $2,135 ($2,200 − $65)
Medicare, 1.45%−$30.96On $2,135
Federal income tax−$170.00Depends on the W-4
State income tax−$70.00Depends on the state
Net pay$1,621.67What reaches Dev's account

So Dev's take-home pay is about 74% of gross, and $110 of the "missing" money is Dev's own retirement savings, not a cost.

Two things Dev checks:

  1. Is the 401(k) percentage right? 5% of $2,200 is $110. ✓ If Dev's employer matches up to 5%, the match should show somewhere, often on the stub or in the 401(k) account.
  2. Is withholding on track? Dev runs the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator once a year using this stub and adjusts the W-4 if it shows a big refund or a bill coming. (How to fill out a W-4.)

(Fictional example. Income tax withholding varies with your W-4, state and other income; the federal and state amounts here are illustrative.)

How to check your pay stub

1. Check the pay period and pay rate

Make sure the dates are right and your salary or hourly rate matches your offer letter or last raise.

2. Check the hours

For hourly work, compare the hours with your own record, including overtime. Overtime should be paid at the correct rate.

3. Check your benefit deductions

Compare insurance, 401(k) and HSA amounts with what you chose at enrolment. A wrong plan or missing deduction is easy to fix early and painful to fix at year-end.

4. Check your tax details

Make sure your state is right (especially if you moved or work remotely) and that federal withholding looks reasonable. A sudden change can mean your W-4 was entered incorrectly.

5. Check the YTD totals now and then

Every few months, glance at YTD gross and YTD taxes. They should grow steadily. Big jumps or drops are worth asking about.

6. Save your stubs

Keep them, digitally or on paper, at least until you've checked them against your W-2. Landlords and lenders often ask for recent stubs too.

Where to find your stub

Many employers no longer print pay stubs. Look in your payroll or HR portal, often under "pay", "earnings statements" or "documents". If you can't find it, ask HR. Download each one as a PDF so you still have them if you leave the job and lose access to the portal.

Mistakes worth catching

  • Wrong pay rate after a raise or job change.
  • Missing overtime or hours.
  • Benefits you didn't choose, or the wrong coverage level.
  • 401(k) not starting after you enrolled, so you miss out on the match.
  • The wrong state withholding tax after a move.
  • Social Security still being withheld after you've passed the year's wage cap, if you have only one employer (if you had two employers, the extra comes back as a credit on your tax return).

If you find an error, raise it with HR or payroll in writing, with the stub attached. Most mistakes are corrected on the next paycheck.

Bonuses, overtime and odd paychecks

Some paychecks look very different from the usual one:

  • Bonuses are often withheld at a flat federal rate (commonly 22% for supplemental wages up to $1 million), which can be higher or lower than your normal rate. The actual tax is settled when you file your return.
  • Overtime shows as a separate earnings line at its own rate. Check the hours and the rate.
  • The first and last paychecks of a job are often partial, and the last may include payment for unused paid time off, depending on your employer and state.
  • Three-paycheck months happen twice a year if you're paid every two weeks, and deductions may be taken from all three.

If one paycheck looks strange, compare the earnings lines first. Most differences are explained there.

Direct deposit and splitting your pay

Many employers let you split your direct deposit between accounts. Sending a fixed amount straight to a savings account means you save before you can spend, which is one of the easiest habits to keep. The CFPB's pay stub worksheet suggests the same idea. (How to build an emergency fund.)

Using your stub to budget

Budget from net pay, not salary. If you're paid every two weeks, remember that two months a year have three paychecks: many people budget for two a month and send the third to savings or debt. (How to make a budget you'll keep.)

Common questions

Why is my first paycheck smaller than I expected?

Common reasons are a partial pay period, benefits deductions starting, a higher-than-expected tax withholding, or the gap between gross salary and take-home pay. Check the pay period dates and each deduction against what you chose at enrolment.

What does YTD mean on a pay stub?

Year-to-date: the running total of your earnings, taxes and deductions since January 1 (or since you started the job). Your final YTD figures for the year should match your W-2.

What is OASDI on my pay stub?

OASDI stands for Old-Age, Survivors and Disability Insurance, the official name for Social Security. It's generally 6.2% of your wages up to the yearly wage cap.

Can my employer take money out of my paycheck without asking?

Taxes and court-ordered garnishments are taken without your consent. Most other deductions, like insurance and retirement contributions, need your agreement. If you see one you don't recognise, ask payroll.
