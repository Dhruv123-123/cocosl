Roth or Traditional IRA? It mostly comes down to one question

Pay the tax now or later? How Roth and Traditional IRAs differ, why many people early in their career lean Roth, what you can withdraw, and how to open one.

Roth vs Traditional IRA sounds complicated. It mostly comes down to one question: do you pay the tax now, or later?

An IRA (individual retirement account) is an account you open yourself, separate from any workplace plan. Money inside it can be invested, usually in low-cost index funds, and grows without yearly tax on the gains. The two main types differ in when the tax happens.

The short version

Traditional IRARoth IRA
Tax on money going inMay be tax-deductible nowNo deduction; you contribute after-tax money
Tax on money coming out in retirementTaxed as incomeTax-free, if the rules are met
Withdraw your contributions earlyGenerally taxed, often plus a 10% penaltyContributions can come out any time, tax- and penalty-free
Income limits to contributeNone to contribute (deduction can be limited)Yes: eligibility phases out at higher incomes
Required withdrawals in your 70sYesNo, for the original owner

The one question: is your tax rate higher now or later?

  • If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than today, paying tax now (Roth) tends to win.
  • If you expect a lower bracket later, taking the deduction now (Traditional) tends to win.

Nobody knows future tax rates, but many people in their 20s are earning less now than they will later in their career, which is why Roth is a common choice early on. If you're in a high bracket today, the Traditional deduction gets more attractive.

Not sure? Plenty of people split the difference over time: Roth while income is lower, Traditional (or a pre-tax workplace plan) as it rises.

Roth money isn't locked away (mostly)

The biggest myth about Roth IRAs is that the money is stuck until you're 60. With a Roth IRA, your contributions (the dollars you put in) can come back out at any time without tax or penalty.

The growth is different. Withdraw earnings early and you can owe income tax plus a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies. Earnings come out tax-free once you're 59½ and at least five years have passed since your first Roth IRA contribution.

Example: you put in $3,000 and it grows to $3,400. You could take back the $3,000. The $400 should stay.

That makes starting less scary, but it still shouldn't double as your emergency fund. Money you pull out can't go back in beyond the yearly limit, so the tax-free growth on it is gone for good.

The rules to check before you open one

  • You need earned income (wages, self-employment) at least equal to what you contribute. A spouse without income may be able to use a spousal IRA.
  • Yearly limit: for 2026 the IRS limit is $7,500 if you're under 50, across all your IRAs combined (higher with the catch-up contribution if you're 50 or older). Check the IRS contribution limits page for the current figure.
  • Roth income limits: above a certain modified adjusted gross income, the amount you can put into a Roth shrinks, then disappears. The thresholds change yearly; the IRS publishes them.
  • Deadline: you can usually contribute for a tax year up until that year's filing deadline (typically mid-April of the next year).

How to open one (about 20 minutes)

  1. Pick a brokerage with no account fees and no minimums, offering low-cost index funds. Most large brokerages qualify.
  2. Open "Roth IRA" or "Traditional IRA" (not a regular taxable brokerage account, unless that's what you want).
  3. Link your bank and set an automatic monthly contribution, even $50.
  4. Actually invest the money. Deposits often land as cash. Choose a fund, such as a broad index fund or a target-date fund, and set the contributions to buy it automatically. We walk through every screen in how to start investing with $100 a month.

Before an IRA: the 401(k) match

If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contributing enough to get the full match usually comes before an IRA, because the match is an immediate return on your money. See the raise you're ignoring: your 401(k) match.

Common questions

Can I have both a Roth and a Traditional IRA?

Yes, but the yearly limit is shared across both.

Can I contribute to an IRA and a 401(k)?

Yes. Workplace plans have their own separate limit. If you're covered by a workplace plan, your Traditional IRA deduction may be limited at higher incomes.

What should I invest in inside the IRA?

An IRA is just the account; you choose the investments. Many beginners use a single target-date fund or a broad, low-cost index fund. Check the expense ratio before you buy.

Is a Roth IRA worth it with small amounts?

Yes. Small, regular contributions get the most time to grow tax-free. $100 a month is a fine start.
