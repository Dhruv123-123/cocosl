The earned income tax credit: who qualifies, how much it's worth, and how to claim it

The 2026 EITC amounts and income limits, who qualifies with and without children, and a worked example of a single parent's refund.

The **earned income tax credit (EITC)** is one of the largest sources of help for working families in the US, and one of the most often missed. The IRS estimates that about one in five eligible workers doesn't claim it, often because they don't file a return, think they earn too little to bother, or don't know they qualify.

It's a refundable credit: if it's bigger than the tax you owe, the IRS pays you the difference. For 2026 returns, filed in 2027, it's worth up to $8,231 for a family with three or more qualifying children, and up to $664 for a worker with no children.

This guide covers who qualifies, how much it's worth, how to claim it, and a worked example.

How much it's worth for 2026

The credit rises with your earnings up to a maximum, stays flat for a range, then phases out as income rises. For 2026, for single filers and heads of household:

Two$7,316$23,890$58,629

The phase-out ranges are higher for married couples filing jointly. The income used is your earned income or your AGI, whichever gives the smaller credit. You also can't have more than $12,200 of investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains) in 2026.

Who qualifies

To claim the EITC, you generally need to:

  • Have earned income from a job or self-employment. Unemployment benefits, Social Security and most other benefits don't count as earned income.
  • Have a valid Social Security number (you, your spouse if filing jointly, and any qualifying children you claim), issued by the due date of the return.
  • Be a US citizen or resident alien for the whole year.
  • Not file as married filing separately, with limited exceptions for separated spouses.
  • Be within the income and investment income limits above.

Without a qualifying child, you also need to be at least 25 and under 65 at the end of the year, have lived in the US for more than half the year, and not be claimed as someone else's dependent.

What makes a child a qualifying child

A child counts for the EITC if they meet all of these:

  • Relationship: your son, daughter, stepchild, foster child, sibling, half-sibling or step-sibling, or a descendant of any of them (a grandchild, niece or nephew).
  • Age: under 19 at the end of the year, or under 24 if a full-time student for at least five months of the year, or any age if permanently and totally disabled. The child must also be younger than you (or your spouse, if filing jointly), unless disabled.
  • Residency: lived with you in the US for more than half the year.
  • Joint return: isn't filing a joint return with someone else (with a narrow exception).

Only one person can claim a child for the EITC. If a child lives with more than one adult who could claim them, IRS tie-breaker rules decide who can.

A worked example: Mia's refund

Mia (a fictional example) is a single parent with a six-year-old. In 2026 Mia worked as a pharmacy technician and earned $21,000, and $900 of federal income tax was withheld from paychecks. Mia files as head of household.

Income tax: the head of household standard deduction for 2026 is $24,150, which is more than Mia's income. Mia's taxable income is zero, so Mia owes no federal income tax.

Earned income tax credit: with one qualifying child, the maximum is $4,427. Mia's $21,000 is below the $23,890 point where the credit starts to phase out, so Mia gets the full $4,427.

Child tax credit: up to $2,200 per qualifying child for 2026, of which up to $1,700 is refundable. Because Mia owes no income tax, only the refundable part helps: $1,700.

Federal income tax owed$0
Tax withheld from paychecks$900
Earned income tax credit (refundable)$4,427
Refundable child tax credit$1,700
Federal refundabout $7,027

That's a third of Mia's yearly pay. And none of it would arrive if Mia didn't file: Mia's income is below the standard deduction, so Mia isn't required to file. That's exactly the situation in which people miss the EITC.

Mia uses a free VITA site to file, chooses direct deposit, and decides in advance what the refund is for: a $1,000 emergency fund first, then a car repair fund and an overdue dental bill. (What to do with your tax refund.)

How to claim it

  1. File a federal return, even if you're not required to. That's the only way to claim the credit.
  2. Answer the questions about qualifying children in your tax software, or attach Schedule EIC if you're filing on paper with children.
  3. Use free help if you can. IRS Free File and Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) sites prepare returns for free for people who are likely to qualify, and VITA volunteers are trained on the EITC rules. (Filing your first return.)
  4. Keep records showing that your children lived with you, like school or medical records, in case the IRS asks.

When the refund arrives

By law, the IRS can't issue refunds that include the EITC or the refundable child tax credit before mid-February, so it has time to check for fraud. The IRS says most of these refunds for people who file early, choose direct deposit and have no other issues arrive by early March. You can track yours with "Where's My Refund?".

Other credits to check at the same time

If you qualify for the EITC, look at these too: the child tax credit (partly refundable, as in Mia's example), the child and dependent care credit if you paid for childcare so you could work, the Saver's Credit if you contributed to a retirement account, and education credits if you or a dependent paid college tuition. Tax software and VITA volunteers check these together. (Tax deductions vs tax credits.)

Mistakes to avoid

  • Not filing because you earn too little. You can't get the credit without a return.
  • Claiming a child who doesn't qualify, often because of the residency test, or because someone else in the family is also claiming them. Agree within the family who claims each child.
  • Including the wrong income. Report all earned income, including cash and self-employment income. Leaving some out can reduce or disqualify the credit.
  • Paying for an expensive refund advance. Some preparers offer loans against your refund. Read the fees, and remember that most e-filed returns with direct deposit are refunded within a few weeks.

If the IRS denies your EITC because of an error that wasn't due to reckless or intentional disregard of the rules, you can usually claim it again in later years. If it finds the error was reckless or fraudulent, you can be barred for two or ten years, so get it right.

How the credit changes as your income changes

Because the EITC phases in and out, a change in income can move the credit noticeably. If Mia's income rose to $30,000 next year, still with one child, the credit would start shrinking above $23,890 and would be roughly $1,000 smaller. Mia would still keep far more of the $9,000 raise than the credit lost: the credit is designed so that earning more leaves you better off overall. If your income is close to the edges of these ranges, the IRS's free EITC Assistant tool can check your eligibility and estimate the credit in a few minutes.

Claiming it for past years

If you qualified in an earlier year but didn't claim the credit, you can generally still claim it by filing a return (or an amended return) within three years of the original due date. That can mean money from years you thought were finished.

Common questions

Can I get the earned income credit with no children?

Yes, if you're between 25 and 64 at the end of the year, have earned income within the limits, and meet the other rules. For 2026 the maximum credit without a qualifying child is $664, and it phases out completely above $19,540 for single filers.

Do I have to file taxes to get the EITC?

Yes. You have to file a federal tax return to claim it, even if your income is below the filing requirement and you owe no tax. That's the most common reason eligible people miss it.

Does self-employment income count for the EITC?

Yes. Net earnings from self-employment count as earned income. Report all of it, because leaving income out can change or disqualify the credit, and self-employment tax still applies to it.

When will I get my EITC refund?

The IRS can't issue refunds that include the EITC before mid-February. It says most early filers who use direct deposit and have no issues receive these refunds by early March.
