Standard deduction vs itemizing: how to tell which saves you more

The 2026 standard deduction, what you can itemize, the new SALT and charity rules, and two worked returns showing when itemizing wins.

Every tax return comes down to one choice that most software makes for you: take the **standard deduction**, a flat amount anyone can claim, or **itemize**, adding up specific expenses instead. You pick whichever is bigger.

For about nine in ten filers, the standard deduction wins, and it isn't close. But some people, especially homeowners in higher-tax states, can save real money by itemizing, and the rules changed for 2026 returns in ways that tip the decision for some households. This guide explains both, the new rules, and two worked returns that show where the line falls.

The 2026 standard deduction

Filing statusStandard deduction (2026)
Single or married filing separately$16,100
Head of household$24,150
Married filing jointly$32,200

People who are 65 or older, or blind, get an additional standard deduction on top. And for 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older can also take a separate deduction of up to $6,000 each (phasing out at higher incomes), whether they itemize or not.

What you can itemize

Itemized deductions go on Schedule A. The main ones:

  • Mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt on your main home and a second home (higher limits apply to some older mortgages).
  • State and local taxes (SALT): state and local income tax (or sales tax instead), plus property tax. The cap rose sharply under the 2025 tax law: for 2026 it's $40,400 ($20,200 if married filing separately), shrinking for incomes above $505,000 but never below $10,000. It's scheduled to rise 1% a year through 2029 and then fall back to $10,000.
  • Charitable gifts to qualifying organizations. Starting in 2026, itemizers can deduct only the amount above 0.5% of their AGI.
  • Medical and dental expenses above 7.5% of AGI.
  • A few others, such as casualty losses from federally declared disasters and gambling losses (up to gambling winnings).

Many things people expect to deduct aren't on the list: rent, ordinary commuting, personal credit card interest, and most work expenses for employees.

The new rule for people who don't itemize

Starting with 2026 returns, people who take the standard deduction can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to qualifying charities ($2,000 for married couples filing jointly). Gifts of goods and gifts to donor-advised funds don't count.

That small change matters for the decision. A household that itemizes gives up this deduction, so itemizing now has to beat the standard deduction plus whatever non-itemizer charitable deduction you'd get.

A worked example: Ari and Noor, two versions

Ari and Noor (a fictional couple) are married, file jointly, and have AGI of $150,000. They give $2,500 in cash to charities each year. Their 0.5% charitable floor is 0.5% of $150,000: $750.

Version 1: a modest mortgage.

Itemized deductionAmount
Mortgage interest$11,000
State income tax and property tax (under the cap)$9,500
Charitable gifts ($2,500 minus the $750 floor)$1,750
Total itemized$22,250

Against that: the standard deduction of $32,200, plus $2,000 of charitable deduction for non-itemizers, for $34,200. The standard deduction wins by almost $12,000. Itemizing would be a mistake.

Version 2: a bigger mortgage in a higher-tax state.

Itemized deductionAmount
Mortgage interest$19,000
State income tax and property tax (under the cap)$14,000
Charitable gifts ($2,500 minus $750)$1,750
Total itemized$34,750

Against $34,200 with the standard deduction and the non-itemizer charitable deduction. Itemizing wins, but only by $550. In the 22% bracket, that's worth about $121 of tax.

The lesson from version 2: being slightly over the standard deduction is worth very little. And under the old $10,000 SALT cap, Ari and Noor's $14,000 of state and property tax would have been capped, leaving them well under the standard deduction. The higher cap is what pushes some homeowners in higher-tax states over the line from 2025 onward.

A quick way to check

  1. Add up your mortgage interest (from Form 1098), state and local income and property taxes (up to the cap), and charitable gifts above 0.5% of your AGI.
  2. Add medical costs above 7.5% of AGI, if you had large ones.
  3. Compare the total with your standard deduction, plus the $1,000 or $2,000 non-itemizer charity deduction if you give cash to charity.

If you're far below, take the standard deduction and don't spend time collecting receipts for Schedule A. If you're close, let your tax software compare both. It does this automatically when you enter the numbers.

Bunching: itemizing every other year

If you're close to the line every year, bunching can help. You concentrate deductible expenses you control, like charitable gifts and sometimes property tax payments, into one year so that you itemize that year, then take the standard deduction the next.

For example, if Ari and Noor in version 2 gave two years' worth of donations ($5,000) in one year, their itemized total that year would be $37,250. The next year, with no gifts, they'd take the $32,200 standard deduction. Over the two years that's $69,450 of deductions, against $69,500 from itemizing both years. For them, bunching makes no real difference, because their mortgage interest and state taxes already put them over the line. Bunching pays off when it's the charitable gifts themselves that would carry you over the standard deduction. Some people use a donor-advised fund to bunch while spreading the actual grants to charities over time (contributions to donor-advised funds don't qualify for the non-itemizer deduction).

Bunching is worth a quick calculation if you give regularly and your itemized total is close to the standard deduction. It isn't worth changing your giving for unless the numbers clearly work.

Records to keep if you itemize

If itemizing wins, keep the paperwork that backs up each line: Form 1098 from your mortgage lender, property tax bills, your W-2s or state return for state income tax withheld, and written acknowledgments from charities. For any single gift of $250 or more, you need a written acknowledgment from the charity before you file. For gifts of goods, note what you gave and its fair market value (roughly what it would sell for in a thrift shop, not what you paid). For medical expenses, keep receipts and insurance statements showing what you paid yourself.

Even if you take the standard deduction, keep receipts for cash gifts to charity if you claim the new non-itemizer deduction. You don't send them with your return, but the IRS can ask.

Who should look at itemizing

  • Homeowners with a large mortgage, especially in the early years of the loan when most of each payment is interest.
  • People in higher-tax states with significant state income tax and property tax, now that the SALT cap is higher.
  • People with very large medical bills in a year, above 7.5% of AGI.
  • Generous givers, especially if they can bunch donations.

If none of those applies, the standard deduction is almost certainly yours, and the non-itemizer charitable deduction is a small bonus if you give cash to charity.

Married filing separately

One rule catches couples out: if you're married and file separate returns, and one spouse itemizes, the other must itemize too, even if their itemized total is tiny. The standard deduction is not available to them. Couples considering separate returns, for example because of income-driven student loan repayment, should run the numbers both ways before deciding.

State returns are separate

Some states let you itemize on your state return even if you take the federal standard deduction, or require you to match your federal choice. Others have their own standard deduction amounts. Check your state's rules, especially if your federal decision was close. (Filing your first return.)

Common questions

Should I itemize or take the standard deduction?

Take whichever gives the bigger deduction. For 2026, add up mortgage interest, state and local taxes (up to the $40,400 cap), charitable gifts above 0.5% of AGI, and medical costs above 7.5% of AGI. If that's less than your standard deduction plus any non-itemizer charity deduction, take the standard deduction.

Can I deduct charitable donations without itemizing?

Starting with 2026 returns, yes: up to $1,000 of cash gifts to qualifying charities, or $2,000 for married couples filing jointly. Gifts of goods and contributions to donor-advised funds don't qualify for this deduction.

What is the SALT cap for 2026?

For 2026, the cap on state and local tax deductions is $40,400, or $20,200 for married people filing separately. It's reduced for incomes above $505,000 but not below $10,000, and it's scheduled to return to $10,000 in 2030.

Is it worth itemizing if I'm only slightly over the standard deduction?

It's worth taking, but it's worth little: you save your tax rate times the difference. Being $500 over in the 22% bracket saves about $110. Bunching deductions into alternate years can make itemizing more valuable.
