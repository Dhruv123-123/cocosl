Tax deductions vs tax credits: why a $1,000 credit beats a $1,000 deduction

How deductions and credits each lower your tax, the new 2026 deductions for tips, overtime and seniors, and a worked return showing what each one saves.

Tax software asks a long list of questions, and most of them are hunting for two things: **deductions** and **credits**. Both lower your tax bill. But they work in completely different ways, and a dollar of one is worth very different amounts from a dollar of the other.

The short version: a deduction lowers the income you're taxed on; a credit lowers the tax itself. For most people, a $1,000 credit is worth several times as much as a $1,000 deduction.

This guide explains both, lists the ones worth knowing for 2026 returns (filed in 2027), including the new deductions for tips, overtime and people over 65, and runs a worked return so you can see exactly what each one saves.

How a deduction works

A deduction is subtracted from your income before tax is calculated. It lowers your taxable income.

What a deduction saves depends on your tax bracket. If your top bracket is 12%, a $1,000 deduction lowers your tax by $120. If it's 22%, the same deduction saves $220. A deduction is worth more to people with higher incomes.

There are three kinds:

  • Adjustments to income (sometimes called "above-the-line" deductions). These come off before your adjusted gross income (AGI) is calculated, and you get them whether or not you itemize. Examples: deductible traditional IRA contributions, health savings account (HSA) contributions you make yourself, student loan interest (up to $2,500), and half of self-employment tax.
  • The standard deduction or itemized deductions. You choose one: a flat amount ($16,100 for single filers in 2026, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly) or the total of specific expenses like mortgage interest, state and local taxes and charitable gifts. (Standard deduction vs itemizing.)
  • Deductions you can take on top of either. Under the 2025 tax law, several new deductions are available whether you itemize or not, covered below.

How a credit works

A credit is subtracted from the tax you owe, after it's been calculated. A $1,000 credit lowers your tax by $1,000, whatever your bracket.

Credits come in two kinds, and the difference matters a lot for people with lower incomes:

  • Nonrefundable credits can bring your tax down to zero, but not below. If you owe $600 and qualify for a $1,000 nonrefundable credit, you save $600 and the other $400 is lost.
  • Refundable credits can go below zero: if the credit is bigger than your tax, the IRS pays you the difference as part of your refund. That's why some families with little or no tax owed still get large refunds.

Some credits are partly refundable. For 2026, the child tax credit is worth up to $2,200 per qualifying child, of which up to $1,700 can be refundable.

Deductions worth knowing for 2026

  • Standard deduction: $16,100 single, $24,150 head of household, $32,200 married filing jointly.
  • Overtime pay: for 2025 through 2028, workers can deduct qualified overtime pay (the extra "half" in time-and-a-half that federal law requires) up to $12,500 a year, or $25,000 on a joint return. It phases out at higher incomes.
  • Tips: workers in occupations that customarily receive tips can deduct qualified tips, up to $25,000 a year, for 2025 through 2028. It also phases out at higher incomes.
  • Seniors: people 65 and older can take an extra deduction of up to $6,000 each, for 2025 through 2028, on top of the standard deduction. It phases out above $75,000 of modified AGI for single filers ($150,000 for joint returns).
  • Car loan interest: up to $10,000 a year of interest on a loan for a new vehicle whose final assembly was in the US, for 2025 through 2028, with income limits.
  • Charitable gifts without itemizing: starting with 2026 returns, people who take the standard deduction can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to qualifying charities ($2,000 for joint returns).
  • Student loan interest: up to $2,500 a year, with income limits.
  • Traditional IRA contributions: deductible up to the limit if you're not covered by a workplace plan, or within income limits if you are. (IRA contributions before the deadline.)

Each of these has eligibility rules. Good tax software asks about them; if you're unsure, IRS guidance on each one explains who qualifies.

Credits worth knowing for 2026

  • Earned income tax credit (EITC): refundable, for workers with low to moderate incomes. Up to $8,231 for 2026 with three or more qualifying children, and up to $664 with none. (The EITC, explained.)
  • Child tax credit: up to $2,200 per qualifying child under 17, with up to $1,700 refundable.
  • American opportunity tax credit: up to $2,500 per eligible student for the first four years of college, 40% of it refundable.
  • Lifetime learning credit: 20% of up to $10,000 of tuition and related expenses, so up to $2,000 per return, for any level of college or job-skills courses. Nonrefundable.
  • Saver's credit: a credit for contributing to a retirement account, for single filers with AGI up to $40,250 in 2026 ($80,500 for joint returns). Nonrefundable.
  • Child and dependent care credit: a share of what you pay for care so you can work.

A worked example: Sky's return

Sky (a fictional example) is single and earned $48,000 in 2026 from a warehouse job, including $3,000 of overtime premium (the extra half on overtime hours). Sky also:

  • put $1,000 into a traditional IRA (Sky's job has no retirement plan, so it's deductible);
  • gave $400 in cash to a food bank;
  • paid $1,500 in tuition for an evening course at a community college.

Without any of these, Sky's taxable income is $48,000 minus the $16,100 standard deduction: $31,900. Tax: 10% on the first $12,400 ($1,240) plus 12% on the remaining $19,500 ($2,340), for a total of $3,580.

Now add the deductions:

Tax on $27,500: $1,240 plus 12% of $15,100 ($1,812), for $3,052. The three deductions, totalling $4,400, saved $528: exactly 12% of $4,400, because every one of those dollars would otherwise have been taxed at Sky's 12% bracket.

Now add the credit. The lifetime learning credit is 20% of Sky's $1,500 tuition: $300. It comes straight off the tax, taking it from $3,052 to $2,752.

So $4,400 of deductions saved $528, and $1,500 of tuition (through a credit) saved $300. Put another way: each dollar of deduction saved Sky 12 cents; each dollar of credit saved a full dollar.

One more thing to notice: the Saver's Credit didn't help Sky, because Sky's AGI ($47,000) is above the $40,250 limit for single filers. If Sky had earned less, or contributed more to deductible retirement accounts, it might have.

How to make sure you get what you're owed

  • Keep the paperwork through the year. Tuition forms (1098-T), student loan interest (1098-E), charity receipts, and pay stubs showing overtime. A folder or a notes app is enough.
  • Answer every question in your tax software, even the ones that seem unlikely. That's how it finds credits.
  • Check refundable credits if your income was low. Even if you owe no tax, refundable credits like the EITC and the refundable part of the child tax credit can mean a refund. You have to file to get them.
  • Look at your withholding afterwards. New deductions, like overtime, might mean you're having too much tax withheld. (How to fill out a W-4.)
  • Get free help if it's confusing. IRS Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) sites prepare returns for free for many people, and they know the credits well. (Filing for the first time.)

A quick test: deduction or credit?

If a tax break is described as "up to $X off your tax", it's a credit. If it's "deduct up to $X" or "$X off your income", it's a deduction. To estimate a deduction's real value, multiply it by your top tax bracket. To estimate a credit's value, take it at face value (but check whether it's refundable if your tax is already low).

Common questions

Is a tax credit better than a deduction?

Dollar for dollar, yes. A credit reduces your tax by its full amount, while a deduction reduces your taxable income, so it saves only your tax rate times the deduction. A $1,000 deduction saves $120 in the 12% bracket; a $1,000 credit saves $1,000.

What is a refundable tax credit?

A credit that can be paid to you even if it's more than the tax you owe. The earned income tax credit is fully refundable; the child tax credit is partly refundable. Nonrefundable credits can only reduce your tax to zero.

Can I take the new overtime and tips deductions if I take the standard deduction?

Yes. For 2025 through 2028, the deductions for qualified overtime pay and qualified tips are available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction, subject to their limits and income phase-outs.

Do I need receipts for deductions?

You should keep records that support every deduction and credit you claim, such as receipts for charitable gifts, tuition forms and pay records. You don't send them with your return, but the IRS can ask to see them.
