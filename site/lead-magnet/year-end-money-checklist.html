Part 1

10 money moves before January 1

Each takes 5 to 10 minutes. Do them all in an afternoon, or one a day until New Year's.

  • Before Dec 31Spend what's left in your health FSACheck your balance and deadline in the benefits portal. FSAs are generally use-it-or-lose-it; some plans give a grace period or let you carry over a limited amount. Glasses, contacts, prescriptions and many over-the-counter medicines usually qualify.
  • Before the last payrollSet next year's 401(k) percentageAt least enough for the full employer match. Got a raise? Add 1%. Contributions for this year must come out of this year's paychecks, so December is the last chance to add more for 2026.
  • Before JanuaryRun the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator15 minutes with a recent pay stub. If you're heading for a big refund or a bill, give payroll a new W-4 for January. Especially after a side gig, a new job or getting married.
  • Check your credit reports and consider a freezeFree at AnnualCreditReport.com. Look for accounts you don't recognise and wrong late payments. A freeze at all three bureaus is free, doesn't affect your score and blocks most new-account fraud.
  • Cancel subscriptions you didn't use this fallList every recurring charge from the last three months of statements. Watch for free trials from holiday sales that are about to start charging.
  • Before Dec 31Use work perks that reset in JanuaryLearning stipends, wellness or phone allowances, commuter benefits. Check the benefits portal or ask HR.
  • Write down your net worthEverything you own (cash, investments, retirement, car at resale value) minus everything you owe. Negative is normal early on: it's a baseline for next December.
  • List next year's known big costsCar registration, insurance renewals, annual subscriptions, weddings, travel, next year's holidays. Each with its month and amount.
  • Set up in JanuaryTurn them into sinking fundsDivide each cost by the months until it's due and automate a transfer to separate savings. Example: $600 of travel in June, starting in January, is $100 a month.
  • Pick three goals with a number, a date and an automationNot "save more" but "$2,400 in the emergency fund by December, $100 every payday". Set the transfers before January 1.

Two deadlines to remember. 401(k) contributions for 2026 come from 2026 paychecks. IRA and HSA contributions for 2026 can generally be made until the tax filing deadline, April 15, 2027.

Your holiday plan in 4 numbers

About 30 minutes. One total, four categories, one per-person gift number.

1. Your total

Money you have now or will earn this month, minus bills through month-end and a small cushion, plus anything already set aside. Not next month's paycheck, and not a card balance you'll pay off "eventually".

Part 3

Black Friday and checkout traps

Deals can be real. Stores are designed to make you decide fast. These checks slow it down.

Before you buy: 5 checks

  • Was it on my list before the sale?If not, it goes in the cart for 24 hours. Countdown timers and "only 2 left" are there to rush you.
  • Is it really cheaper?Check the price history: some "sale" prices are what it cost in October.
  • Cost per usePrice ÷ times you'll realistically use it. A $40 gadget used twice is $20 a use; a $90 appliance used 100 times is 90¢.
  • Which card?For online shopping, a credit card you pay in full is usually safer than debit: federal law caps your liability for unauthorized credit card charges at $50, and the money doesn't leave your account while you dispute it.
  • Does it fit the category number?If not, pick something else, or move money from another category on purpose.

The store card at checkout. "20% off today" often comes with deferred interest: if any of the promo balance is left when the promo ends, interest is charged all the way back to the purchase date. Store card rates are often near 30%. Unless you'd pay it in full on the first statement, say no thanks.

Pay in 4 without the hangover

  • One plan at a timeStacked plans are how a $50 payment becomes a $200 week.
  • Know the late fee and where payments come fromAn auto-pull from a thin checking account can mean a late fee and an overdraft.
  • Treat it as creditSome providers report payments to the credit bureaus.
  • Only for things you could pay for todayIt's a payment schedule, not extra money.
