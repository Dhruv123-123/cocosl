If you have no credit history, or a damaged one, getting approved for a regular credit card can be hard. A **secured credit card** is one of the most common ways to start. You put down a deposit, and the card works like a normal credit card. Used carefully, it can help you build a credit history without paying any interest at all.

This guide explains how secured cards work, what to look for, how to use one to build credit, and how to get your deposit back.

## How a secured card works

A secured card requires an upfront payment. A consumer guide published for the FTC explains it simply: you deposit money with the bank, and the deposit is usually **$300 to $500**. Your credit limit is often equal to your deposit, though sometimes it's lower.

After that, it works like any credit card:

- You make purchases up to your limit.
- You get a monthly statement with a due date.
- You pay the balance, in full or in part.
- If you carry a balance, you pay interest.

The deposit isn't used to pay your bill each month. It's security for the bank in case you stop paying. You still owe your monthly payments.

## Secured card vs prepaid card vs debit card

| | Secured credit card | Prepaid card | Debit card |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is it credit? | Yes | No | No |
| Can it build credit history? | Yes, if the issuer reports to the bureaus | No | No |
| Needs a deposit? | Yes, usually refundable | You load money onto it | Uses your bank balance |
| Interest | If you carry a balance | None | None |

The FTC guide notes that a secured card can help you build or improve your credit history, and that it usually has lower fees than a prepaid card.

## What to look for

The same guide suggests looking for a card with **no application fee** (which it says to avoid), a **low annual fee**, **no processing fees** and a **low interest rate**. A few more things to check:

- **It reports to all three credit bureaus.** Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. If it doesn't report, it won't help you build credit.
- **A path to "graduate."** Some issuers review your account and move you to a regular, unsecured card, returning your deposit.
- **The deposit amount and limit.** A higher limit makes it easier to keep your balance low compared with your limit.
- **Penalty fees and the APR**, in case you ever carry a balance.

A bank or credit union where you already have an account is a good place to start. ([How to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/).)

## How to use a secured card to build credit

### 1. Put one small, regular bill on it
A phone bill or a streaming subscription works well. Small, predictable charges are easy to pay off.

### 2. Set up autopay for the full statement balance
Paying the full statement balance by the due date means you pay no interest on purchases. Autopay makes sure you're never late.

### 3. Keep the balance low
The CFPB's advice for good credit includes keeping credit card balances low compared with your limits. On a $300 card, a $25 balance is about 8%. ([Credit utilization explained](/blog/credit-utilization-explained/).)

### 4. Never miss a payment
Payment history is one of the biggest factors in credit scores. A single late payment can set you back.

### 5. Check your credit reports
After a few months, check your reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, free weekly, to make sure the card shows up with on-time payments. ([How to check your credit report](/blog/how-to-check-your-credit-report/).)

### 6. Ask about upgrading
After six to twelve months of on-time payments, ask the issuer whether you can move to an unsecured card and get your deposit back.

## A worked example: Zed's first year

Zed (a fictional example) has no credit history and opens a secured card with a **$300 deposit**, a **$300 limit** and **no annual fee**. The card reports to all three bureaus.

| Month | What Zed does | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Puts only the $25 phone bill on the card; sets up autopay of the full statement balance | Reported balance about 8% of the limit |
| 2–6 | Leaves it running; never late | On-time history builds |
| 7 | Checks all three credit reports | The card appears on all three, with on-time payments |
| 12 | The issuer reviews the account | Zed moves to an unsecured card with a $1,000 limit; the **$300 deposit is refunded** |

Total interest paid: **$0**. Total effort: about ten minutes a month.

(Fictional example. Whether and when an issuer upgrades your account varies.)

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Treating the deposit as a payment.** You still need to pay your bill each month.
- **Maxing out the card.** A balance close to the limit can hurt your score, even if you pay on time.
- **Carrying a balance to "build credit."** You don't need to pay interest to build credit. Paying in full works.
- **Closing it too soon.** If it has no annual fee, keeping the account open after you get another card can help the length of your credit history.
- **Applying for several cards at once.** Each application can mean a hard inquiry.

## Getting your deposit back

You usually get the deposit back when:

- the issuer upgrades you to an unsecured card, or
- you close the account with a zero balance.

If you close the account with a balance, the issuer may use the deposit to pay it off and refund what's left. Ask the issuer how its refund works and how long it takes.

## Other ways to build credit

A secured card isn't the only option:

- **Credit-builder loans** from some credit unions and online lenders hold the money you borrow in an account while you make payments, then release it. ([Self credit builder review](/blog/self-credit-builder-review/).)
- **Becoming an authorised user** on a family member's card with a long, on-time history can help, if the issuer reports authorised users.
- **A student card** may be available if you're in college. ([Best bank account for college students](/blog/best-bank-account-for-college-students/).)

## How long it takes

Most credit scoring models need at least several months of history on your report before they can produce a score. Building a strong score takes longer: steady on-time payments and low balances over a year or more. There's no shortcut, but the habits are simple. ([What is a good credit score?](/blog/what-is-a-good-credit-score/).)

## How to compare secured cards

When you compare cards, line up the same few details for each one:

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Minimum deposit | How much cash you need to start |
| Credit limit vs deposit | A limit equal to the deposit is more common; a higher limit helps utilization |
| Annual fee | Many secured cards have none |
| Other fees | Application, processing or monthly fees add up quickly |
| Reports to all three bureaus | Needed to build credit across all your reports |
| Graduation policy | Whether and when you can move to an unsecured card and get the deposit back |
| APR | Matters only if you carry a balance, but check the penalty APR too |

Read the card's terms before applying. Card issuers must show the main rates and fees in a summary table, often called the Schumer box, so they're easy to compare.

## Using a secured card responsibly

Treat the card like a debit card that happens to build credit: only use it for things you've already budgeted for and have the cash to pay. Check the app once a week, and turn on alerts for every purchase. That keeps the balance low and helps you spot fraud early.

## Common questions

### Does a secured credit card build credit as fast as a regular card?
If the issuer reports to the credit bureaus, a secured card is reported much like any other credit card, so on-time payments count the same way. What matters most is paying on time and keeping the balance low.

### Can I be denied for a secured credit card?
Yes, though approval is usually easier than for an unsecured card. Issuers may still check your income, existing debts, or banking history. If you're turned down, ask why, and consider a credit union or a credit-builder loan.

### What happens to my deposit if I miss payments?
Missing payments leads to late fees and negative marks on your credit report. If the account is closed for non-payment, the issuer can use the deposit to cover what you owe. That's why autopay is so useful.

### Should I choose a secured card with an annual fee?
Many secured cards have no annual fee, so it's worth comparing first. A small fee might be worth it if the card offers a clear path to an unsecured card or reports to all three bureaus when others don't.
