A certificate of deposit, or **CD**, is one of the simplest places to keep money you won't need for a while. You agree to leave a set amount with a bank or credit union for a set time, and in return you usually get a fixed rate. The trade-off is flexibility: take the money out early and you'll likely pay a penalty.

This guide explains how CDs work, how they compare with savings accounts, the fine print to check, and how a CD ladder can give you both a fixed rate and regular access to your money.

## How a CD works

Investor.gov, the SEC's investor education site, describes a CD as a savings product that holds a set amount of money for a set period, such as six months, one year or five years, with the bank paying interest over that time. When the CD **matures**, you get your money back plus the interest. It notes CDs are considered one of the safest savings options.

The main features:

- **Term**: how long the money is committed, often from three months to five years.
- **Rate**: usually fixed for the whole term, and shown as an **APY**, which includes compounding. ([APR vs APY explained](/blog/apr-vs-apy-explained/).)
- **Minimum deposit**: some CDs have none; others need $500, $1,000 or more.
- **Early withdrawal penalty**: what you lose if you take money out before maturity.

## CDs vs high-yield savings accounts

| | CD | High-yield savings account |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | Usually fixed for the term | Variable; can change any time |
| Access to money | Locked until maturity (penalty to withdraw early) | Available any time |
| Best for | Money with a known date | Emergency funds and flexible goals |
| Deposit insurance | FDIC or NCUA, up to the limits | FDIC or NCUA, up to the limits |

A CD can make sense when you know you won't need the money until a specific date and want to lock in a rate. For an emergency fund, a high-yield savings account is usually better because you can reach it immediately. ([High-yield savings vs CDs](/compare/high-yield-savings-vs-cds/).)

## The fine print to check

### Early withdrawal penalties
Investor.gov says a CD's disclosure should explain any penalties for withdrawing early. Penalties are often a set number of days or months of interest, and on a short CD the penalty can cost more than the interest earned so far, cutting into your deposit. Check before you open.

### What happens at maturity
Many CDs **renew automatically** into a new CD of the same length, at whatever rate the bank is offering then. Most banks give you a short grace period after maturity to withdraw or change the term without a penalty. Put the maturity date in your calendar.

### Callable CDs
Some CDs can be "called" (ended early) by the bank, usually when rates fall. You get your money and the interest earned so far, but you lose the high rate. Callable CDs sometimes pay a bit more for that risk.

### Variable or step-up rates
Not all CDs have a fixed rate. Some step up on a schedule or follow a benchmark. Read how the rate changes.

## Deposit insurance

CDs at insured banks are covered by the **FDIC**, and at insured credit unions by the **NCUA**. The standard FDIC coverage is **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category**. Investor.gov points out that the limit applies to all your accounts at the same bank, not to each CD separately. If you have more than that at one bank, ask how to structure it or spread it across banks.

## Brokered CDs

You can also buy CDs through a brokerage account. These **brokered CDs** come from banks but are sold by brokers, and can sometimes pay higher rates. Investor.gov notes that deposit brokers aren't licensed or certified and that no state or federal agency approves them, so it advises checking a broker's background and complaint history. Brokered CDs can also behave differently: you may be able to sell them before maturity, but the price can be lower than what you paid if rates have risen.

## How to build a CD ladder

A CD ladder spreads money across CDs that mature at different times, so some money becomes available regularly while the rest earns longer-term rates.

### 1. Decide the amount and the spacing
Pick how much to ladder and how often you want money to come free: for example, every year for five years.

### 2. Split the money into equal rungs
With $10,000 and a five-year ladder, that's five rungs of $2,000.

### 3. Open CDs with staggered terms
Put one rung each into one-, two-, three-, four- and five-year CDs.

### 4. Reinvest each rung as it matures
When the one-year CD matures, either use the money or put it into a new five-year CD. After four years, every rung is a five-year CD, and one matures every year.

### 5. Review rates each time
Each maturity is a chance to compare rates at other banks or move the money to savings if you need it.

## A worked example: Bea's car fund

Bea (a fictional example) has **$10,000** she's saving to buy a car next summer, in about a year. She compares two options (rates are illustrative):

| Option | Rate | Interest after one year |
|---|---|---|
| High-yield savings | 3.75% APY, variable | about $375, if the rate doesn't fall |
| 1-year CD | 4.10% APY, fixed | $410 |

The CD pays a little more and the rate is locked in. But the CD's early withdrawal penalty is **90 days of interest** (about **$100**), and Bea doesn't have a separate emergency fund.

So Bea splits the money: **$3,000 stays in savings** as an emergency buffer and **$7,000 goes into the 1-year CD**. She earns about **$287** from the CD and about **$113** from savings, around **$400** in total, while keeping $3,000 she can reach any day.

(Fictional example with illustrative rates. Actual rates and penalties vary by bank.)

## When CDs make sense

- **Saving for a dated goal**: a car, a wedding, a home down payment in a year or two. ([How to save for a house down payment](/blog/how-to-save-for-a-house-down-payment/).)
- **Locking in a rate** you think is good, when you won't need the money.
- **Avoiding temptation**: the penalty can stop you dipping into money set aside for a goal.

## When they don't

- **Your emergency fund**: you need it available instantly. ([How much emergency fund do I need?](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/).)
- **Money you might need soon** without a clear date.
- **Long-term goals decades away**, such as retirement, where investing may be more suitable for some people.
- **High-interest debt**: paying off a credit card usually "earns" more than a CD pays.

## Where to find CD rates

CD rates vary a lot between banks and credit unions, and the highest rates are often at online banks and credit unions rather than large branch banks. When you compare:

- **Compare APY for the same term**, such as one year against one year.
- **Check the minimum deposit** and whether a higher rate needs a bigger balance.
- **Read the penalty** in days or months of interest, not just "penalty applies."
- **Check what happens at maturity**: automatic renewal, the grace period and how you'll be notified.
- **Confirm deposit insurance** by checking the institution with the FDIC or NCUA.

## CDs and taxes on your savings

Because CD interest is generally taxable each year, a CD held in a regular account earns a little less after tax than its APY suggests. If you're saving for retirement, some banks and brokerages let you hold CDs inside an IRA, where the interest isn't taxed each year. That can suit money you want to keep safe inside a retirement account, though it may earn less over long periods than other investments.

## Common questions

### Can you lose money in a CD?
Not from the bank failing, up to the insurance limits, if the CD is at an FDIC- or NCUA-insured institution. You can lose some interest, and occasionally part of your deposit, if you withdraw early and pay a penalty. A brokered CD sold before maturity can also be worth less than you paid.

### Is CD interest taxable?
Generally, yes. CD interest is usually taxable in the year it's paid or credited, even if you leave it in the CD. Your bank sends a tax form showing the interest.

### What's a no-penalty CD?
A no-penalty CD lets you withdraw your money before maturity without an early withdrawal penalty, usually after a short initial period. The rate is often lower than a standard CD of the same length.

### Should I choose a longer CD for a higher rate?
Only if you're confident you won't need the money for the whole term. Longer terms often pay more, but not always, and early withdrawal penalties are usually larger on longer CDs.
