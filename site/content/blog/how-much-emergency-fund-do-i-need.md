Everyone agrees you need an emergency fund. The hard part is the number. "Three to six months of expenses" is the classic advice, but that's a wide range, and for someone starting from zero it can feel impossibly far away.

This guide breaks the question into pieces you can actually answer: which expenses count, how many months fit your situation, and what to aim for first. It takes about 10 minutes, and you end with one target figure and a date.

## Why a number matters

Without a target, saving tends to stall. You put a little aside, something comes up, and the money disappears into ordinary spending. A specific number does two jobs: it tells you when to stop worrying, and it tells you when you've saved enough to start sending extra money elsewhere, like debt payoff or investing.

It also matters because emergencies are common. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey of household finances, 63% of adults said they would cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent. That leaves a large minority who would need to borrow, sell something or couldn't pay it at all. A small cushion is what moves you from the second group to the first.

## Step 1: count only the essentials

Your emergency fund doesn't need to replace your whole lifestyle. In a real emergency, like losing your job, you'd cut the extras. So the starting figure is your **essential monthly costs**:

| Include | Leave out |
|---|---|
| Rent or mortgage | Eating out |
| Utilities, internet, phone | Streaming and subscriptions you'd cancel |
| Groceries | Holidays and travel |
| Transport to work (car payment, insurance, fuel or transit) | Shopping and hobbies |
| Insurance premiums, including health | Extra debt payments above the minimum |
| Minimum debt payments | Saving and investing contributions |
| Childcare and medicine | Gifts |

Use last month's bank statement and add up the left column. If you don't have a budget yet, the total from [a four-line budget](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/) works too.

## Step 2: choose your months

How many months of essentials you need depends on how likely and how long a loss of income would be.

| Your situation | Suggested months |
|---|---|
| Stable salaried job, two incomes in the household, no dependents | 3 |
| Stable job, single income | 3 to 4 |
| Single income with dependents, or one income supports the household | 6 |
| Commission, seasonal or contract work | 6 or more |
| Freelance or self-employed | 6 to 12 |
| Job in an industry with frequent layoffs, or a specialised role that takes a long time to replace | add 1 to 2 months |
| Older car, older home, or a health condition with unpredictable costs | add 1 month |

These are rules of thumb, not formulas. The question to ask yourself is: "If my income stopped tomorrow, how long would it realistically take to replace it?"

## Step 3: multiply, then subtract what you have

**Essential monthly costs × months = your target.** Subtract what's already in savings, and divide the gap by what you can save each month. That's your date.

If the date is years away, that's normal. What matters is that each month gets you closer and you know where you're going.

## Step 4: set milestones

A big target is easier to reach in stages:

1. **$1,000, or one month of essentials, whichever is smaller.** This covers most single surprises: a car repair, a vet bill, a flight home. ([The 8-week plan to a starter fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)
2. **One full month of essentials.**
3. **Three months.** At this point most job losses are survivable without debt.
4. **Your full target.**

If you have high-interest credit card debt, a common approach is to reach milestone 1, then put extra money toward the debt, and return to building the fund once it's paid. A small fund stops new emergencies going on the card. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)

## A worked example: Omar sets a target

Omar (a fictional example) is 27, single, and works in tech marketing, an area that has seen layoffs. Omar has **$2,000** in savings and wants to know whether that's enough.

**Step 1: essentials.**

| Cost | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,150 |
| Utilities and phone | $160 |
| Groceries | $380 |
| Transport | $220 |
| Insurance | $140 |
| Minimum student loan payment | $150 |
| **Total essentials** | **$2,200** |

**Step 2: months.** Single income (3 to 4) plus a layoff-prone industry (add 1) gives **4 months**, a bit cautious but reasonable.

**Step 3: target.** $2,200 × 4 = **$8,800**. Omar has $2,000, so the gap is **$6,800**. At $400 a month, that's about **17 months**.

**Step 4: milestones.**

| Milestone | Amount | Reached in |
|---|---|---|
| One month of essentials | $2,200 | Month 1 |
| Three months | $6,600 | Month 12 |
| Full target (4 months) | $8,800 | Month 17 |

Seventeen months felt long, so Omar looked for ways to speed it up: a tax refund of $900 and a $600 bonus went straight to the fund, cutting the time to about 13 months. After that, the $400 a month moves to investing.

(Numbers are illustrative. Your essentials, months and savings rate will differ.)

## Where to keep it

An emergency fund has to be **safe and quick to reach**, not invested for growth:

- **A high-yield savings account** at an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union) is the usual home. It's separate from checking, so you're less tempted to spend it, and it earns interest. ([Best high-yield savings accounts](/compare/best-high-yield-savings-accounts/).)
- **Not the stock market.** Emergencies often arrive at the same time as market falls, like a recession that costs jobs. You don't want to be selling investments at a loss to pay rent.
- **CDs** can work for part of a large fund, but early withdrawal penalties make them awkward for money you might need next week. ([High-yield savings vs CDs](/compare/high-yield-savings-vs-cds/).)

## What counts as an emergency

Decide this before it happens, so you're not making the call at a stressful moment. A useful test: **is it necessary, urgent and unexpected?**

- **Yes:** job loss, a medical bill, an essential car or home repair, an emergency flight for a family crisis.
- **No:** a sale, a holiday, a new phone because the old one is slow, a planned annual bill.

Predictable costs, like car insurance renewals, holidays and gifts, belong in **sinking funds**, not the emergency fund. Otherwise the fund gets drained by things that weren't emergencies at all. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

## After you use it

Using the fund is the point of having it, so don't feel bad about it. Pause any extra investing or debt payments for a few months and refill it at your normal savings rate, starting with milestone 1.

## Ways to build it faster

Saving a fixed amount each month is the core of it, but a few one-off boosts can cut months off the timeline. Send part or all of a **tax refund**, a **bonus** or a **raise** straight to the fund before it reaches your spending account. Sell things you no longer use. And look at your fixed bills once: switching a phone plan or insurance policy can free up a monthly amount that goes to savings automatically.

## When to recalculate

Look at your number again when any of these change:

- you move, or your rent changes;
- you have a child, or someone starts relying on your income;
- you change jobs, especially to freelance or commission work;
- a partner's income starts or stops;
- you take on a car payment or other new fixed bill.

A quick yearly check, perhaps when you do your taxes, is enough for most people.

## Common questions

### Is $1,000 enough for an emergency fund?
It's a good first goal, because it covers many common surprises like a car repair. But it usually won't cover a job loss. Once you have $1,000, keep going toward one month of essential expenses, then three or more.

### Should I build an emergency fund or pay off debt first?
Many people do both in stages: a small starter fund first, so new emergencies don't go on a credit card, then extra payments on high-interest debt, then the rest of the emergency fund. Low-interest debt doesn't usually need to be paid off before you finish the fund.

### Should my emergency fund cover my partner's expenses too?
If you share household costs, base the target on your shared essential expenses and how many incomes would still be coming in if one stopped. Two stable incomes usually need fewer months than one.

### Can I keep my emergency fund in a brokerage account?
It's usually not recommended. Investments can fall in value just when you need the money. Keep at least the first few months in an insured savings account you can reach within a day or two.
