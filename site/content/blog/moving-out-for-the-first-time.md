Moving out for the first time is exciting, and expensive in ways that catch almost everyone. The rent is the number you plan around, but before you get the keys you may need two or three times that in cash, and the monthly bills include things that were invisible while you lived with family.

This guide breaks the move into two budgets, **the upfront cost** and **the monthly cost**, shows how to check you can afford both, and walks through a worked example of saving for a first apartment.

## Can you afford to move out?

Start with the monthly side, because that's what you'll live with. A common guideline is to keep **rent at or below about 30% of your gross (pre-tax) income**. In many cities that's hard, so a second check helps: after rent and the other fixed bills, is there enough left from your **take-home pay** for food, transport, savings and a bit of fun?

If rent alone would take more than about 40% of your take-home pay, look at a roommate, a cheaper area or waiting a few more months to save. ([Budget by salary](/budget/) shows typical take-home pay and a monthly plan by income.)

## Budget 1: the upfront cost

These are the one-time costs to get in the door and set up:

| Cost | What to expect |
|---|---|
| First month's rent | Due at signing or move-in |
| Security deposit | Often one month's rent; limits vary by state |
| Last month's rent | Some landlords ask for it upfront too |
| Application fees | Per application, often non-refundable |
| Broker or move-in fees | In some cities and buildings |
| Moving costs | A van, movers, or pizza for friends |
| Utility deposits and setup | Electricity, gas, internet installation; some need a deposit without a credit history |
| Furniture and basics | Bed, a place to sit, kitchen basics, cleaning supplies, shower curtain, bins |
| First grocery and household shop | Stocking an empty kitchen costs more than a normal week |
| Renters insurance | The first premium |
| Buffer | 10% or a few hundred dollars for things you'll forget |

Furniture is the most flexible line. A mattress, a few chairs and basic kitchen gear are enough to start; used furniture, family hand-me-downs and community swap groups can cover most of the rest.

## Budget 2: the monthly cost

Bills that move with you, plus the new ones:

- **Rent**
- **Utilities:** electricity, gas, water, sewer and trash (ask the landlord which are included and what past tenants paid)
- **Internet**
- **Phone**
- **Renters insurance** ([what it covers](/blog/renters-insurance-explained/))
- **Groceries and household supplies:** toilet paper, cleaning products and toiletries add up
- **Transport:** fuel, parking, transit pass
- **Subscriptions** you take over from the family plan
- **Savings:** including a sinking fund for things like replacing a broken kettle or a winter heating bill

Put them all in one place before you sign anything. The CFPB's budget worksheet is a simple free template, or use [a four-line budget](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/).

## How to plan your move

### 1. Set your rent ceiling
Work out the most you can pay from your take-home pay and other bills, not from what listings show. Stick to it when you start viewing.

### 2. Add up the upfront cost
Use the table above with real figures: one month's rent for the deposit unless you know otherwise, quotes for a van, and a furniture list.

### 3. Save for it in a separate account
Divide the upfront total by the months until your move, and set up an automatic transfer on payday into a separate high-yield savings account. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

### 4. Check your credit and documents
Landlords often check credit and income. Pull your free credit reports, fix any errors, and have recent pay stubs or an offer letter ready. With no credit history, ask whether a co-signer or a larger deposit is accepted. ([How to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/).)

### 5. View in person, then read the lease
Never pay for a place you haven't seen, or had a trusted person see. Read the whole lease: rent due date, late fees, who pays which utilities, rules on guests and pets, and what happens if you need to leave early.

### 6. Document the condition on move-in day
Take photos and video of every room, including any damage, and send a copy to the landlord. This is how you get your deposit back later.

## A worked example: Cole's first apartment

Cole (a fictional example) earns **$52,800** a year, about **$3,300 a month** after tax and benefits, and has found one-bedroom apartments around **$1,200** a month. Rent would be about 27% of gross pay and 36% of take-home pay: tight but workable.

**Upfront cost:**

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| First month's rent | $1,200 |
| Security deposit | $1,200 |
| Application fees (2) | $100 |
| Van and helpers | $250 |
| Utility setup and deposit | $150 |
| Used couch, new mattress, kitchen basics | $900 |
| First grocery and household shop | $150 |
| Renters insurance, first month | $15 |
| Buffer | $300 |
| **Total** | **$4,265** |

**Monthly cost:**

| Bill | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,200 |
| Utilities | $140 |
| Internet | $60 |
| Phone | $40 |
| Renters insurance | $15 |
| Groceries | $350 |
| Transport | $180 |
| Household supplies | $40 |
| **Total** | **$2,025** |

That leaves about **$1,275** a month for eating out, fun, savings and anything else, which is a workable cushion.

**The plan:** Cole saves **$600 a month** while still at home, so the $4,265 takes about **7 months**. Cole signs a lease in month 8, with the upfront cost already covered and nothing on a credit card.

## Avoid rental scams

Rental scams target people moving for the first time. The FTC lists warning signs, including:

- rent far below similar places nearby;
- an owner who can't show the property in person, often because they're "out of the country";
- pressure to decide or pay quickly;
- requests to pay by wire transfer, gift cards or cryptocurrency;
- requests for your Social Security number or pay stubs before you've agreed to rent;
- the same address listed elsewhere under a different name.

Search the address online, confirm who owns it, and pay by a method you can trace, only after you've seen the place and signed a lease.

## Your first month: set it up so it runs

- **Set up autopay** for rent and fixed bills, timed after payday.
- **Make a weekly spending number** for groceries and everything else.
- **Start a small emergency fund** again if the move used most of your savings. ([How much you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/).)
- **Meet your utilities' billing cycle:** the first bill can include setup fees or a partial month, so don't be alarmed if it's odd.

## Moving with roommates

Sharing cuts rent and utilities, often by a lot, but agree on the money before you sign: who pays what share of rent (by room size is common), how utilities and shared supplies are split, and what happens if someone leaves early. Put it in writing, even informally. If everyone signs the lease, each of you may be responsible for the full rent if someone else doesn't pay.

## Getting your deposit back when you leave

Your deposit is your money, and getting it back starts on move-in day. Keep your move-in photos, report repairs in writing as they come up, and avoid changes the lease doesn't allow, like painting. Before you move out, clean thoroughly, fix small damage, take a new set of photos and give written notice by the date in your lease. Give the landlord your forwarding address in writing. Most states set a deadline for returning deposits and require an itemised list of any deductions, so check your state's rules if the money doesn't arrive.

## Common questions

### How much money should I have saved before moving out?
Enough for the upfront costs (often first month's rent, a deposit, moving and basic furniture) plus a small emergency fund. For many people that's two to four times the monthly rent, but add up your own list to be sure.

### What do landlords look for on a rental application?
Most look at income (often a multiple of the rent), credit history, rental history and references. If you have no credit history, ask whether a co-signer, guarantor or larger deposit would help.

### Is it cheaper to live with roommates?
Usually, yes. Splitting rent and utilities can lower your housing costs significantly. The trade-offs are less privacy and shared responsibility for the lease and bills.

### What's the first thing to buy for a new apartment?
A bed, then kitchen basics and cleaning supplies. Everything else can wait until you've lived there a few weeks and know what you actually need.
