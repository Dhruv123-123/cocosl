Most budgets don't fail in month one. They fail in week three, when the "eating out" category is somehow empty, groceries are over, and you stop opening the spreadsheet because it only makes you feel bad.

The problem usually isn't you. It's that a typical budget asks you to police a dozen categories every day. This guide shows a simpler setup: **four lines, one weekly spending number, and a 20-minute check-in once a week.**

## Why most budgets fail

Monthly categories feel huge on the 1st and empty by the 20th. You can't tell you're overspending until it's too late to fix, and every small purchase becomes a decision: is this "groceries" or "household"? Is coffee "food" or "fun"?

Three things make a budget stick instead:

1. **Fewer decisions.** One number to watch, not twelve.
2. **A short feedback loop.** Weekly, so a bad week is a small problem, not a ruined month.
3. **Automation for everything that doesn't change.** Bills and savings move on their own.

## The four-line budget

Split your monthly take-home pay (what actually lands in your account, after tax and deductions) into four lines:

| Line | What goes in it | How it's handled |
|---|---|---|
| **Bills** | Rent, utilities, phone, insurance, minimum debt payments, subscriptions you're keeping | Automated on payday |
| **Funds** | Monthly amounts for irregular-but-predictable costs: car repairs, gifts, annual renewals | Automated to savings |
| **Goals** | Emergency fund, extra debt payments, investing | Paid first, on payday |
| **Weekly spending** | Groceries, eating out, transport top-ups, fun, everything else | One number per week |

Bills, funds and goals get set up once and then run themselves. The only thing you actively manage is the weekly number.

## Step by step: build yours in an evening

### 1. Find your real monthly take-home pay

Look at your last two or three paychecks, not your salary. If you're paid every two weeks, multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12. If your income varies, use your *lowest* typical month.

### 2. List every fixed bill

Open your bank and card statements for the last 60 days and write down every recurring charge. Add anything that's billed yearly too. While you're there, [hunt down forgotten subscriptions](/blog/find-and-cancel-subscriptions/).

### 3. Add your funds

List the costs that aren't monthly but definitely happen: car registration, holiday gifts, the annual software renewal, a phone that will eventually break. Divide each yearly cost by 12. That's a [sinking fund](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/), and it's the single biggest fix for "surprise" expenses.

### 4. Pick your goals

If you don't have one yet, start with a [starter emergency fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/). After that: extra payments on high-interest debt, then investing. Pick an amount you can keep up every month, even a small one.

### 5. Calculate your weekly number

Weekly number = (take-home pay − bills − funds − goals) × 12 ÷ 52.

Multiplying by 12 and dividing by 52 turns a monthly amount into a true weekly one (there are about 4.33 weeks in a month, not 4).

### 6. Automate it

On payday, set automatic transfers for bills, funds and goals. Many people use two checking accounts: one for bills (never touched by a card) and one for spending, where the weekly number lands. If your bank lets you, schedule a weekly transfer of exactly your number into the spending account.

## A worked example

Maya (a fictional example) takes home **$3,050 a month**.

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Bills: rent $1,250, utilities $90, phone $45, insurance $110, student loan minimum $180, subscriptions $28 | $1,703 |
| Funds: car $40, gifts $35, annual renewals $15, medical $25 | $115 |
| Goals: emergency fund $250, extra on credit card $150 | $400 |
| **Left for spending** | **$832** |

$832 × 12 ÷ 52 = **$192 a week**. Groceries, takeout, rides, a concert ticket: all of it comes from $192. When it's gone, the week's done. When there's money left on Sunday, Maya chooses: roll it into next week, or send it to a goal.

## The 20-minute weekly check-in

Put a recurring 20-minute event in your calendar, same day every week. In it:

1. **Check the spending balance** (2 minutes). How much of this week's number is left?
2. **Decide what to do with it** (3 minutes). Roll over, or move it to a goal. If you overspent, the overspend comes out of next week's number. No guilt, just maths.
3. **Look ahead** (10 minutes). Any birthdays, trips or bills in the next two weeks? If a fund should cover it, make sure it can.
4. **One small improvement** (5 minutes). Cancel one thing, renegotiate one bill, or bump a goal by $10.

Once a month, do a slightly longer reset: confirm the automations ran, update balances, and adjust the weekly number if your pay or bills changed.

## What if the weekly number is tiny (or negative)?

That's useful information, not a failure. It means your fixed costs are high relative to your income, and no app or category system would have fixed that. Your levers, in rough order of impact:

- **Housing and transport**, the two biggest bills, at your next lease or renewal.
- **Debt minimums**, by calling lenders about hardship programs or speaking to a nonprofit credit counsellor.
- **Goals**, temporarily, while keeping at least a small emergency-fund transfer.
- **Income**: an extra shift or side work, sent straight to the gap.

## Common questions

### Should I use an app or a spreadsheet?
Whichever you'll still open next month. Apps sync transactions automatically; spreadsheets and notebooks make you look at the numbers. We compare them honestly in [app vs spreadsheet vs notebook](/blog/budgeting-app-vs-spreadsheet/).

### What about irregular income?
Budget from your lowest normal month. In good months, send the extra to a "buffer" fund first, and pay yourself the same weekly number from it in lean months.

### Is this zero-based budgeting?
It's close. Every dollar gets a job (bill, fund, goal or the weekly number), but you only track one category day to day.

### How long does setup take?
About an evening. After that, 20 minutes a week.
