**Credit utilization** is one of the biggest factors in your credit score, and one of the fastest to change. It's simply how much of your available credit card limits you're using. Unlike payment history, which builds slowly over years, utilization is recalculated whenever your card balances are reported, so lowering it can show up in your score within a month or two.

This guide explains how utilization is calculated, what counts as "good", the statement-date detail that trips up even people who pay in full, and a worked example.

## What utilization is

**Credit utilization = card balances ÷ card limits.**

If you have one card with a $2,000 limit and a $600 balance, your utilization is **30%**.

It's part of the "amounts owed" factor, which FICO says makes up about **30%** of a FICO score, second only to payment history. Scoring models look at it two ways:

- **Overall utilization:** all card balances divided by all card limits.
- **Per-card utilization:** each card on its own. One maxed-out card can hurt even if your overall number is low.

Utilization mainly applies to **revolving credit**, like credit cards and lines of credit. Instalment loans such as car loans and student loans count in the "amounts owed" category differently.

## What's a good utilization ratio?

There's no magic number, but two common guidelines:

- The CFPB notes that many experts recommend using **less than 30%** of your total credit limit.
- People with the **highest scores** often keep it **under 10%**.

Lower is generally better, with one quirk: FICO notes that in some cases a low ratio can help more than using none of your available credit at all. Using a card lightly and paying it off is a common approach.

## The statement-date catch

Here's what surprises many people: **you can pay your card in full every month and still show high utilization.**

That's because card issuers usually report your balance to the credit bureaus once a month, around your **statement closing date**, not your payment due date. If you spend $1,800 during the month and pay it in full on the due date, the bureaus may still see the $1,800 balance from the statement.

You don't pay any interest in that case. But your score treats it as a high balance.

## How to lower your utilization

### 1. Find each card's statement closing date
It's on your statement or in your card app, usually about three weeks before the payment due date.

### 2. Pay before the statement closes
Make a payment a few days before the closing date, so the balance that gets reported is low. You can still pay the rest by the due date as normal.

### 3. Spread spending across cards
If one card carries most of your spending, move some regular bills to another card to keep each card's utilization lower.

### 4. Pay down balances you carry
If you carry a balance from month to month, paying it down lowers utilization and saves interest. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)

### 5. Ask for a higher limit, carefully
A higher limit lowers your ratio if your spending stays the same. Ask your issuer whether the request involves a hard credit check, and only do it if you won't spend more.

### 6. Keep old cards open
Closing a card removes its limit from the calculation, which raises your overall utilization. If an old card has no annual fee, keep it open and use it occasionally.

## A worked example: Ines fixes the timing

Ines (a fictional example) pays both cards in full every month and never pays interest, but a credit app shows a "high utilization" warning.

**Before:**

| Card | Limit | Balance reported | Utilization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Card 1 (most spending) | $3,000 | $1,800 | 60% |
| Card 2 | $2,000 | $200 | 10% |
| **Overall** | **$5,000** | **$2,000** | **40%** |

Ines's statement shows the whole month's spending, and that's what the bureaus see, even though Ines pays it all a few weeks later.

**The fix:** three days before Card 1's statement closes, Ines pays $1,500 of the month's spending. The statement then shows **$300**.

**After:**

| Card | Limit | Balance reported | Utilization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Card 1 | $3,000 | $300 | 10% |
| Card 2 | $2,000 | $200 | 10% |
| **Overall** | **$5,000** | **$500** | **10%** |

Same spending, same money, no interest either way. Only the **timing** of one payment changed, and the reported utilization dropped from 40% to 10%. Ines sets a phone reminder for a few days before each statement date.

(Fictional example. How much a score changes depends on the whole credit report and the scoring model, and nobody can promise a particular result.)

## Utilization only matters when it's reported

Because utilization is a snapshot, a high month doesn't follow you forever. Once a lower balance is reported, many scoring models look only at the new figure, though some newer models also consider how balances have changed over time. That's different from late payments, which can stay on your reports for years.

It also means that if you're about to apply for a mortgage or car loan, paying balances down a month or two beforehand can be worthwhile. ([What is a good credit score?](/blog/what-is-a-good-credit-score/).)

## Common myths

- **"Carrying a small balance helps your score."** It doesn't. You don't need to pay interest to have a reported balance; a statement balance you then pay in full does the same job.
- **"Closing unused cards helps."** Usually the opposite: it can raise utilization and shorten your credit history.
- **"Utilization doesn't matter if you pay in full."** It still matters, because of the statement-date reporting described above.
- **"Debit card spending counts."** It doesn't. Debit cards don't affect your credit at all.

## Utilization and a new credit history

If you're building credit from scratch, utilization matters even more, because you may only have one card with a small limit. On a $300 secured card, a $100 statement balance is already 33%. Keeping a single small bill on the card, and paying before the statement if needed, keeps the ratio low. ([How to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/).)

## Business cards and authorized user cards

Some business credit cards don't report to your personal credit file unless you fall behind, so their balances may not count in your personal utilization. Authorized user cards often do appear on your report, which means a parent's or partner's high balance can raise your ratio. If that's happening, ask to be removed or ask them to pay down the card before its statement date.

## When high utilization is unavoidable

Sometimes a big expense has to go on a card: a car repair, a medical bill, a move. If that happens:

- don't panic about the score, which will recover once the balance comes down;
- focus on paying the balance off as quickly as you can, highest interest rate first;
- consider whether a lower-rate option makes sense for a large balance ([lower your card's rate](/blog/how-to-lower-your-credit-card-interest-rate/));
- avoid applying for new credit while the balance is high, if you can.

## A monthly routine that keeps utilization low

You don't need to watch your score every day. A short routine once a month does most of the work:

1. **Note each card's statement closing date** in your calendar, a few days before it. That's the balance that usually gets reported.
2. **Check each card's balance a few days before that date.** If one card is running high, make a payment before the statement closes, even if the due date is weeks away.
3. **Pay the statement balance in full by the due date** to avoid interest. Utilization affects your score; interest affects your wallet, and that matters more.
4. **Glance at your total limits once a quarter.** If an issuer lowered a limit or you closed a card, your ratio may have jumped without any change in spending.

Set an alert in each card's app for when the balance passes a level you choose, such as 25% of the limit. That way you only act when something actually needs attention.

## Common questions

### Does credit utilization include all my cards?
Scoring models usually look at both your overall utilization across all cards and the utilization on each card. One card near its limit can lower your score even if your total is low.

### How quickly does lowering utilization raise my score?
Often within a month or two, once the lower balance is reported to the credit bureaus. Utilization is based on recent balances, so changes show up faster than most other factors.

### Is 0% utilization bad?
It isn't bad, but some scoring models reward a small reported balance more than none at all. Using a card lightly and paying it off each month is a simple way to show low, non-zero utilization.

### Should I ask for a credit limit increase to lower utilization?
It can help if your spending stays the same. Ask whether the request involves a hard inquiry, and avoid it if a higher limit would tempt you to spend more.
