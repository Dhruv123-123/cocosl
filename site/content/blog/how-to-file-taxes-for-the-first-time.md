The first tax return feels much harder than it is. Most people filing for the first time have one or two forms, take the standard deduction, and are done in an hour or two. The hard part is knowing what the pieces are and what order to do them in.

This guide walks through the whole thing: what you need, how to choose a way to file, the steps in order, and a worked first return on a $38,000 salary so you can see how the numbers fit together. It covers a federal return for the 2026 tax year, filed in early 2027. Your state may want its own return as well; we'll come back to that.

## Do you even have to file?

For 2026, if you're single, under 65 and not claimed as someone's dependent, you generally have to file if your gross income was at least **$16,100**, the standard deduction for the year. There are other triggers too: if you had **$400 or more of self-employment income** (freelance, gig or side-hustle work), you have to file whatever your total income was.

But "have to" is the wrong question for most first-time filers. If your employer withheld federal income tax from your paychecks, the only way to get any of it back is to file. Plenty of students and part-time workers earn less than the filing threshold, have tax withheld anyway, and leave a refund unclaimed because nobody told them to file. If box 2 of your W-2 shows any federal tax withheld, file.

## What you'll need

Gather these before you start. Most arrive by the end of January.

- **Form W-2** from every employer you worked for during the year. Employers have to send it by January 31. Box 1 is your taxable wages; box 2 is the federal income tax already withheld.
- **Form 1099-NEC** or **1099-K** if you did freelance or gig work. Even if a client doesn't send one, the income still counts.
- **Form 1099-INT** from any bank that paid you $10 or more in interest. Interest under $10 still counts; the bank just doesn't have to send a form.
- **Form 1098-T** if you paid college tuition, and **Form 1098-E** if you paid student loan interest. Both can lower your tax.
- **Your Social Security number** and, if you want your refund fast, your **bank routing and account numbers** for direct deposit.
- **Last year's return**, if you filed one. For a true first return, you won't have it, and that's fine.

One detail that trips up students: if a parent can claim you as a dependent, you'll need to tick a box saying so, and your standard deduction may be smaller. Talk to them before you file so you don't both claim the same thing.

## Choosing how to file

You have four main options, and for a simple first return, the cheapest ones work well.

- **IRS Free File.** Guided tax software from IRS partner companies, free for federal returns if your income is under a limit the IRS sets each year. Start from the IRS website rather than a search result, so you land on the free version and not a paid upgrade.
- **Free File Fillable Forms.** Electronic versions of the paper forms, free at any income, with basic maths but no step-by-step help. Good if you're comfortable reading the form instructions.
- **Commercial tax software.** Many companies offer a free tier for simple returns; check carefully which forms and which state returns it covers before you start, because upgrades often appear halfway through. ([Tax software for simple returns](/blog/best-tax-software-for-simple-returns/), and [FreeTaxUSA vs TaxAct](/compare/freetaxusa-vs-taxact/).)
- **Free help in person.** The IRS's Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) program prepares returns for free for people with low to moderate incomes, people with disabilities and people who speak limited English. Tax Counseling for the Elderly (TCE) does the same for people 60 and older.

Paper filing is still allowed, but it's slower in both directions: the return takes longer to process, and a refund by paper check takes longer to arrive.

## Filing your first return, step by step

### 1. Confirm your filing status
Most first-time filers are **single**. If you were married on December 31, you'll choose married filing jointly or separately. Head of household has strict rules about paying for a home for a qualifying person; don't pick it just because you live alone.

### 2. Enter your income from every form
Type in each W-2 and 1099 exactly as shown. Software asks for the box numbers, so you don't need to understand every box, just copy them carefully. Leaving out a form is the most common reason the IRS sends a letter later, because it gets a copy of every one.

### 3. Take the standard deduction unless itemizing is clearly bigger
For 2026, the standard deduction is **$16,100** for single filers. You'd only itemize if your mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable gifts added up to more, which is rare for a first return.

### 4. Check for credits and deductions you qualify for
The common ones for young filers: education credits for tuition (from Form 1098-T), the student loan interest deduction (up to $2,500 of interest, from Form 1098-E), and the earned income tax credit if your income is low. Good software asks about each one.

### 5. Compare tax owed with tax withheld
The software works out your tax, then compares it with what was withheld (box 2 on your W-2s). If more was withheld than you owe, you get a refund. If less, you owe the difference.

### 6. Choose direct deposit and file electronically
E-filing with direct deposit is the fastest way to a refund. The IRS says most of those refunds are issued within 21 days.

### 7. Keep a copy
Save a PDF of the full return and the forms you used. The IRS can generally ask questions about a return for three years after you file, and next year's return is much faster with this one in front of you.

## A worked example: Jamie's first return

Jamie (a fictional example) graduated last spring and started a full-time job in the summer, after a part-time job during the last semester. Jamie is single, isn't claimed by their parents, and has these forms:

- Two W-2s that together show **$38,000** in wages (box 1) and **$2,900** of federal tax withheld (box 2).
- A 1099-INT from a high-yield savings account showing **$45** of interest.

Here's how the return comes together:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Wages | $38,000 |
| Interest | $45 |
| **Total income** | **$38,045** |
| Standard deduction (single, 2026) | −$16,100 |
| **Taxable income** | **$21,945** |

Now the tax. The US uses **brackets**, which means each slice of income is taxed at its own rate, not the whole amount at one rate. For a single filer in 2026, the first $12,400 of taxable income is taxed at 10% and the next slice at 12%:

| Slice | Rate | Tax |
|---|---|---|
| First $12,400 | 10% | $1,240 |
| Remaining $9,545 | 12% | about $1,145 |
| **Total federal tax** | | **about $2,385** |

Jamie's employers withheld $2,900, so Jamie has paid about **$515 more than the tax owed**. That's the refund. (The real return uses IRS tax tables, which round slightly differently, so the exact figure can differ by a few dollars.)

Two things are worth noticing. First, even though Jamie is "in the 12% bracket", their tax is only about 6.3% of total income, because the standard deduction and the 10% slice come first. Moving into a higher bracket never makes your whole income taxed at the higher rate. Second, the refund isn't a gift: it's Jamie's own money, withheld from paychecks and returned without interest. ([What to do with it](/blog/what-to-do-with-your-tax-refund/).)

If Jamie had also done $3,000 of freelance design work on the side, the return would change in two ways: the $3,000 would be added to income, and Jamie would owe **self-employment tax** (Social Security and Medicare for the self-employed) on it, because no employer withheld anything. That's the most common surprise for first-time filers with side income, and the reason to set some of every freelance payment aside. ([Budgeting on income that changes every month](/blog/how-to-budget-with-irregular-income/).)

## Deadlines, extensions and what happens if you're late

The deadline for 2026 returns is **April 15, 2027**. If you need more time, you can request an automatic six-month extension with **Form 4868**, free and online, by the April deadline.

An extension gives you more time to *file*, not more time to *pay*. If you think you'll owe, pay your best estimate by April 15, because interest and penalties are generally charged on whatever is unpaid after that date. If you're getting a refund, there's no penalty for filing late, but there's also no reason to wait.

If you owe and can't pay all of it, file on time anyway and pay what you can. The penalty for not filing is usually much bigger than the penalty for not paying, and the IRS offers payment plans for many people.

## Your state return

Most states with an income tax want their own return, usually due on the same day. Your W-2 shows state wages and state tax withheld in boxes 15 to 17. Many tax programs prepare the state return from the federal numbers, but check the price first: "free federal" sometimes comes with a paid state return. A few states have no income tax on wages at all, in which case there's nothing to file for wages.

If you moved states during the year or worked in one state and lived in another, you may need two state returns. That's a good case for VITA or for software that handles part-year residents.

## After you file

- **Track the refund** with "Where's My Refund?" on the IRS website, usually available about a day after an e-filed return is accepted.
- **Check your withholding for this year.** If your refund was large, or you owed, the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can suggest a new Form W-4 to give your employer. A first job that started mid-year often over-withholds, because payroll assumes you'll earn that salary for all 12 months.
- **Watch out for scams.** The IRS generally first contacts people by mail, not by text, email or social media, and it won't demand immediate payment by gift card or wire transfer.

## Common first-return mistakes

- **Leaving out a form.** Wait until every W-2 and 1099 has arrived before you file. A missing form usually means a letter from the IRS months later.
- **Typos in bank details.** A wrong digit in the account number sends the refund to the wrong place or back to the IRS, which delays it by weeks.
- **Both you and a parent claiming you.** Agree first who's claiming what. If a parent claims you, you still file your own return if you have income; you just tick the box.
- **Ignoring side income** because no 1099 arrived. All income counts, form or no form.
- **Paying for an upgrade you don't need.** If software asks for money, check whether a free option covers your forms before you pay.

## Common questions

### Do I have to file taxes if I only worked part time?
Not always, but you usually should. If your income was below the filing threshold you may not be required to file, but if federal tax was withheld from your pay, filing is the only way to get it back. With $400 or more of self-employment income, you must file.

### What if I made a mistake after I filed?
You can file an amended return on Form 1040-X. For simple maths errors, the IRS often corrects them itself and sends a notice. If you forgot a W-2 or 1099, amend as soon as you notice.

### Can I file my taxes for free?
Yes, many people can. IRS Free File offers guided software for free below an income limit, Free File Fillable Forms is free at any income, and VITA volunteers prepare returns for free for people with low to moderate incomes. Many commercial programs also have free tiers for simple returns.

### How long should I keep my tax records?
Generally at least three years after you file, since that's how long the IRS usually has to ask questions about a return. Keep the return itself, your W-2s and 1099s, and receipts for anything you deducted.
