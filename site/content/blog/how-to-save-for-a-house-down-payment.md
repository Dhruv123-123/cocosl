"How do I save for a house?" is one of the most common money questions in America, and the honest first answer is: probably for longer, and for more than the down payment alone. The good news is that the target is usually smaller than the 20% you've heard about, and a clear plan makes it far less daunting.

This guide covers how much cash you really need, the down payment options, where to keep the money while you save, and a worked three-year plan.

## How much down payment do you need?

Less than 20%, in most cases. Common options, according to the CFPB:

- **Conventional loans:** many lenders accept 5% to 15% down, and some programs allow 3% for qualifying buyers. Below 20% down, you'll usually pay **private mortgage insurance (PMI)**, a monthly cost added to your payment until you build enough equity.
- **FHA loans:** as little as 3.5% down in most cases, with lower minimum credit scores than most conventional loans, plus mortgage insurance.
- **VA and USDA loans:** zero-down options for eligible veterans and service members, and for eligible buyers in rural areas.
- **State and local down payment assistance** programs, which vary a lot and are worth checking before you set your target.

So why does everyone talk about 20%? Because it avoids PMI on a conventional loan, lowers your monthly payment and means you start with more equity. It's a good goal, not a requirement. Waiting years to reach 20% while rents and prices rise isn't automatically the cheaper choice; neither is buying with the bare minimum and no cushion. The right number is the one that leaves you with a payment you can afford *and* savings left over.

## The cash you really need: four buckets

The down payment is only the biggest of four amounts:

1. **Down payment:** the percentage of the price you put in.
2. **Closing costs:** fees for the loan, title, appraisal, inspections and taxes, typically **2% to 5% of the price**, paid at closing.
3. **Move-in money:** movers, basic repairs, the things a home needs that an apartment didn't (a lawnmower, a ladder, window coverings). A couple of thousand dollars goes fast.
4. **An emergency fund that stays untouched:** the CFPB suggests having at least three months of living expenses saved before you move in. Homes break, and there's no landlord to call.

The first three are your savings target. The fourth is a separate account you don't spend.

## Build your savings plan

### 1. Pick a realistic price range
Look at what homes you'd actually buy cost in your area, and what payment you could carry. A lender's pre-approval tells you the most you could borrow, which is not the same as what fits your budget.

### 2. Choose your down payment percentage
3%, 3.5%, 5%, 10% or 20%. Run the monthly payment at each, including PMI and property tax, before you choose.

### 3. Add closing costs and move-in money
Use 3% of the price for closing costs as a planning number, plus a fixed amount for moving and setting up.

### 4. Subtract what you've already saved
Only count money set aside for the house, not your emergency fund.

### 5. Divide by the months until you want to buy
That's your monthly savings target. If it's not possible, change the price, the timeline or the percentage, not the emergency fund.

## A worked example: Casey and Morgan's 3-year plan

Casey and Morgan (a fictional couple) rent together and want to buy in about three years. Homes they'd buy in their area cost around **$280,000**. They already have a **$6,000 emergency fund** (which they'll leave alone) and **$5,000** saved toward a house.

**The 5% plan:**

| Bucket | Amount |
|---|---|
| Down payment (5% of $280,000) | $14,000 |
| Closing costs (about 3%) | $8,400 |
| Moving and setting up | $2,000 |
| **Target** | **$24,400** |

They keep the savings in a high-yield account. Assuming a hypothetical 4% APY for three years, their existing $5,000 grows to about $5,620, so they need about $18,780 more. Spread over 36 months, that's about **$492 a month**, or roughly $246 each.

**The 10% plan:** a $28,000 down payment pushes the target to $38,400 and the monthly savings to about **$859**. That's a big jump, so they run the numbers both ways: 10% down means a smaller loan and lower PMI, but 5% down means buying on time with the same emergency fund intact. They choose the 5% plan, with a rule that any raises or bonuses go to the house fund, which could take them closer to 10% without straining the monthly budget.

**What makes the $492 possible:** they find it the way most people do, not in one place but several. They redirect the $150 a month they'd been spending on a second streaming bundle and takeout, Casey's two extra paychecks each year (being paid every two weeks means two months with three paychecks) go straight to the house fund, and they increase the transfer each time either of them gets a raise.

## How much house can you afford?

Lenders will tell you the most you can borrow, usually based on your debt-to-income ratio. That number is a ceiling, not a target. A more comfortable check is to add up the full monthly cost of owning: mortgage payment, property tax, homeowners insurance, PMI if you're under 20% down, HOA fees, and a maintenance allowance. Then compare it with your current rent plus what you're saving each month for the house. If the total is close to that combined figure, you've already proved you can carry it, because you've been doing it for months.

## Where to keep a down payment fund

The rule of thumb: money you need within about three to five years shouldn't be in the stock market. A 20% market drop the year before you buy could set you back a year or more.

Good homes for house money:

- **High-yield savings account:** easy access, FDIC-insured at an insured bank, rate changes with the market. The simplest choice. ([How to open one](/blog/how-to-open-a-high-yield-savings-account/).)
- **Certificates of deposit (CDs):** a fixed rate for a fixed term. Good if you know your buying date; there's usually a penalty for withdrawing early.
- **Treasury bills:** short-term US government debt, bought through TreasuryDirect or a brokerage. Interest is exempt from state and local income tax.
- **Money market funds:** in a brokerage account, similar yields to savings accounts but not FDIC-insured. ([Savings vs money market, compared](/blog/high-yield-savings-vs-money-market/).)

Many savers use a mix: a high-yield savings account for the next 12 months of contributions, and CDs or T-bills timed to mature around the expected purchase date.

## Ways to speed it up

- **Automate the transfer** for the day after payday, so the house fund is paid before you are.
- **Point windfalls at it:** tax refunds, bonuses, gifts, the extra paychecks. ([What to do with a tax refund](/blog/what-to-do-with-your-tax-refund/).)
- **Lower your biggest cost temporarily.** A cheaper rental or a roommate for the saving years can shave a year off the timeline.
- **Check first-time buyer programs** in your state and city before you set the final target. Some offer grants or forgivable loans toward the down payment or closing costs.
- **Clear high-interest debt first.** It raises your monthly cash and improves your mortgage rate. Lenders look closely at your debt compared with your income.

And a word of caution: be careful about taking money from a 401(k) for a down payment. Loans and withdrawals can carry taxes, penalties or repayment rules, and they reduce your retirement savings. Some plans and the rules for IRAs have first-time buyer provisions, but read the details (or ask a tax professional) before you rely on them.

## Rent vs buy: run the comparison

Buying isn't automatically better than renting. Owning comes with property tax, insurance, maintenance (a common planning number is 1% of the home's value a year) and transaction costs that take years to earn back. A rent-vs-buy calculator that includes all of those, and the return on investing the money you'd otherwise put into a down payment, gives you an honest break-even year. If you expect to move within a few years, renting often wins.

## Common questions

### How much should I save for a down payment?
Many buyers put down 3% to 10%, not 20%. Add 2% to 5% of the price for closing costs and some money for moving, and keep a separate emergency fund. Below 20% down on a conventional loan, expect to pay PMI.

### Where should I save for a house?
In safe, accessible accounts: a high-yield savings account, CDs timed to your buying date, or Treasury bills. Avoid the stock market for money you'll need within about three to five years.

### Is it better to wait until I have 20% down?
Not always. 20% avoids PMI and lowers your payment, but waiting years can cost more if prices and rents rise. Compare the monthly payment and total cost at different down payments, and never buy without an emergency fund left over.

### Can I use my 401(k) for a down payment?
Some plans allow loans or hardship withdrawals, and IRAs have some first-time buyer rules, but these can carry taxes, penalties or repayment requirements and they shrink your retirement savings. Get the specific rules for your plan before deciding.
