Most budgeting advice quietly assumes the same paycheck arrives every two weeks. If you freelance, drive for a delivery app, work on commission, earn tips, or run a small business, it doesn't. One month is great, the next is thin, and a normal budget either feels pointless in the good months or impossible in the bad ones.

The fix isn't a cleverer spreadsheet. It's changing the shape of the money: instead of spending income as it arrives, you pay it into a holding account and pay yourself a **steady salary** from there. This guide shows how to set that up, how much tax to set aside, and a worked example with twelve months of real-looking income.

## Why ordinary budgets fail on irregular income

Two things go wrong.

**The good months get spent.** A $7,000 month feels like a raise, so spending rises to match it. Then a $2,600 month arrives and there's nothing left from the good one.

**Tax isn't taken out.** Employees have tax withheld from every paycheck. Freelancers and gig workers usually don't, so a big chunk of every payment belongs to the IRS and the state, but it sits in your checking account looking like spending money until April.

Both are timing problems, and the system below fixes both.

## The system in one paragraph

All income goes into one **holding account**. From each payment, a fixed percentage moves straight to a **tax account**. On the same day each month, you pay yourself a **fixed salary** from the holding account into your everyday checking. Your budget runs on that salary, exactly as if you had a regular job. In good months, the holding account grows. In thin months, it covers the gap. Once it has a few months of salary in it, the bad months stop being emergencies.

## Set up the system

### 1. Find your bare-bones number
Add up what you must spend in a month to live: rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport, minimum debt payments, phone. Leave out everything optional. This is the floor your salary must never fall below.

### 2. Look at the last 12 months of income
Use bank deposits or invoices, before tax. Write down each month. Work out the average, and note the lowest three months.

### 3. Pick a tax set-aside percentage
Self-employed people pay income tax plus **self-employment tax** (Social Security and Medicare, about 15.3% on most net earnings). Many freelancers set aside 25% to 30% of each payment as a starting point. Your real rate depends on your income, deductions and state, so check it against last year's return or with a tax professional, and adjust.

### 4. Choose your salary
Start with a salary you could pay yourself even in a below-average year: somewhere between your bare-bones number and your average after-tax income. Being conservative at first is the point. You can raise it later.

### 5. Open the accounts
You need three: a **holding account** (a separate savings account that receives all income), a **tax account** (another savings account you don't touch except to pay tax), and your normal **checking account** for the salary. Online banks make it easy to open and name several accounts. ([How to open a high-yield savings account](/blog/how-to-open-a-high-yield-savings-account/).)

### 6. Automate what you can
Have clients and platforms pay into the holding account. Set a monthly transfer of your salary to checking on the same date. If your bank allows rules, move the tax percentage automatically; otherwise do it by hand the day each payment arrives.

### 7. Build the buffer before you raise the salary
Aim for at least two months of salary in the holding account, then three. Until the buffer is there, don't increase the salary, however good a month looks.

## A worked example: Robin's year

Robin (a fictional example) is a freelance designer. Here is Robin's income, before tax, over the past 12 months:

| Month | Income |
|---|---|
| 1 | $3,100 |
| 2 | $5,800 |
| 3 | $4,200 |
| 4 | $2,600 |
| 5 | $6,900 |
| 6 | $4,400 |
| 7 | $3,800 |
| 8 | $5,200 |
| 9 | $2,900 |
| 10 | $7,400 |
| 11 | $4,600 |
| 12 | $3,500 |
| **Total** | **$54,400** |

That's an average of about **$4,533 a month**, but the range runs from $2,600 to $7,400, and the three worst months average about $2,867.

**Tax:** Robin sets aside **25%** of every payment. On the year, that's $13,600 into the tax account, leaving about **$3,400 a month** on average after tax.

**Bare bones:** Robin's must-pay costs are about **$2,500** a month.

**Salary:** Robin picks **$3,000** a month: comfortably above bare bones, and about $400 below the average after-tax income. That gap is what grows the buffer over time.

Here's how the first six months play out, starting from a holding account with **$3,000** in it (one month of salary, from savings):

| Month | Income | Tax (25%) | Salary paid | Holding account at month end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | | | | $3,000 |
| 1 | $3,100 | $775 | $3,000 | $2,325 |
| 2 | $5,800 | $1,450 | $3,000 | $3,675 |
| 3 | $4,200 | $1,050 | $3,000 | $3,825 |
| 4 | $2,600 | $650 | $3,000 | $2,775 |
| 5 | $6,900 | $1,725 | $3,000 | $4,950 |
| 6 | $4,400 | $1,100 | $3,000 | $5,250 |

Two things to notice. In month 4, Robin's worst month, the salary still arrived in full; the holding account simply dipped. And by month 6, the holding account has grown to about **$5,250**, well on the way to the two-month buffer of $6,000.

Over the full year, the holding account gains roughly $4,800 (the $40,800 after tax, minus $36,000 of salary). Once it reaches three months of salary ($9,000), Robin has a choice: raise the salary, or move the extra into an emergency fund, retirement savings or a business equipment fund. Either way, it's a decision made calmly, not in the glow of one good month.

## Taxes: the part that hurts if you skip it

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax for the year, the IRS generally expects you to pay **quarterly estimated taxes** instead of all at once in April. The due dates for each tax year are usually **April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15** of the following year (moved to the next business day when a date falls on a weekend or holiday).

Paying too little during the year can mean an underpayment penalty, even if you pay everything by April. A common way to stay safe is the "safe harbor": paying at least as much as last year's total tax in four equal pieces (110% of it if your adjusted gross income was over $150,000). Then, whatever this year's income turns out to be, you generally won't owe a penalty, though you may still owe a balance in April.

Your tax account makes this easy: each quarter, you pay the IRS from money that was set aside for exactly that. At the end of the year, compare what you set aside with what you actually owed, and adjust the percentage. Your state may have its own estimated tax schedule too.

Keep good records as you go: business expenses like software, equipment and part of your phone bill can reduce your taxable income, but only if you can show them.

## Handling the very bad month

Even with a buffer, a long dry spell can drain the holding account. Have a plan ready before it happens:

1. **Drop to the bare-bones salary.** Robin can go from $3,000 to $2,500 for a few months without missing a bill.
2. **Pause extras automatically.** Sinking funds and extra debt payments stop until the buffer recovers. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)
3. **Keep the tax account separate.** It's tempting to borrow from it. If you must, write down the amount and repay it first when income returns.
4. **Look at the pipeline, not just the balance.** Invoices sent but unpaid, contracts coming up, seasonal patterns. Many freelancers have a predictable slow season; plan the buffer around it.

## Mixed income: a job plus side work

If you have a regular paycheck plus irregular side income, you can use a simpler version: budget on the paycheck alone, and send side income to a holding account. Set aside the tax share first, then use the rest for goals: debt payoff, an emergency fund, or a specific purchase. The budget never depends on side income, so a slow side month costs nothing.

## Tools that help

You don't need special software. Any bank that lets you open several named savings accounts at no cost will do. A simple spreadsheet with three columns (income received, tax set aside, salary paid) is enough to track the holding account. Some budgeting apps let you budget only money you already have, which suits irregular income well. ([How to choose a budgeting app](/blog/how-to-choose-a-budgeting-app/), or [YNAB vs EveryDollar](/compare/ynab-vs-everydollar/).)

And on the spending side, the same weekly spending number that works for salaried people works here, because your salary is now regular. ([The four-line budget](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/).)

## Common questions

### How much should I pay myself from irregular income?
Start with a salary you could cover in a below-average year: above your bare-bones costs but below your average after-tax income. The difference builds a buffer. Raise the salary only once the holding account has two or three months of pay in it.

### How much should freelancers set aside for taxes?
Many set aside 25% to 30% of each payment as a starting point, because self-employed people pay self-employment tax on top of income tax. The right figure depends on your income, deductions and state. Check it against your actual return each year and adjust.

### What if I have no savings to start the holding account?
Start the system anyway with a lower salary for the first few months, close to your bare-bones number. Every month that income beats the salary, the buffer grows. A tax refund or one strong month can also seed it.

### Do I need to pay quarterly estimated taxes?
Generally, if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax for the year after withholding and credits. Paying quarterly helps you avoid an underpayment penalty. The IRS website has the current due dates and a worksheet.
