Life insurance is one of those things many people know they "should" think about and then put off, partly because the options are confusing. The most important choice is between **term** and **permanent** (often **whole**) life insurance, and for many households the answer is simpler than the sales pitches suggest.

This guide explains how each type works, who needs life insurance in the first place, how to estimate the amount, and what to check before you buy.

## Do you need life insurance?

Life insurance pays money to the people you choose (your beneficiaries) when you die. The key question is: **would anyone struggle financially if your income or unpaid work stopped?**

You're more likely to need it if:

- you have **children** or other dependants;
- a **partner** relies on your income to pay the mortgage or rent;
- you provide **unpaid care**, such as childcare, that would be expensive to replace;
- someone else has **co-signed debts** with you.

You may not need it, or need much, if nobody depends on your income and you have enough savings to cover final expenses. New Jersey's insurance department lists reasons to buy, including replacing income lost when a wage earner dies and making sure dependants don't take on significant debt.

## How term life insurance works

**Term** insurance covers you for a set period, often 10, 20 or 30 years. If you die during the term, your beneficiaries receive the death benefit. If you outlive the term, the coverage ends and nothing is paid.

- The NAIC (the National Association of Insurance Commissioners) says term **generally has lower premiums in the early years**.
- New Jersey's insurance department notes term **doesn't build up cash value** you can use in the future.

Term is designed to cover the years when people depend on you most: while children are growing up or the mortgage is being paid down.

## How whole and other permanent life insurance works

**Permanent** life insurance (whole life, universal life and variable universal life) is designed to last your whole life, as long as premiums are paid.

- The NAIC says permanent insurance **provides long-term financial protection**, and because of its savings element, **premiums tend to be higher**.
- Many permanent policies build **cash value** that grows over time and can sometimes be borrowed against or withdrawn, which reduces the death benefit.

Permanent insurance can make sense in specific situations: a lifelong dependant, certain estate planning needs, or business arrangements. Those are worth discussing with an independent professional.

## Term vs whole life at a glance

| | Term life | Whole life |
|---|---|---|
| How long it lasts | A set number of years | Your whole life, if premiums are paid |
| Cost | Lower premiums, especially early on | Much higher premiums for the same death benefit |
| Cash value | None | Builds cash value |
| Simplicity | Simple | More complex, with fees and policy terms to understand |
| Best for | Covering income during working and child-raising years | Lifelong needs, some estate or business planning |

## How to work out how much life insurance you need

### 1. Start with a rule of thumb
The NAIC notes some insurance experts suggest buying **five to eight times your current income**, but says working through your actual needs gives a more accurate figure.

### 2. Add up what your family would need
Consider years of income support, the remaining mortgage or rent, childcare, education goals, debts and final expenses.

### 3. Subtract what you already have
Take off savings, investments and any workplace life insurance (which often ends if you leave your job).

### 4. Pick a term that matches the need
Choose a term that lasts until your youngest child is independent or the mortgage is paid off.

### 5. Revisit after big life changes
A new child, a new home, a divorce or a big raise can change how much you need.

## A worked example: Gus works out his cover

Gus (a fictional example) is 32, earns **$60,000** a year, and has a partner and two children aged 2 and 5. The rule of thumb gives **$300,000 to $480,000**. Gus works through his family's actual needs:

| Need | Amount |
|---|---|
| Income support: $30,000 a year for 10 years | $300,000 |
| Remaining mortgage | $210,000 |
| Childcare and education fund | $60,000 |
| Final expenses | $15,000 |
| **Total needs** | **$585,000** |
| Minus savings | −$25,000 |
| Minus workplace life insurance | −$60,000 |
| **Coverage to buy** | **$500,000** |

He gets two **hypothetical quotes** (illustrative, not real offers): a 20-year, $500,000 term policy at about **$28 a month**, and a $500,000 whole life policy at about **$390 a month**.

Gus chooses the term policy. Of the roughly $360 a month he doesn't spend on whole life, he puts some into his retirement plan and some into the family's emergency fund. ([How to build an emergency fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)

## Shopping tips

The NAIC's shopping advice includes:

- **Feel confident in the agent and the company** before you buy.
- **Don't buy life insurance unless you intend to stick with your plan**: dropping a policy early can waste what you've paid.
- **Review the application carefully** before signing, to be sure the answers are complete and accurate.

A few more things to check:

- **Compare quotes** from several insurers for the same amount and term.
- **Check the insurer's financial strength** and that it's licensed in your state.
- **Name beneficiaries** (and backups) and keep them up to date.
- **Know what's excluded** and what happens if you miss a payment.

## Workplace life insurance

Many employers offer free life insurance, often one or two times your salary, with the option to buy more. It's useful, but it usually ends when you leave the job and may not be enough on its own. Treat it as a top-up, not the whole plan. ([First job 401(k) setup](/blog/first-job-401k-setup/) covers other benefits worth checking at a new job.)

## How the buying process works

Buying life insurance usually follows the same steps:

1. **Get quotes** for the amount and term you want, from several insurers or through an independent agent.
2. **Apply**, answering questions about your health, lifestyle, job and family history. Answer accurately: wrong answers can give the insurer grounds to refuse a claim.
3. **Medical underwriting**: some policies need a short medical exam or health records; others use only your answers and existing data.
4. **Offer**: the insurer approves you at a price based on your risk class, which may differ from the quote.
5. **Accept and pay** the first premium to put the policy in force.

Once you have the policy, store it with your other important documents and tell your beneficiaries which insurer to contact.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Buying only the workplace policy** and assuming it's enough.
- **Waiting too long.** Premiums are based partly on age and health, so they tend to be lower when you're younger.
- **Forgetting beneficiaries.** If a beneficiary has died or you've divorced, update the policy; it usually overrides a will.
- **Letting a policy lapse.** Set up automatic payments and check the grace period for missed premiums.
- **Buying more complexity than you need.** If you can't explain how a policy works, don't buy it yet.

## Life insurance and your other plans

Life insurance works best as part of a wider plan. Make sure your partner or a trusted person knows where your policies, accounts and important documents are kept. Review beneficiaries on your retirement accounts too, since those usually pass directly to the named person. If you have young children, a will that names a guardian is just as important as the policy.

## Common questions

### Is whole life insurance a good investment?
For most people, it's best to think of life insurance as protection, not investment. Whole life builds cash value, but premiums are much higher, and fees and policy terms can be complex. Many people buy term insurance and invest separately. If you're considering whole life, compare it carefully and get independent advice.

### What happens when a term policy ends?
The coverage stops and no money is paid out. Some term policies let you renew (usually at a much higher price) or convert to a permanent policy without a new medical exam. Check whether your policy has those options.

### Do stay-at-home parents need life insurance?
Often, yes. Replacing childcare, household management and other unpaid work can be expensive. Many families buy a term policy for a stay-at-home parent to cover those costs.

### Can I have more than one life insurance policy?
Yes. Some people combine a workplace policy with an individual term policy, or "ladder" two term policies of different lengths so coverage decreases as their needs fall.
