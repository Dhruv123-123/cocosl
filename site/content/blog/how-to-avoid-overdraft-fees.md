An overdraft fee is one of the most expensive charges in everyday banking. Many banks and credit unions charge **$30 or more** each time your account goes below zero, and several small purchases on the same day can each trigger one. A $5 coffee can end up costing $40.

The good news is that most overdraft fees can be avoided with a few settings and one habit. This guide explains how overdrafts work, the setting most people have never checked, and a backup plan for tight weeks.

## How overdrafts work

When you try to spend more than is in your checking account, the bank can do one of three things:

1. **Decline the transaction.** You're embarrassed at the checkout, but you pay no overdraft fee.
2. **Pay it anyway and charge an overdraft fee**, then expect you to bring the account back above zero.
3. **Return a check or electronic payment unpaid**, often with a non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee, and the company you were paying may add its own returned-payment fee.

Which one happens depends on the type of transaction and your account settings.

## The setting that matters most: debit card opt-in

Under federal rules, banks and credit unions **can't charge overdraft fees on everyday debit card purchases and ATM withdrawals unless you've opted in** to overdraft coverage for them. If you haven't opted in, those transactions are usually just declined.

Many people opted in when they opened their account without realising. You can change this at any time by contacting your bank, and often in the app's settings.

Opting out doesn't stop overdrafts from **checks and automatic payments** (like bill autopay). Those can still be paid with a fee or returned, depending on your bank. That's why the next steps matter too.

## How to stop overdraft fees

### 1. Check your overdraft settings
Log in or call your bank and ask: "Am I opted in to overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions? What's your fee, how many can you charge per day, and is there a grace period?" Turn the opt-in off if you'd rather be declined than charged.

### 2. Link a savings account as backup
Many banks offer **overdraft protection transfers**: if checking runs short, money moves from a linked savings account to cover it. There may be a transfer fee, but it's usually lower than an overdraft fee, and some banks charge nothing.

### 3. Turn on low-balance alerts
Set a text or app alert for when your balance drops below an amount, like $100. It's the single best early warning.

### 4. Time your bills after payday
Move autopay dates for rent, phone, insurance and loans to a few days after you're paid. Most companies let you choose the date. ([Budgeting when you're paid every two weeks](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/).)

### 5. Keep a buffer in checking
Leave a small cushion, like $100 to $200, that you treat as zero. It absorbs timing problems and forgotten charges.

### 6. Watch pending transactions
Your "available balance" may not include pending card holds or checks that haven't cleared. Track what's coming out, not just what's shown.

### 7. Consider an account without overdraft fees
Some banks and credit unions have removed overdraft fees or offer accounts that simply decline transactions. If yours charges high fees, switching may be worth it. ([Best bank accounts for college students](/blog/best-bank-account-for-college-students/).)

## A worked example: Esme's expensive Thursday

Esme (a fictional example) has **$60** in checking on the Thursday before payday. During the day:

| Transaction | Amount | Balance after |
|---|---|---|
| Groceries (debit card) | $45.00 | $15.00 |
| Lunch (debit card) | $12.00 | $3.00 |
| Ride home (debit card) | $15.00 | −$12.00 |
| Streaming renewal | $9.99 | −$21.99 |
| Phone bill (autopay) | $30.00 | −$51.99 |

**If Esme had opted in to debit overdraft coverage**, and the bank charges $35 per overdraft, the ride, the streaming renewal and the phone bill could each trigger a fee: **$105 in fees for being about $52 short.**

**Without the opt-in**, the ride is declined at the point of payment and costs nothing. The streaming and phone autopays may still be paid with a fee, or returned with an NSF fee, depending on the bank. Fewer fees, but not zero.

**With a linked savings account**, the shortfall is covered by one transfer, often for a small fee or free.

**With a $200 buffer** in checking, nothing goes negative at all. One evening of moving money a few months earlier would have saved $105.

## If you've already been charged

- **Bring the account above zero quickly.** Some banks waive the fee if you do it within a day or two, and some charge extra fees if you don't.
- **Ask for a refund.** Call and politely ask the bank to waive the fee, especially if it's your first or it was a small amount. Many will, once.
- **Look for the pattern.** If it keeps happening, it's usually a timing issue between bills and paydays, which the steps above fix.

## If it happens because there isn't enough money

Sometimes overdrafts aren't a timing problem: the money just doesn't stretch to payday. In that case, fees make a tight month even tighter. A few things that help:

- a **weekly spending number** so you see the shortfall coming ([how to stop living paycheck to paycheck](/blog/how-to-stop-living-paycheck-to-paycheck/));
- calling billers before a due date to ask for a new date or a payment arrangement;
- building even a small buffer, starting at $10 or $20 a week ([how much emergency fund you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/)).

Avoid payday loans to cover overdrafts: their costs are usually much higher than the fees you're trying to avoid.

## How transaction order can make it worse

Banks process the day's transactions in a particular order, and the order can change how many fees you're charged. Debit card purchases may be authorised at the checkout but post a day or two later, while autopays can arrive early in the morning. A gas station or hotel may place a temporary hold larger than your actual purchase, which reduces your available balance until it's released.

You don't need to memorise your bank's rules. Just remember that the balance in your app may not include everything that's on its way, and keep your buffer for exactly this reason.

## Checking accounts built to avoid overdrafts

If overdrafts keep happening, look for an account designed to prevent them. Some are sold as "checkless" or "second chance" accounts and simply decline transactions you can't cover, rather than charging a fee. Some credit unions and online banks also offer low-cost accounts with free overdraft transfers or no overdraft fees at all. Read the fee schedule before you open one.

## Overdraft lines of credit

Some banks offer an **overdraft line of credit**: a small loan that covers shortfalls automatically. You pay interest (and sometimes a fee) instead of a flat overdraft charge, which is usually cheaper for small amounts. But it's still borrowing, so treat it as a backup, not part of your monthly budget.

## A 20-minute overdraft-proofing session

If you only do one thing after reading this, set aside 20 minutes this week:

1. Open your banking app and find the overdraft settings. Note whether you're opted in and what the fee is.
2. Link a savings account for overdraft transfers, even if it only has a small balance.
3. Set a low-balance alert at $100 or another amount that gives you time to react.
4. List your autopays and their dates, and move any that fall just before payday.
5. Move $20 into checking as the start of a buffer, and set a small weekly transfer to build it.

That's it. Most people who do this never pay an overdraft fee again, and the ones who do usually see it coming.

## Common questions

### Can I turn off overdraft protection?
Yes. You can opt out of overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions at any time by contacting your bank. Those transactions will then usually be declined instead of creating a fee. Ask separately about checks and automatic payments.

### How many overdraft fees can a bank charge in one day?
It depends on the bank. Many charge for each transaction that overdraws the account, and some set a daily limit on the number of fees. Check your account's fee schedule or ask your bank.

### Does an overdraft hurt my credit score?
An overdraft itself isn't usually reported to the credit bureaus. But if you leave a negative balance unpaid, the bank may close the account and send it to collections, which can show up on your credit report.

### Is a linked savings account better than overdraft coverage?
Usually, yes. A transfer from your own savings typically costs less than an overdraft fee, and some banks don't charge for it at all. It only works if there's money in the savings account.
