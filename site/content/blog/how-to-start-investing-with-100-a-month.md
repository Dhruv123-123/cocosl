A hundred dollars a month doesn't sound like investing. It sounds like a phone bill. But money invested in your twenties gets the one thing nobody can buy later: time.

As a hypothetical, **$100 a month for 40 years at a 7% average annual return** grows to roughly **$262,000**, from $48,000 of contributions. Returns aren't guaranteed and real markets go up and down, but the maths of starting early is hard to argue with.

Here's the whole setup, in the order that makes sense.

## Step 0: Before you invest

Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years. Before putting money in the market:

1. **Have a starter emergency fund**, so a car repair doesn't force you to sell investments at a bad time. [Here's an 8-week plan for $1,000](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).
2. **Deal with high-interest debt.** Paying off a credit card at 22% APR is a guaranteed 22% "return". Few investments promise anything like it. See [avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).
3. **Take any employer 401(k) match first.** It's often an instant 50–100% on what you put in. See [how a 401(k) match works](/blog/401k-match-explained/).

## Step 1: Pick the account

The account is the container; the investments go inside it.

| Account | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **401(k) / 403(b)** at work | Getting the match | Contributions come from your paycheck |
| **Roth IRA** | Most beginners with earned income | Contributions can come back out any time; growth tax-free if rules are met |
| **Traditional IRA** | Higher earners wanting a deduction now | Taxed on the way out |
| **Taxable brokerage account** | Goals before retirement, or after maxing the above | No special tax benefits, no limits |

Not sure between Roth and Traditional? [It mostly comes down to one question](/blog/roth-vs-traditional-ira/).

## Step 2: Open it (about 15 minutes)

Choose a large brokerage with **no account fees, no minimums, fractional shares or low fund minimums, and low-cost index funds**. You'll need your Social Security number, employer details and bank account details. Then link your bank.

## Step 3: Choose what to buy

This is where people freeze. Two simple options:

### Option A: One target-date fund

A [target-date fund](https://www.finra.org/investors/learn-to-invest/types-investments/investment-funds/target-date-funds) holds a mix of stock and bond funds built around the year you expect to retire (e.g. "2065"). It starts mostly in stocks and gradually shifts toward bonds as that year approaches, rebalancing automatically. One fund, done. Check its expense ratio; index-based versions are usually cheapest.

### Option B: A broad index fund

An index fund buys everything in a market index, such as the total US stock market or the whole world's stock market. One share gives you a tiny slice of every company in it. Many people pair a total stock market fund with a bond fund, but a single broad fund is a fine start.

Whichever you pick, check the **[expense ratio](/blog/expense-ratios-explained/)**. On a long horizon, the difference between 0.03% and 1% is huge.

## Step 4: Actually invest the money

This is the step people miss. **Depositing money into a brokerage account isn't the same as investing it.** Deposits usually land as cash (sometimes called a "settlement fund" or "core position"). You need to buy the fund.

Check your account: under "positions" or "holdings" you should see the fund you chose, not just cash.

## Step 5: Automate it

Set an **automatic monthly transfer** from your bank and an **automatic investment** into your chosen fund on the same schedule. Put the date a day or two after payday. Now it happens whether or not you remember.

Then increase it whenever your income goes up: a raise, a paid-off debt, a cancelled subscription.

## Step 6: What to do when the market drops

It will. Markets have fallen sharply many times and recovered over long periods, though there's no guarantee about timing. When it happens:

- **Don't check your balance daily.** Once a month at your money check-in is plenty.
- **Keep the automatic contributions going.** You're buying at lower prices.
- **Don't sell because it's down**, unless your goals or timeline changed.

The single most expensive investing mistake for many beginners isn't picking the "wrong" fund. It's selling in a panic.

## How $100 a month can grow (hypothetical)

| Years | You put in | Value at 7% a year (hypothetical) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | $12,000 | ~$17,000 |
| 30 | $36,000 | ~$122,000 |
| 40 | $48,000 | ~$262,000 |

*Monthly contributions, monthly compounding, 7% annual return assumed for illustration, before fees and taxes. Not a prediction. Try your own numbers with the SEC's Investor.gov compound interest calculator.*

## Common questions

### Is $100 a month really worth it?
Yes. The habit matters more than the amount, and you can increase it later. Small amounts started early often beat larger amounts started late.

### Should I buy individual stocks?
We don't cover stock picking. Diversified, low-cost funds spread your risk across hundreds or thousands of companies.

### What about crypto?
Out of scope here. It's highly volatile and speculative; it isn't a substitute for a diversified retirement account.

### How often should I check my investments?
Monthly or quarterly is plenty. Make sure contributions arrived and were invested, then close the app.
