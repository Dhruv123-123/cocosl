Scam texts are now one of the most common ways criminals try to steal money and personal information. They're short, they look official, and they arrive on the device you carry everywhere. Many follow the same handful of scripts: a package problem, an unpaid toll, a bank alert, a job offer or a prize.

This guide explains the red flags, the most common types, how to check a message safely, and exactly what to do if you've already clicked.

## The red flags

The FTC lists several signs of a scam text. Most scam texts show at least two of these:

- **It's unexpected.** You didn't order anything, didn't expect a refund, didn't apply for that job.
- **It's urgent.** "Pay today," "your account will be closed," "final notice."
- **It asks for information or money.** Passwords, account numbers, your Social Security number, a card number, or a payment. The FTC notes that legitimate companies won't ask for your account information by text.
- **It wants you to click a link or reply.** Often a short or odd-looking link, or a "reply YES to confirm."
- **It's too good to be true.** A free gift card, prize or loan approval you didn't apply for.
- **It comes from an odd number or email address**, not a short code you recognise.

## The common types

### Delivery scams
"Your package is on hold. Update your address here." Real carriers usually don't text you about a delivery you didn't sign up to track, and they don't charge redelivery fees by text link.

### Toll and parking scams
"You have an unpaid toll balance of $4.15. Pay now to avoid a late fee." The small amount is designed to make you pay without thinking, and the payment page captures your card details.

### Bank and card alerts
"Did you make a $989 purchase? Reply NO to stop it." Replying or calling the number in the text connects you with the scammer, who then "helps" you move your money or reveal a code.

### Job and task scams
Messages offering easy pay for liking videos or reviewing products, which later ask you to deposit money to "unlock" earnings.

### Wrong-number messages
A friendly "Hi, is this Sam?" that turns into a long conversation, then an investment or cryptocurrency pitch.

## What to do with a suspicious text

### 1. Don't click, don't reply
Not even "STOP" on a message you don't recognise: it confirms your number is active.

### 2. Check independently
If it might be real, go to the company's official app or website yourself, or call a number you already know, like the one on the back of your card. Never use the link or number in the text.

### 3. Forward it to 7726
Forwarding the text to **7726** (which spells SPAM) helps your mobile carrier block similar messages.

### 4. Report it
Use your messaging app's "report junk" option, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

### 5. Delete it
Once reported, delete the message so you don't tap it by mistake later.

## A worked example: Otto's week of texts

Otto (a fictional example) receives three texts in one week:

| Text | Red flags | What Otto does |
|---|---|---|
| "USPS: your package is held. Update your address: [short link]" | Unexpected, link, urgency | Opens the official carrier app: nothing is being delivered |
| "Toll balance due $4.15. Pay today to avoid a $50 fee: [link]" | Urgent, small payment, link | Logs in to the toll account through the app Otto already uses: balance $0 |
| "Bank alert: did you attempt a $989 purchase? Reply YES or NO" | Unexpected, asks for a reply | Calls the number on the back of the card: no such purchase |

Otto forwards all three to 7726, reports them at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and deletes them. Total time: about ten minutes, and nothing lost.

**If Otto had clicked and entered card details** on the toll page, the steps would be: call the card issuer to report fraud and get a new card; check recent transactions; change any password entered on the page (and anywhere else it's reused); and consider a fraud alert or credit freeze if other personal information was shared.

## If you clicked or shared information

Act quickly. What to do depends on what you shared:

- **Card number:** call your card issuer, report the fraud and ask for a new card. Review recent charges.
- **Bank login or account details:** call your bank using a number you trust, change your password and turn on two-step verification.
- **A one-time code:** contact the company immediately. Someone may be trying to get into your account.
- **Your Social Security number:** go to IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan, and consider a **credit freeze** at all three bureaus, which is free. ([How to freeze your credit](/blog/how-to-freeze-your-credit/).)
- **Money sent by gift card, wire or crypto:** contact the company you used right away and ask about reversing it, then report it to the FTC. These payments are hard to recover, which is why scammers ask for them.
- **You installed something:** run your phone's security updates, remove unknown apps and change important passwords from a different device.

## How to cut down on scam texts

- **Turn on spam filtering** in your phone's message settings and your carrier's free tools.
- **Use two-step verification** on email, banking and shopping accounts, preferably with an authenticator app.
- **Be careful where you share your number**, especially on giveaways and forms.
- **Talk to family**, especially older relatives and teens, about the common scripts. A family "safe word" helps with impersonation scams.

## Why scam texts work

Scam texts aren't aimed at careless people. They're designed to catch anyone on a busy day. Most use the same three pressure points:

- **Urgency.** A deadline ("today," "within 24 hours") pushes you to act before you think.
- **Authority.** A familiar name, such as a bank, delivery company, toll agency or government office, makes the message feel routine.
- **Small asks.** A $4 toll or a $1.99 redelivery fee seems too small to be worth checking, which is exactly the point: the real goal is your card number.

Knowing the playbook helps. When a message makes you feel rushed, worried or excited, treat that feeling as a cue to slow down and check it another way.

## Help family members spot them

Older relatives and teenagers are frequent targets, often with different scripts: grandparent and "family emergency" scams for one, job, prize and gaming scams for the other. A short conversation helps more than a long warning:

- **Share the main rule:** never use the link or number in an unexpected message. Go to the company directly.
- **Agree a family check-in.** If someone texts claiming to be a relative in trouble, call that relative on their usual number.
- **Set up spam filtering together** on their phone, and show them how to forward a text to 7726.
- **Make it easy to ask.** Tell them you'd rather check ten real messages than miss one scam, so there's no embarrassment in asking.

## Keep a short list of real contacts

The simplest defence is knowing in advance where you'd check. Save your bank's fraud number, your card issuer's number and your carrier's support line in your contacts, and keep the official apps you use for deliveries and tolls. Then, when a text arrives, you already have a trusted route that doesn't involve anything in the message.

## Common questions

### What happens if I click a link in a scam text?
Often nothing happens just from opening a page, but the page may try to get you to enter passwords, card details or personal information, or to download something. If you entered anything, act quickly to secure that account or card. If you only opened it, close the page and don't enter anything.

### Should I reply STOP to a scam text?
It's generally better not to reply at all to messages you don't recognise. Replying confirms your number is active. Forward the message to 7726, report it as junk and delete it.

### How do scammers get my number?
Numbers come from data breaches, public listings, marketing lists, or simply automated dialling of number ranges. Getting a scam text doesn't necessarily mean your information was leaked.

### Will my bank ever text me asking for my account details?
Legitimate companies won't ask for your account information by text. Banks may send fraud alerts, but if you're unsure, ignore the text and call the number on the back of your card or use the bank's official app.
