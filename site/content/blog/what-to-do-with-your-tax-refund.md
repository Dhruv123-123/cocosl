For a lot of people, the tax refund is the biggest single deposit of the year. In the 2025 filing season, the IRS reported an average refund of about **$2,939**. That's enough to make a real dent in a goal, or to vanish into a month of slightly-nicer-than-usual spending without anyone quite noticing where it went.

Two things are worth knowing before it lands. First, a refund isn't a bonus: it's your own money, withheld from your paychecks during the year, coming back without interest. Second, because it arrives all at once, it's one of the easiest chances all year to make a big move with no change to your monthly budget.

This guide gives you a simple order of priorities, a worked example, and a way to decide whether you'd rather have the money in your paychecks instead.

## Before it arrives: decide on paper

The single best thing you can do is decide what the refund is for *before* it hits your account. Money that arrives without a plan gets absorbed into everyday spending within weeks, a pattern most of us know well.

Write down the expected amount (your tax software shows it when you file), then split it using the order below. When the deposit lands, move the money the same day.

## The order: five priorities

Not everyone needs every step. Start at the top and stop when the refund runs out.

### 1. Catch up on anything overdue
Past-due rent, utilities, or a bill already in collections come first. Late fees and service shut-offs cost more than anything else on this list.

### 2. Build or top up a starter emergency fund
If you don't have $1,000 set aside for surprises, this is the moment. A refund can do in one day what takes months of $50 transfers. ([Why $1,000 first](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)

### 3. Pay down high-interest debt
Credit card balances at 20% to 30% are the most expensive money most people owe. Putting a refund on the highest-rate card saves interest at that rate, a sure return few investments can match. ([Which debt first](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)

### 4. Fund the future
Once there's a cushion and no high-interest debt: retirement (an IRA contribution for the previous tax year can often be made until the April filing deadline), a down payment fund, or sinking funds for known costs coming up this year. ([Sinking funds](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

### 5. Keep some for yourself, on purpose
A plan that leaves nothing for enjoyment tends to get abandoned. Deciding up front that 10% to 20% is yours to spend makes it a choice rather than a leak, and makes the rest of the plan easier to stick to.

## A worked example: Taylor's $2,400 refund

Taylor (a fictional example) expects a **$2,400** refund. Taylor has $300 in savings, a $1,500 card balance at 24.99% and no overdue bills. Here's the split Taylor writes down before filing:

| Where | Amount | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund | $700 | Brings savings to $1,000 |
| Credit card | $1,200 | Cuts the 24.99% balance from $1,500 to $300 |
| Car repair sinking fund | $200 | Starts the fund for the year's maintenance |
| Spend | $300 | A weekend away, guilt-free |
| **Total** | **$2,400** | |

On the day the refund arrives, Taylor moves the $700 and $200 into savings and makes the $1,200 card payment. The $300 stays in checking for the weekend.

What it achieves: a full starter emergency fund, a card balance down 80% (so the interest on it drops from about $31 a month to about $6), and a car fund that will cover the next oil change and tires. ([A car fund, worked out](/blog/car-repair-sinking-fund/).) The remaining $300 on the card can be cleared from the next paycheck.

Without a plan, the most likely outcome is that the $2,400 sits in checking and is gone by May, with the card balance unchanged.

## Should you change your withholding?

A refund means you paid more tax than you owed during the year. Some people like that: it works as forced saving. Others would rather have the money in each paycheck.

For Taylor, the $2,400 refund works out to about **$92 a paycheck** across 26 paychecks. That $92 every two weeks could have gone onto the credit card from January, cutting the interest earlier, or into savings earning interest instead of sitting with the IRS earning none.

The trade-off is discipline: $92 a paycheck is easier to spend without noticing than a lump sum.

If you'd like a smaller refund (or you owed tax and want to avoid that), the **IRS Tax Withholding Estimator** works out a new W-4 for you in about 15 minutes. Have a recent pay stub and last year's return handy. You then give the new W-4 to your employer's payroll team. Aim for a small refund or a small balance due, not zero exactly; your income and situation can change during the year.

A good time to check: early in the year, and again after any big change like a new job, a raise, a second job, marriage or a baby.

## If you owed tax instead

Not everyone gets a refund. If you owed, three things help:

- **Pay what you can by the April deadline.** Interest and penalties are usually calculated on what's unpaid after that date, so even a partial payment reduces them.
- **Ask about a payment plan.** If you can't pay in full, the IRS offers online payment plans for many people. It costs less than missing the payment, and far less than putting a large tax bill on a credit card at 25%.
- **Fix it for next year.** A balance due usually means too little was withheld, which is common with a second job, freelance income or a mid-year raise. Run the Tax Withholding Estimator and give payroll a new W-4. If you have income with no withholding, like freelance work, look at quarterly estimated payments.

## How long does a refund take?

The IRS says most refunds for e-filed returns with direct deposit are issued within 21 days, though some take longer, especially if a return needs extra review or claims certain credits. Paper returns and paper checks are slower. "Where's My Refund?" on the IRS website shows the status about a day after an e-filed return is accepted.

Plan with that in mind: don't schedule a payment that depends on the refund until the money has actually arrived.

## One more option: split the deposit

When you file, you can usually ask for your refund to be split across up to three accounts using IRS Form 8888 (your tax software asks this when you choose direct deposit). That means the emergency fund and savings shares can land directly in a separate savings account, so the plan happens without you having to move anything. It's the closest thing to automating a once-a-year decision.

## If your refund is small

A $300 refund can still do a job. Put it all toward one thing: the emergency fund, the highest-rate card, or a sinking fund that's behind. Splitting a small refund five ways spreads it too thin to notice. And if a small refund was a disappointment, that's good news in disguise: it means you had more of your money during the year.

## Watch out for

- **Refund advance loans and "instant refund" offers.** Some are free, some aren't. Read the fees and the APR before signing. Most e-filed returns with direct deposit are refunded within a few weeks anyway.
- **Scams.** The IRS doesn't contact people by text, email or social media to ask for personal or financial information, or to ask you to "verify" your refund. Check the status of your refund only through the IRS's own "Where's My Refund?" tool.
- **Paying for filing you don't need.** If your return is simple, there are free and low-cost ways to file. ([Tax software for simple returns](/blog/best-tax-software-for-simple-returns/).)

## Common questions

### What is the smartest thing to do with a tax refund?
Use it in order: catch up on overdue bills, build a $1,000 emergency fund if you don't have one, pay down high-interest debt, then save or invest for future goals. Setting aside a small portion to enjoy helps you stick to the plan.

### Is it worth putting a refund toward a card balance?
If you already have a small emergency fund, yes: paying off a card at 20% to 30% saves interest at that rate, with no risk. If you have no savings at all, put part of it into a starter emergency fund first so the next surprise doesn't go straight back on the card.

### Is it better to get a big refund or a bigger paycheck?
Financially, a bigger paycheck is better, because the money is yours during the year instead of sitting with the IRS without interest. Some people prefer a refund as forced savings. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can help you change your W-4 if you'd rather have it in each paycheck.

### Can I put my tax refund in an IRA?
Yes. You can deposit it into an IRA like any other money, and contributions for the previous tax year can generally be made until the tax filing deadline in April. The 2026 IRA contribution limit is $7,500.
