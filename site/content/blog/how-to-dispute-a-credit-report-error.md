One in five people has an error on at least one of their credit reports, according to a study the FTC published in 2013, and some of those errors are serious enough to change the interest rate they're offered. The good news: you can check your reports for free, and you can dispute mistakes yourself, also for free. You don't need a credit repair company, and the law gives you exactly the same rights they'd use on your behalf.

This guide covers how to get your reports, what counts as an error, how to dispute with both the credit bureau and the company that reported it, and what to do if the dispute doesn't work. There's a worked example and sample wording along the way.

## Step zero: get all three reports, free

There are three nationwide credit bureaus: **Equifax, Experian and TransUnion**. Each keeps its own report on you, built from what lenders choose to send it, so an error can appear on one and not the others.

The official site for free reports is **AnnualCreditReport.com**, which the FTC directs people to. You can get a free report from each bureau there **every week**. Be careful with look-alike sites and "free credit report" ads that sign you up for paid monitoring; go to the address directly.

Pull all three on the same day so you can compare them side by side. Save each one as a PDF: you'll need the report details if you dispute.

## What counts as an error?

Read every section of each report, not just the score summary. Common errors include:

- **Identity mix-ups:** accounts that aren't yours, often from someone with a similar name or a wrongly entered Social Security number. These can also be a sign of identity theft.
- **Wrong payment history:** a payment marked late when you paid on time, or an account shown as delinquent after you'd brought it current.
- **Wrong balances or limits:** a paid-off loan still showing a balance, or a credit limit reported too low (which makes your utilization look higher).
- **Duplicate accounts:** the same debt listed twice, sometimes once by the original lender and once by a collector, both as unpaid.
- **Outdated information:** most negative items, including late payments and collections, generally must come off after seven years. Many bankruptcies stay for ten.
- **Accounts you closed shown as open**, or accounts closed by the lender shown as closed by you.
- **Wrong personal details:** old addresses and name misspellings. These don't affect your score, but a strange address or employer can point to a mixed file or fraud.

What isn't an error: a late payment that really was late, or a collection that really is yours and accurate. Those can't be removed by disputing, whatever a credit repair ad says. Accurate negative information falls off on its own timeline. (You can still ask a lender for a "goodwill" removal of a genuine one-off late payment; some agree, many don't.)

## How to dispute an error, step by step

### 1. List every error and the evidence for each
For each item: which report it's on, the account name and number, what's wrong, and what proves it. Evidence can be a bank statement showing the payment, a letter saying the debt was paid, or a police or FTC identity theft report.

### 2. Dispute with each bureau that shows the error
You can dispute online, by phone or by mail. Online is fastest, but a letter by certified mail with a return receipt gives you a clear record. Either way, explain what's wrong and why, ask for it to be corrected or removed, and attach copies (never originals) of your evidence.

### 3. Dispute with the company that reported it
The lender, card issuer or collector is called the **furnisher**. Send it a dispute in writing too, at the dispute address listed on your report or on its website. The CFPB recommends disputing with both, because the furnisher has the original records.

### 4. Keep copies and a log
Write down the date you sent each dispute, how, and any confirmation number. Keep copies of everything.

### 5. Wait for the results, then pull your reports again
Bureaus generally have **30 days** to investigate (sometimes up to 45). They must forward your dispute and evidence to the furnisher, and send you the results in writing. If an item is changed or removed, you can ask the bureau to send the corrected report to anyone who checked your credit recently.

### 6. If it's not fixed, escalate
See "If the dispute is rejected" below.

## What to write

Keep it short and factual. Something like:

> I am writing to dispute the following information on my credit report. The items I dispute are circled on the attached copy of my report.
>
> [Account name], account number ending [1234], is reported as 30 days late in March 2026. This is inaccurate: I paid this account on time. A copy of my bank statement showing the payment on March 12, 2026, is enclosed.
>
> I request that this information be corrected to show the account as paid on time.
>
> [Your name, address, date of birth, and the report confirmation number if you have one]

One error per paragraph, one sentence of evidence per error. Long letters with legal citations don't work any better than clear ones.

## A worked example: Drew cleans up two errors

Drew (a fictional example) is planning to apply for a car loan in the spring and pulls all three reports in February. Two things stand out:

1. **A late payment that wasn't.** Drew's credit card shows a 30-day late payment for August on Experian and TransUnion, but not on Equifax. Drew's bank statement shows the payment cleared two days before the due date.
2. **A paid medical collection.** A $412 bill from an urgent care visit, which Drew paid to the collection agency in full last year, is still showing as a collection on Equifax. The three bureaus have said they don't include paid medical collections on credit reports, so this one shouldn't be there at all.

Drew also notices an old address from a college apartment. That's fine: old addresses are normal and don't affect a score.

Here's what Drew does:

| Day | Action |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | Downloads the bank statement page and the collector's "paid in full" letter. Files online disputes with Experian and TransUnion for the late payment (attaching the statement) and with Equifax for the collection (attaching the letter). Saves the confirmation numbers. |
| Day 2 | Mails a short dispute letter to the card issuer's dispute address, certified with return receipt, with the bank statement. |
| Day 24 | TransUnion emails results: the late payment has been removed. |
| Day 29 | Experian's results arrive: also removed. Equifax deletes the medical collection. |
| Day 35 | Drew pulls all three reports again to confirm, and saves the new copies. |

About five weeks, a couple of hours of work, and no fees. Drew applies for the car loan in April with clean reports. (A single late payment can lower a good score noticeably, so removing a wrong one is worth the effort before any big application.)

## If the dispute is rejected

Sometimes the furnisher tells the bureau the information is accurate, and the bureau keeps it. You still have options:

- **Send more evidence.** If your first dispute was thin, a second one with clearer documents can succeed. Address the specific reason given in the results letter.
- **Dispute directly with the furnisher** if you haven't already. Furnishers generally must investigate a direct dispute and respond within 30 days.
- **Add a statement to your report.** You can ask the bureau to include a short statement explaining your side. It won't change your score, but anyone reading the report will see it.
- **File a complaint with the CFPB.** The CFPB forwards complaints about credit reporting to the company, which is expected to respond. It's free and online.
- **Contact your state attorney general**, or talk to a consumer protection lawyer if the error is causing real harm and the companies won't fix it. Some lawyers take Fair Credit Reporting Act cases without charging up front, because the law can make the company pay legal fees.

## If an account isn't yours at all

An account you never opened may be identity theft rather than a simple mistake. In that case:

1. Report it at **IdentityTheft.gov**, the FTC's site, which creates an identity theft report and a recovery plan.
2. Send that report with your disputes. Bureaus generally must block fraudulent information when you provide an identity theft report.
3. **Freeze your credit** at all three bureaus. It's free, takes a few minutes per bureau, and stops new accounts being opened in your name. ([How to freeze your credit](/blog/how-to-freeze-your-credit/).)

## Should you pay a credit repair company?

Usually, no. Credit repair companies can only do what you can do yourself for free: dispute inaccurate information. They cannot legally remove accurate negative information. Under federal law they can't charge you until they've done the work they promised, and they must give you a written contract and a three-day cancellation period. Any company that asks for money up front, tells you to dispute accurate information, or suggests creating a "new" credit identity is a red flag.

## Keeping your reports clean

- **Check your reports a few times a year**, and always a couple of months before a big application like a mortgage, car loan or apartment.
- **Set up autopay for at least the minimum** on every card and loan. One missed payment is the most damaging thing on most reports. ([How credit scores work](/blog/how-credit-scores-work/).)
- **Keep records of payoffs and settlements**, especially "paid in full" letters from collectors. They're what make a future dispute easy.

## Common questions

### How long does a credit report dispute take?
Bureaus generally have 30 days to investigate after receiving a dispute, and sometimes up to 45 if you send more information during the investigation. Online disputes often come back sooner.

### Does disputing hurt your credit score?
No. Filing a dispute doesn't lower your score. If the error is removed, your score may go up, depending on what was wrong.

### Can I remove a late payment that was accurate?
Not by disputing it, because accurate information can stay for up to seven years. You can ask the lender for a goodwill adjustment, explaining the circumstances, especially if your record is otherwise good. Some lenders agree; they don't have to.

### Do I need to dispute with all three bureaus?
Only with the bureaus whose reports show the error. Each bureau keeps its own report, so an error on one may not be on the others. It's also worth disputing with the company that reported it, which fixes the source.
