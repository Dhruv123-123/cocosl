Groceries are one of the few big costs you can change this week. Rent and car payments are locked in for months; the grocery bill resets every time you walk into a store. That makes it the most common place people look when money is tight, and also the place where "just spend less" advice falls apart, because you still have to eat.

The goal here isn't a bare-bones diet. It's spending less for the same or better food, mostly by cutting waste and impulse buys, which is where a surprising share of most grocery budgets goes. This guide gives you a way to set a realistic budget, the changes that save the most, and a four-week plan with a worked example.

## Step one: know what you actually spend

Most people underestimate their grocery spending, because it's spread across big weekly shops, quick top-up trips, the convenience store, and delivery apps. Before setting a target, look at the last two months of bank and card statements and add up everything that was food bought to eat at home. Divide by two.

Then, separately, add up takeout and delivery. It isn't groceries, but it's often what happens when the grocery plan fails, so it belongs in the same conversation.

## What's a realistic grocery budget?

It depends heavily on household size, ages, location and diet. The USDA publishes monthly **food plans** that estimate what a nutritious diet costs at four levels (Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost and Liberal), broken down by age and sex and updated with prices each month. They're a useful sanity check: if you're spending far above the Moderate-Cost level for your household, there's probably room to save without eating worse.

A simpler method: take your current spending and aim to cut it by **20% to 30%** over a month. For most households that's achievable through the changes below, without changing *what* you eat very much.

## Where the money goes (and where it leaks)

Grocery overspending usually comes from four places:

- **Waste.** Food bought and then thrown away. The USDA estimates that 30% to 40% of the US food supply is wasted, and households account for a large share of that. Every bag of spinach that turns to slime was money spent on nothing.
- **Unplanned trips.** Each "quick stop for milk" tends to come home with three or four other things.
- **Convenience premiums.** Pre-cut fruit, single-serve packs and ready meals cost much more per pound than the plain versions.
- **Brand loyalty.** Store brands are often made to similar standards and cost noticeably less.

Fix those, and most of the savings take care of themselves.

## The 4-week plan

### 1. Week 1: track and clear out
Shop as normal but keep every receipt. Before shopping, take everything out of the fridge, freezer and cupboards that needs using, and plan the week's meals around it. Most kitchens hold a week of meals that nobody has noticed.

### 2. Week 2: plan meals and shop once
Pick five or six dinners for the week, write a list from them, and shop **once**. Plan for leftovers (cook once, eat twice) and leave a night or two for "use it up" meals. Check what's on sale before you plan, not after.

### 3. Week 3: switch to store brands and compare unit prices
For staples (rice, pasta, canned goods, flour, frozen vegetables, cleaning supplies), buy the store brand. Use the **unit price** on the shelf label (price per ounce or per pound) to compare sizes and brands. Bigger isn't always cheaper.

### 4. Week 4: set the budget and a weekly number
Use what you learned to set a monthly grocery budget, divide it into a **weekly number**, and shop to that. A weekly number is easier to stick to than a monthly one, because you know by Wednesday whether you're on track.

## A worked example: Nora and Ben, $900 to $650

Nora and Ben (a fictional couple) look at their statements and find they're spending about **$900 a month** on groceries, plus about $250 on takeout. They aim for **$650**, about 28% less, without giving up the food they like.

Here's what they change, and their estimate of what each change saves:

| Change | Estimated monthly saving |
|---|---|
| One planned shop a week instead of four or five trips | $80 |
| Planning meals around what's already in the fridge, and a "use it up" night | $60 |
| Store brands for staples and cleaning supplies | $45 |
| Buying whole produce and cheese instead of pre-cut | $25 |
| Cooking a double batch twice a week for lunches | $40 |
| **Total** | **$250** |

That takes them from about $900 to about **$650 a month**, or **$150 a week**.

Their first month isn't perfect. Week 2 runs over, at $185, because they restocked spices and oil. By week 4 they're at $145. And there's a bonus they hadn't planned on: with a fridge full of planned meals, takeout drops from $250 to about $150, because "there's nothing to eat" stops being true. Combined, that's about **$350 a month** back in the budget, or $4,200 a year, which they send to an emergency fund. ([How to build an emergency fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)

The changes that worked best for them were the boring ones: one trip a week and planning around the fridge. The store-brand switch saved less than they expected, because they already bought some. That's typical: the biggest savings are usually in waste and unplanned trips, not coupons.

## More ways to save

- **Shop with a list and a full stomach.** Both are well-tested ways to reduce impulse buying.
- **Use your freezer.** Bread, meat bought on sale, cooked rice, soups, overripe bananas. Freezing is the easiest way to cut waste.
- **Buy whole, prep yourself.** A whole chicken, a block of cheese or a head of lettuce usually costs much less per pound than the prepared version.
- **Stock up on sales for things you'd buy anyway.** Canned goods, frozen vegetables and pantry staples keep for months. Only stock up on what you know you'll use.
- **Try a cheaper store for staples.** Discount grocers and warehouse clubs can be much cheaper for some items. A warehouse membership only pays off if you'll use the bulk sizes before they spoil.
- **Look at store apps and loyalty programs** for digital coupons on things on your list. Skip coupons for things you wouldn't otherwise buy.
- **Watch the "best by" dates sensibly.** Many dates are about quality, not safety; the USDA notes that most foods (apart from infant formula) don't legally require them. Use your senses, and when in doubt about safety, throw it out.

## If money is very tight

If you're struggling to afford enough food, help is available, and using it is what it's there for:

- **SNAP** (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) helps eligible households buy groceries. Eligibility depends on income, household size and your state's rules; your state SNAP office can check.
- **WIC** supports pregnant people, new parents and young children with specific foods and nutrition help.
- **Food banks and pantries** provide free groceries, no questions asked in many places. Search for your local food bank, or call 211 for local help.
- **School meal programs** may provide free or reduced-price meals for children.

## Keeping it going

The plan works only if it becomes a habit. Two things help most:

- **Keep the weekly number visible.** A note on the fridge or a line in your budget app.
- **Make a list of 10 "easy dinners"** you both like, with their ingredients. On weeks when planning feels like too much, pick five from the list and you're done.

The grocery budget fits inside the rest of your spending plan as one of the flexible categories. ([The four-line budget](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/).) If you're working through how much of your income should go to needs like food, the 50/30/20 rule is a good starting point. ([The 50/30/20 rule](/blog/50-30-20-rule/).)

## Common questions

### How much should a couple spend on groceries a month?
It varies a lot with location, diet and ages. The USDA's monthly food plans estimate the cost of a nutritious diet at four spending levels for different households, which gives you a realistic benchmark. Start from what you spend now and aim to cut 20% to 30% through planning and less waste.

### What's the fastest way to cut my grocery bill?
Shop once a week from a list, and plan meals around what's already in your fridge. Those two changes cut impulse buys and waste, which are usually the biggest leaks.

### Are store brands really as good?
Often, yes, especially for staples like rice, pasta, flour, canned goods, frozen vegetables and cleaning products. Many are made to similar standards and cost noticeably less. Try a few and keep the ones you like.

### Is meal planning worth it?
For most households, yes. It cuts waste, reduces trips, and makes takeout less likely on busy nights. Even planning four or five dinners a week, with leftovers, makes a noticeable difference.
