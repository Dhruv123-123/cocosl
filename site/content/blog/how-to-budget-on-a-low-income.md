Most budgeting advice assumes there's money left over to divide up: 50% here, 30% there, 20% to savings. On a low income, the numbers often don't work that way. The essentials can take almost everything, and a single surprise bill can knock the whole month over.

That doesn't mean budgeting is pointless. It means a different kind of budget: one that starts with **needs, in order of importance**, finds every dollar of help you're entitled to, and builds even a tiny cushion. This guide walks through that approach and a worked example.

## Why percentage rules don't fit

The 50/30/20 rule (needs, wants, savings) is a useful starting point for many incomes. But if rent alone is 50% of your take-home pay, the rule tells you nothing useful. Trying to force it can make you feel like you're failing when you're doing your best. ([The 50/30/20 rule, explained](/blog/50-30-20-rule/).)

A needs-first budget asks a simpler question: **what has to be paid, in what order, and what's left?**

## The needs-first budget

### 1. Write down your real monthly income
Use take-home pay, benefits, child support and any regular side income. If it changes month to month, use your lowest typical month. ([Budgeting with irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-with-irregular-income/).)

### 2. List your needs in priority order
When money is tight, pay these first:

1. **Housing:** rent or mortgage, because losing your home makes everything else harder.
2. **Utilities:** electricity, heat, water.
3. **Food.**
4. **Transport to work** and essential trips.
5. **Childcare** you need to work.
6. **Medicine and essential health costs.**
7. **Phone and internet**, often needed for work, school and benefits.
8. **Minimum debt payments**, starting with secured debts (like a car loan you need for work).

### 3. See what's left
Subtract needs from income. If the number is positive, that's what covers everything else. If it's negative, go to the "if needs cost more than you earn" section below.

### 4. Set a small buffer goal
Even $5 or $10 a week, moved automatically to a separate savings account, builds a cushion for the next surprise. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 survey of household finances, 63% of adults said they could cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent, so many others are in the same position. A small buffer is the first step out of it. ([How much emergency fund you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/).)

### 5. Give the rest a weekly number
Divide what's left by the weeks in the month. That weekly number covers household items, clothes and the small things that make life bearable. Track it once a week, not every day.

## Find the help you may qualify for

Many people on low incomes don't claim help they're entitled to. It's worth checking:

- **SNAP (food assistance).** Eligibility is based on household income, size and other rules, and you apply through your state. For October 2026 through September 2027, the gross monthly income limit for a one-person household in the 48 contiguous states is **$1,729**, higher for larger households. Some states use broader rules.
- **LIHEAP (energy assistance).** A federally funded programme that helps low-income households pay heating and cooling bills, run through states and local agencies.
- **Utility discount programmes.** Many utilities have reduced rates or payment plans for lower-income customers. Ask yours.
- **Lower-cost phone and internet plans**, which some providers offer for qualifying households.
- **The Earned Income Tax Credit** if you work. It's refundable, so it can mean a refund even if you owe no tax. ([The EITC, explained](/blog/earned-income-tax-credit/).)
- **Free tax filing** through IRS Free File or a VITA site, so you don't pay fees to get your refund.
- **Local help:** food banks, community action agencies and 211, a free helpline in most areas that connects people with local services.

## A worked example: Kit's month

Kit (a fictional example) is a single parent of one, bringing home about **$2,150 a month** from part-time work and child support.

**Needs, in order:**

| Need | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,050 |
| Utilities | $140 |
| Groceries | $420 |
| Bus passes | $90 |
| Childcare co-pay | $120 |
| Medicine | $25 |
| Phone | $35 |
| Minimum card payment | $40 |
| **Total** | **$1,920** |

That leaves **$230** for everything else: household supplies, clothes, school costs and anything that goes wrong.

**What Kit changes:**

1. **Applies for SNAP** and **LIHEAP** through the state. If approved, food and energy assistance would free up money in the grocery and utility lines. (Eligibility depends on Kit's state and household; it isn't automatic.)
2. **Switches to a cheaper phone plan**, saving **$20** a month.
3. **Sets an automatic $10 a week** to a separate savings account, about **$43** a month.
4. **Uses a weekly number** of about **$48** for everything else ($230 + $20 − $43 = $207 a month, divided by 4.3 weeks).

It's tight. But Kit now knows exactly what's covered, has a small buffer growing, and has applied for help that could make a real difference.

## If needs cost more than you earn

If the needs list is bigger than your income, the gap won't close by cutting coffee. Options to consider:

- **Call before you miss a bill.** Landlords, utilities and lenders often have hardship plans, due-date changes or payment arrangements, but you usually have to ask before the bill is late.
- **Prioritise housing and utilities**, and ask for a payment plan on lower-priority bills.
- **Look at the big three:** housing, transport and food. A roommate, a cheaper plan or a different commute can matter more than dozens of small cuts.
- **Check income options:** more hours, a different shift, a better-paying job, or a side income. ([How to negotiate a raise](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-raise/).)
- **Avoid high-cost borrowing** like payday loans, which usually make the gap bigger the next month.
- **Talk to a nonprofit credit counsellor** if debt payments are part of the problem.

## Small savings that add up

When every dollar counts, a few habits help without making life miserable:

- **Plan meals around a short list** and cheap staples, and use store brands. ([How to lower your grocery bill](/blog/how-to-lower-your-grocery-bill/).)
- **Cancel anything you don't use.** ([Find forgotten subscriptions](/blog/find-and-cancel-subscriptions/).)
- **Avoid fees:** overdraft fees, late fees and check-cashing fees can quietly take a big share of a small income. ([How to avoid overdraft fees](/blog/how-to-avoid-overdraft-fees/).)
- **Use free things:** libraries, community events, parks.

## Be kind to yourself

Budgeting on a low income takes more skill than budgeting on a high one, not less. If a month goes wrong, it's not a personal failure. Look at what happened, adjust the plan, and keep going. The aim is a little more control and a little more cushion over time.

## Plan for the irregular costs

On a tight budget, the expenses that wreck a month are usually the ones that don't arrive every month: a car registration, a school trip, a birthday, a prescription, a work uniform. They feel like emergencies, but most of them are predictable.

List the ones you had last year and roughly what each cost. Divide the total by 12. Even if you can only set aside part of that amount, a small separate pot means the next one is a planned cost, not a crisis. Start with the one coming up soonest. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

If there's nothing left over to save at all, that's useful information too. It means the next step is on the income or help side, not finding more to cut.

## Check in every week, not just every month

On a small income, a monthly budget can drift off course before you notice. A five-minute check each week works better: look at your checking balance, what's still due before the next payday, and what's left for groceries and transport. If a week runs over, adjust the next one straight away instead of waiting for the month to end. Short, frequent check-ins catch problems while they're still small.

## Common questions

### How do I start budgeting when I have almost no money left over?
Start by listing your needs in priority order and paying them first. Then set a small automatic savings amount, even $5 a week, and give whatever's left a weekly spending number. The goal is control first, savings second.

### Which bills come first when money runs short?
Generally housing, utilities, food, transport to work and essential medicine come first, then minimum debt payments. Call the companies you can't pay before the due date to ask about payment plans or hardship options.

### How can I find out what benefits I qualify for?
Contact your state's benefits agency, check your state's SNAP and energy assistance websites, or call 211 in most areas for local help. A free tax preparation site can also check whether you qualify for the Earned Income Tax Credit.

### Is it worth saving if I can only save $5 a week?
Yes. $5 a week is $260 a year, which can cover a small emergency that might otherwise lead to a late fee, an overdraft or a high-cost loan. The habit matters as much as the amount.
