Form W-4 is the short form you fill out when you start a job. It tells your employer how much federal income tax to take out of each paycheck. Most people fill it in once, in a hurry, on their first day, and never look at it again.

That's how you end up with a surprise: a tax bill in April because too little was withheld, or a big refund, which means you gave the government an interest-free loan all year. Neither is a disaster, but both are avoidable, and fixing your W-4 takes about 20 minutes.

This guide explains what each step of the form does, when to change it, and a worked example of the most common problem: two jobs.

## What the W-4 does (and doesn't do)

The W-4 doesn't change how much tax you owe. Your tax is set by your income, deductions and credits. The W-4 only changes **when** you pay it: a little from every paycheck, or a lump sum in April, or too much during the year and a refund later.

The goal is to get close to zero: a small refund or a small balance due. Since 2020, the form no longer uses "allowances". Instead it asks for specific facts: your filing status, other jobs, dependents, other income and deductions.

## Filling out the form, step by step

### 1. Step 1: personal information and filing status
Your name, address, Social Security number, and filing status: single (or married filing separately), married filing jointly, or head of household. Only choose head of household if you're unmarried and pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person.

### 2. Step 2: multiple jobs or a working spouse
Complete this step if you have more than one job at the same time, or you're married filing jointly and your spouse also works. You have three options: use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator (most accurate), use the worksheet on page 3 of the form, or tick the box in Step 2(c) on the W-4 for **both** jobs (simplest; works best when the two jobs pay about the same).

### 3. Step 3: dependents and other credits
If your income is within the limits, enter the child tax credit for each qualifying child under 17 and the credit for other dependents. This reduces withholding because you'll owe less tax. If you have two jobs, enter this on only one W-4, usually the highest-paying job.

### 4. Step 4(a): other income
Income that isn't from a job and has no tax withheld, like interest, dividends or retirement income. Adding it here means tax is withheld for it from your paycheck. Freelance income can go here too, but many people use Step 4(c) or estimated payments instead.

### 5. Step 4(b): deductions
If you expect deductions beyond the standard deduction, such as itemized deductions or some of the newer deductions for overtime, tips or people 65 and older, the worksheet on the form helps you work out a figure. Entering it reduces withholding.

### 6. Step 4(c): extra withholding
Any extra dollar amount you want withheld from each paycheck. This is the simplest way to fix under-withholding, or to cover tax on side income.

### 7. Step 5: sign and give it to payroll
Sign and date it, and give it to your employer, often through an HR or payroll portal. It usually takes effect within a pay period or two. Keep a copy.

## A worked example: Tess and two jobs

Tess (a fictional example) is single and has two jobs: a full-time office job paying **$40,000** a year (paid every two weeks) and a weekend job at a garden center paying **$14,000**. Tess filled in a basic W-4 at each job, with nothing in Step 2.

Last April, Tess owed money. Here's why.

Each employer withholds as though its job were Tess's only income, so each one gives Tess the full standard deduction and the low 10% bracket. Roughly:

| | Taxed as if it's the only job | Approximate withholding |
|---|---|---|
| Office job, $40,000 | $40,000 − $16,100 = $23,900 taxable | about $2,620 |
| Weekend job, $14,000 | Below the standard deduction | about $0 |
| **Total withheld** | | **about $2,620** |

But Tess only gets **one** standard deduction, and the combined $54,000 is taxed together:

- $54,000 − $16,100 = $37,900 taxable
- 10% of $12,400 + 12% of $25,500 = **about $4,300** of tax

That's a gap of about **$1,680**: roughly the bill Tess faced in April. Nothing went wrong with either employer; they just didn't know about each other.

**The fix.** Tess has two good options:

1. **Tick Step 2(c) on both W-4s.** Each job then withholds at a higher rate. It's simple, though with jobs of very different pay it can over-withhold a little.
2. **Add an extra amount in Step 4(c) on the office job W-4.** $1,680 spread over 26 paychecks is about **$65 a paycheck**.

Tess picks option 2, then runs the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, which suggests a similar figure. From the next paycheck, the office job withholds an extra $65, and next April's return comes out within about $100 of zero.

The real withholding tables are a little more detailed than this simple version, so treat these numbers as an illustration of the gap, not exact figures. The estimator does the precise maths with your pay stubs.

## When to update your W-4

Review it whenever something changes your tax:

- **A new job, or a second job.** The most common cause of under-withholding.
- **Marriage or divorce.** Your filing status changes, and so does the right withholding.
- **A baby or a new dependent.** You may qualify for the child tax credit.
- **A big raise or bonus.** Bonuses are often withheld at a flat rate that may not match your actual rate.
- **Side income or freelance work.** No one is withholding tax on it. ([Self-employment tax, explained](/blog/self-employment-tax-explained/).)
- **New deductions you qualify for**, like the deduction for overtime pay, which can mean you're having too much withheld.
- **A surprise at tax time**, in either direction.

The best time for a routine check is early in the year, when there are the most paychecks left to adjust.

## Big refund or bigger paycheck?

Some people like a big refund as a forced savings plan. That works, but it costs you: the money sits with the IRS all year without earning interest, and it isn't available if you need it. If your refund was $2,400 last year, that's about $92 a paycheck you could have had instead, paid into savings automatically. ([What to do with your tax refund](/blog/what-to-do-with-your-tax-refund/).)

A middle ground is to aim for a refund of a few hundred dollars: enough that you're not likely to owe, without lending the IRS thousands.

## Using the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator

The estimator on IRS.gov is free and doesn't ask for your name or Social Security number. Have these ready:

- your most recent pay stub for each job (and your spouse's, if filing jointly);
- last year's tax return, if you have it;
- an idea of any other income and deductions for this year.

It takes about 15 minutes and gives you exact numbers to put on a new W-4, along with a pre-filled form you can print.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving Step 2 blank with two jobs**, or with a spouse who works. This is the most common cause of a surprise bill.
- **Claiming dependents on both jobs' W-4s.** Enter them on one W-4 only.
- **Claiming exempt without qualifying.** You can only claim exemption from withholding if you had no tax liability last year and expect none this year. It's rare, and the IRS can ask your employer to change your withholding if it's misused.
- **Forgetting to update after a life change.** Set a reminder for January.

## Common questions

### How often can I change my W-4?
As often as you like. Give your employer a new form whenever your situation changes; it usually takes effect within one or two pay periods.

### Why do I owe taxes when I filled out my W-4?
The most common reasons are two jobs or a working spouse with nothing in Step 2, side income with no withholding, or a bonus withheld at a lower rate than your actual tax rate. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can show the gap and a fix.

### Should I check the box in Step 2(c)?
Tick it on both W-4s if you have two jobs that pay roughly the same, or if you and your spouse earn similar amounts. If the incomes are very different, the estimator or the worksheet is usually more accurate.

### Can I have extra tax taken out of my paycheck?
Yes. Enter an amount in Step 4(c), and your employer will withhold it from every paycheck on top of the normal amount. It's a simple way to cover tax on side income or to avoid owing in April.
