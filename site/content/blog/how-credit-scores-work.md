A credit score is a three-digit summary of your credit report, used by lenders, and sometimes landlords and insurers, to guess how likely you are to pay back what you borrow. A better score can mean lower interest rates, smaller deposits and easier approvals.

The good news: the rules are public, and the biggest factors are habits you control.

## Credit report vs credit score

- Your **credit report** is the raw record: your accounts, balances, limits, payment history and recent applications, kept by the three nationwide bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion).
- Your **credit score** is a number calculated from that report by a scoring model. FICO and VantageScore are the most common, and there are many versions, which is why you can see different scores in different apps.

Fix the report and the score follows. You can get your reports free, weekly, at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/), the official site. [Here's how to check them and freeze your credit](/blog/how-to-freeze-your-credit/).

## The five factors (FICO)

FICO publishes roughly how much each category matters for the general population:

| Factor | Weight | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Payment history** | 35% | Paying on time, every time. Late payments (30+ days) hurt the most. |
| **Amounts owed** | 30% | Mostly **utilization**: how much of your credit limits you're using. |
| **Length of credit history** | 15% | Age of your oldest account and the average age of all accounts. |
| **New credit** | 10% | Recent applications and newly opened accounts. |
| **Credit mix** | 10% | Having different types (cards, instalment loans). The least important; never borrow just for this. |

So two habits cover about two-thirds of your score: **pay on time** and **keep balances low relative to your limits**.

## Utilization, and why paying in full can still drop your score

Utilization is your card balances divided by your credit limits. $600 on a $2,000 limit is 30%. Lower is generally better; people with the highest scores often use well under 10%.

Here's the catch. Your card has two dates:

1. **Statement closing date**: when the month's statement is generated. The balance on this date is usually what gets **reported to the bureaus**.
2. **Payment due date**: usually a few weeks later.

If you spend $1,500 on a $2,000 limit and pay it in full on the due date, you never pay interest. But the bureaus may already have seen a $1,500 balance (75% utilization) from the statement date. Your score dips, even though you did nothing wrong.

**The fix:** if you have a big month, make a payment *before* the statement closing date. You can find that date on your statement or in your card app. Utilization has no memory in most scoring models, so the dip reverses once a lower balance is reported.

## Don't cancel that old card (yet)

Closing a credit card you never use can lower your score in two ways:

- **Utilization rises**, because your total available credit shrinks. Close a $3,000-limit card while carrying $1,000 on another $2,000 card, and utilization jumps from 20% to 50%.
- **Your history can get shorter over time.** Closed accounts in good standing can stay on your report for years, but eventually they drop off.

If the old card has **no annual fee**, keeping it open and using it for one small purchase every few months (paid off automatically) is often the better move. If it *does* charge a fee, ask the issuer to switch it to a no-fee card from the same issuer, which typically keeps the account history.

## What doesn't affect your score

- **Checking your own score or report** (a "soft" inquiry).
- **Your income or bank balance.** They're not on your credit report.
- **Debit card use.** It isn't credit.
- **Carrying a balance and paying interest.** This is a myth. You don't need to pay interest to build credit; pay in full.

## How to build credit from scratch

1. **Become an authorised user** on a trusted family member's long-standing, well-managed card.
2. **Get a secured card or student card**, use it for one small recurring bill, and set autopay for the full statement balance.
3. **Pay every bill on time.** Set autopay for at least the minimum on everything, as a safety net.
4. **Be patient.** Scores reward time.

## Common questions

### What's a good credit score?
On the common 300–850 scale, scores in the high 600s and up are generally considered good, and 740+ often gets the best rates. Each lender sets its own cut-offs.

### How long do late payments stay on my report?
Generally up to seven years, though their impact fades over time if you keep paying on time.

### Does applying for a card hurt my score?
A hard inquiry can lower it slightly for a short time. Multiple rate-shopping inquiries for the same kind of loan (e.g. a car loan) within a short window are often counted as one.

### Why are my scores different in different apps?
Different models (FICO vs VantageScore), different versions and different bureaus' data. Watch the trend, not the exact number.
