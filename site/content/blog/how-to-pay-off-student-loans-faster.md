Student loans can feel like a permanent part of your budget. A standard federal plan runs ten years, and other plans can stretch much longer. But you're allowed to pay them off early, there's no penalty for doing it, and small extra amounts make a bigger difference than most people expect.

This guide covers which loan to target, how to make sure your extra money actually reduces the balance (it doesn't always, by default), and a worked example of cutting years off repayment.

## First: should you pay them off faster at all?

Paying extra on student loans is a good move once a few other things are in place. Before you start, check that you:

- **Have a starter emergency fund**, so a surprise bill doesn't go on a credit card. ([How much emergency fund you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/).)
- **Get your full employer 401(k) match**, if you have one. The match is usually worth more than the interest you'd save. ([The 401(k) match, explained](/blog/401k-match-explained/).)
- **Have no high-interest credit card debt.** Card rates are typically far higher than student loan rates, so cards come first. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)

After that, the choice depends partly on your interest rates. Every extra dollar on a 7% loan saves 7% a year in interest, with no market risk. A loan at 3% is less urgent, and some people prefer to invest or save the extra instead. Both are reasonable; there's no single right answer.

One special case: if you're aiming for **Public Service Loan Forgiveness** or another forgiveness programme, paying extra may reduce the amount forgiven. Check how your plan works before prepaying.

## How student loan payments are applied

This is the part most people never see. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a payment usually goes first to **fees**, then to **interest**, and only then to **principal**, the balance itself.

The catch is what happens to money you pay **above** the minimum. Some servicers apply it to principal. Others treat it as an early payment of next month's bill, called **"paid ahead" status**. That means your next bill may be smaller or skipped, and your loan isn't paid off any faster.

The fix is simple: **tell your servicer how to apply extra payments.** You can usually set a standing instruction online or by phone: "Apply any amount above my minimum to principal, on the loan with the highest interest rate, and don't advance my due date."

## How to pay off student loans faster

### 1. List every loan
Log in to your servicer (and studentaid.gov for federal loans) and list each loan's balance, interest rate and type (federal or private). You may have several separate loans even if you make one payment.

### 2. Sign up for autopay
Many federal loan servicers take **0.25 percentage points** off your interest rate when you pay by automatic debit, and many private lenders offer a similar discount. It also makes sure you're never late.

### 3. Choose your target loan
Put extra money on the loan with the **highest interest rate** first (the avalanche method). It saves the most interest. If a small loan is close to paid off and clearing it would keep you motivated, it's fine to finish that one first.

### 4. Set a fixed extra amount
Pick a monthly amount you can keep up, even $25 or $50, and add it to your payment. A fixed amount on autopay works better than "whatever's left over."

### 5. Tell the servicer where to put it
Set the standing instruction described above. Then check your next statement to make sure the extra went to principal on the right loan.

### 6. Add windfalls
Put part of any tax refund, bonus or raise toward the target loan. ([What to do with your tax refund](/blog/what-to-do-with-your-tax-refund/).)

### 7. Roll payments forward
When a loan is paid off, add its whole payment to the next loan on your list. The amount going to debt stays the same, but the payoff speeds up each time.

## A worked example: Bea's four loans

Bea (a fictional example) graduated with four loans totalling **$28,000**:

| Loan | Balance | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| A | $6,000 | 7.5% |
| B | $8,000 | 6.5% |
| C | $9,000 | 5.0% |
| D | $5,000 | 4.5% |

On a standard 10-year schedule, Bea's payments total about **$309 a month**, and Bea would pay roughly **$9,100** in interest over the ten years.

**Bea's plan:** after building a $1,000 starter fund and getting the full 401(k) match, Bea adds **$150 a month**, sets autopay, and tells the servicer to apply the extra to principal on loan A, then B, then C, then D.

| | Standard plan | With $150 extra |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | about $309 | about $459 |
| Time to pay off | 10 years | about 6 years |
| Total interest | about $9,100 | about $4,950 |

Bea saves roughly **$4,150 in interest** and is debt-free about **four years sooner**. The autopay rate cut would save a little more on top.

(Fictional example with approximate figures. Your rates, balances and servicer rules will change the numbers.)

## Refinancing: when it helps and what you give up

Refinancing means taking out a new private loan to pay off your existing ones, ideally at a lower rate. It can save money on **private** loans, and on federal loans if you have a high income, stable job and good credit.

But refinancing **federal** loans into a private loan is permanent, and you lose federal protections such as income-driven repayment, deferment and forbearance options, and access to forgiveness programmes. For many people, those protections are worth more than a slightly lower rate. If you refinance, consider doing it only with private loans, or only once you're confident you won't need those options.

## If money is tight instead

Paying faster only makes sense if the regular payment is comfortable. If it isn't, the priority flips: look at lowering the payment first. Federal loans offer income-driven repayment options, and repayment plans were changed by law in 2025, so use the Education Department's Loan Simulator on studentaid.gov or call your servicer to see what you qualify for now. Never ignore a payment you can't make: missed payments on federal loans can lead to default, which has serious consequences.

## Ways to find the extra money

- **Use a raise.** When your pay goes up, send part of the difference to your loans before you get used to spending it. ([How to negotiate a raise](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-raise/).)
- **Cut a few subscriptions.** ([Find and cancel forgotten subscriptions](/blog/find-and-cancel-subscriptions/).)
- **Try a no-spend month** and send the savings to your target loan. ([The no-spend challenge](/blog/no-spend-challenge/).)
- **Biweekly payments.** Paying half your monthly payment every two weeks adds up to one extra full payment a year, but only if your servicer applies it correctly. Ask first.

## Federal vs private loans: what's different

The steps above work for both, but a few differences matter:

- **Federal loans** have fixed rates set by law, standard protections such as deferment and forbearance, income-driven repayment options, and forgiveness programmes for some borrowers. Your servicer is assigned by the Department of Education.
- **Private loans** come from banks, credit unions and online lenders. Rates can be fixed or variable, protections depend on the lender, and some have a co-signer. Variable-rate private loans can become more expensive if rates rise, which makes them good candidates for extra payments.

If you have both, paying extra on a high-rate private loan first is often the best use of the money, while the federal loans keep their protections.

## Keep an eye on your servicer

Servicers handle millions of accounts and mistakes happen. Every few months, check that extra payments went to principal on the loan you chose, that your due date hasn't moved forward, and that your monthly payment hasn't changed without your asking. If something looks wrong, call, ask for it to be corrected, and keep a note of the date and name. If it isn't fixed, you can submit a complaint to the CFPB.

## Common questions

### Is there a penalty for paying off student loans early?
No. Student loan borrowers can prepay at any time without a prepayment penalty. The thing to watch is how your servicer applies the extra money: ask for it to go to principal, not toward your next bill.

### Which student loan should I pay off first?
To save the most interest, target the loan with the highest interest rate while paying the minimum on the rest. If you need a quick win to stay motivated, paying off the smallest balance first also works; it just costs a little more.

### Should I pay off student loans or invest?
Many people do both: get the full employer match, keep an emergency fund, then split extra money. Higher-rate loans make paying them down more attractive; lower-rate loans make investing or saving more attractive. Investing involves risk, while paying off debt doesn't.

### Does paying off student loans early hurt my credit?
Closing a loan can cause a small, temporary change in your score, but paying debt off is generally good for your finances and your credit over time. On-time payment history stays on your report.
