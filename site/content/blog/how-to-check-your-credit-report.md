Your credit report is the record that lenders, landlords, insurers and sometimes employers look at to decide whether to do business with you. Mistakes on it can cost you a loan, an apartment or a lower rate, and accounts you don't recognise can be the first sign of identity theft. Checking it is free and takes about half an hour.

This guide covers where to get your reports, what to look at line by line, how to spot problems, and how to fix them.

## Credit report vs credit score

Your **credit report** is the detailed history: your accounts, balances, payment history, collections and the companies that have checked your credit. Your **credit score** is a number calculated from that report. You can check your reports for free; scores are sometimes free through your bank or card issuer, and sometimes cost money. Fixing the report is what changes the score. ([How credit scores work](/blog/how-credit-scores-work/).)

## Where to get your free reports

There are three nationwide credit bureaus, **Equifax, Experian and TransUnion**, and each keeps its own report on you. They often differ, because not every lender reports to all three.

The FTC says you can check each bureau's report **once a week for free at AnnualCreditReport.com**, and that it's the only website authorised to fill orders for the free reports that federal law entitles you to. You can also call **1-877-322-8228**.

Be careful with lookalike sites that ask for a credit card or sign you up for a paid service. You never need to pay to see your reports through AnnualCreditReport.com.

## How to check your credit report

### 1. Request all three reports
Go to AnnualCreditReport.com and request your Equifax, Experian and TransUnion reports. You'll answer questions to verify your identity. Download or print each one so you have a copy.

### 2. Check your personal information
Look at your name, addresses, date of birth and employers. An address you've never lived at or a name you've never used can be a simple mistake, or a sign that someone else's information is mixed with yours.

### 3. Go through every account
For each account, check that it's yours, the date it was opened, the credit limit or loan amount, the balance, and the payment history. Look closely for late payments you don't think you made.

### 4. Look at collections and public records
Check any collections accounts: do you recognise the original creditor, and is the amount right? Old debts should eventually drop off your report; negative items generally stay for up to seven years.

### 5. Review the inquiries
Inquiries show who has checked your credit. **Hard inquiries** come from applications you made. **Soft inquiries** (such as your own checks, or pre-approved offers) don't affect your score. A hard inquiry you don't recognise is worth questioning.

### 6. Mark anything wrong
Highlight every error, note which report it's on, and gather any proof you have, such as a bank statement showing an on-time payment.

## What to look for

| Section | What a problem looks like |
|---|---|
| Personal information | Wrong name, address or date of birth; addresses you've never used |
| Accounts | Accounts you didn't open; wrong balances or limits; closed accounts shown as open |
| Payment history | Late payments you made on time |
| Collections | Debts you don't recognise; the same debt listed twice; wrong amounts |
| Inquiries | Hard inquiries from companies you never applied to |

The FTC says to make sure the information is accurate, complete and up to date, and to check your report before applying for credit, a loan, insurance, housing or a job.

## Signs of identity theft

Some errors aren't mistakes. The FTC lists signs that someone may be using your identity, including **accounts you don't recognise on your credit report**, bills for things you didn't buy, withdrawals you didn't make, and bills that suddenly stop arriving (a thief may have changed your billing address).

If you see these, act quickly:

- **Report it at IdentityTheft.gov.** You'll get a free personal recovery plan and pre-filled letters to send to credit bureaus, businesses and debt collectors.
- **Freeze your credit** with all three bureaus. While a freeze is in place, nobody can open a new credit account in your name. It's free to place and lift. ([How to freeze your credit](/blog/how-to-freeze-your-credit/).)
- **Or place a fraud alert**, which asks businesses to verify your identity before opening accounts. You only need to contact one bureau, which must tell the other two.

## A worked example: Uma checks before renting

Uma (a fictional example) is about to apply for an apartment and checks all three reports first. She finds three problems:

| Problem | On which report | What Uma does |
|---|---|---|
| An old address she never lived at | One report | Disputes it as incorrect personal information |
| A store card shown 30 days late in March, though she paid on time | Two reports | Disputes it with both bureaus and the card issuer, attaching her bank statement |
| A **$1,850** collection for a phone account she never opened | All three | Reports identity theft at IdentityTheft.gov, sends the recovery-plan letters, disputes with all three bureaus, and freezes her credit |

About six weeks later, the late payment is corrected, the address is removed and the collection is deleted. Uma applies for the apartment with a clean report.

(Fictional example. Timelines vary; bureaus generally have about 30 days to investigate a dispute.)

## How to fix a mistake

The FTC advises contacting **both the credit bureau and the business that supplied the information** to dispute mistakes. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, you have the right to dispute errors, and the bureau must generally investigate.

- **Dispute with each bureau** that shows the error, online, by mail or by phone. Explain what's wrong and include copies (not originals) of your proof.
- **Contact the company** that reported it (the lender or collector) in writing too.
- **Keep records** of everything you send and receive, and the dates.
- **Check the result.** The bureau will tell you the outcome and give you a free updated report if something changed.

Full step-by-step instructions, with what to include: [How to dispute a credit report error](/blog/how-to-dispute-a-credit-report-error/).

## How often to check

Because weekly reports are free, you can check as often as you like. A simple routine for most people:

- **Every four months**, check one bureau, rotating through all three in a year. That spreads out your checks.
- **Before any big application** (an apartment, a car loan, a mortgage), check all three, early enough to fix errors.
- **After a data breach** or a lost wallet, check all three straight away.

Add a reminder to your calendar so it actually happens.

## Keep your report healthy

A clean report is one part; building a strong one is the other. The CFPB's advice on getting and keeping a good credit score comes down to paying on time, keeping credit card balances low compared with your limits, and only applying for credit you need. ([Credit utilization explained](/blog/credit-utilization-explained/).)

## How to read the account section

Each account entry usually shows the same few pieces of information. Knowing what they mean makes errors easier to spot:

- **Account type**: revolving (credit cards), instalment (car loans, student loans, mortgages) or collection.
- **Status**: open, closed, paid, or charged off. A paid-off loan should show as closed with a zero balance.
- **Balance and limit**: for cards, compare the balance with the limit to see your utilization.
- **Payment history**: often a month-by-month grid. "OK" or "current" means on time; "30," "60" or "90" means days late.
- **Date opened and date of last activity**: these affect how long an item stays on your report.

## Keep copies

Save a copy of each report you download, with the date, in a folder. If an error you disputed reappears, or a lender says something different from what you saw, you'll have a record of what your report showed and when. It also makes the next check faster: you only need to look for what has changed.

## Common questions

### Does checking my own credit report hurt my score?
No. Checking your own report is a soft inquiry and doesn't affect your credit score. Only hard inquiries, from applying for credit, can have a small effect.

### Why are my three credit reports different?
Not every lender reports to all three bureaus, and they may report at different times. That's why it's worth checking all three, especially before a big application.

### How long do late payments stay on a credit report?
Negative items such as late payments and most collections generally stay for up to seven years. Their effect on your score usually fades over time, especially if your recent history is good.

### Do I have to pay to fix errors on my credit report?
No. You can dispute errors yourself for free with the credit bureaus and the company that reported the information. Be wary of companies that charge to "repair" your credit and promise to remove accurate negative information.
