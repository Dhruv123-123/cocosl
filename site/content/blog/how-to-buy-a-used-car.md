A used car can save you thousands compared with a new one, but it's also a purchase where small decisions add up fast: the price, the condition, the loan, and the extras offered at the end. Buying well isn't about being a tough negotiator. It's about doing a few checks in the right order.

This guide walks through budgeting, financing, finding and checking a car, negotiating, and the paperwork, with a worked example of one purchase.

## Before you shop: set the budget

Start with what the car will cost you every month, not the sticker price:

- **Loan payment**, if you're borrowing.
- **Insurance**, which varies a lot by car model. Get quotes for the cars you're considering before you buy. ([How to save money on car insurance](/blog/how-to-save-money-on-car-insurance/).)
- **Fuel, maintenance and repairs.** Older cars usually need more repairs, so set aside money monthly. ([Car repair sinking fund](/blog/car-repair-sinking-fund/).)
- **Registration, taxes and fees**, which can add hundreds or more at purchase.

If you want a rule of thumb for the total, see [How much car can I afford?](/blog/how-much-car-can-i-afford/).

## How to buy a used car

### 1. Get preapproved for a loan
Before you visit a dealer, get a loan quote from a bank or credit union. The CFPB suggests starting with a preapproved offer showing a rate and a maximum loan amount: it tells you what you can borrow and gives you something to compare against the dealer's financing. The CFPB also notes that rate shopping within a short window, generally around 14 to 45 days, is usually counted as a single credit inquiry.

### 2. Shortlist reliable models
Pick two or three models known for reliability and reasonable repair costs. Check typical prices in your area for the year, mileage and trim you're looking at.

### 3. Read the Buyers Guide
The FTC says dealers must display a **Buyers Guide** on every used car they sell, including online. It tells you whether the car is sold **"as is"** (the dealer won't pay for repairs after the sale) or with a **warranty**, and what the warranty covers. If the dealer promises to fix something, get it written on the Buyers Guide: the FTC says the Guide overrides anything in your sales contract.

### 4. Check the history and recalls
A vehicle history report can show reported accidents, flood damage and title problems. Check for open safety recalls too. But the FTC notes that a history report usually won't show mechanical problems and isn't a substitute for an inspection.

### 5. Get an independent inspection
Pay an independent mechanic you choose to inspect the car, even if it's "certified." Ask for a written report with repair estimates. If a seller won't allow an inspection, walk away.

### 6. Test drive it properly
Drive on local roads and the highway, test the brakes, heating and air conditioning, and listen for noises. Check that every light, window and lock works.

### 7. Negotiate the price, then the financing
Agree on the car's price first, using the inspection report to negotiate. Only then talk about financing, trade-ins and extras, one at a time.

### 8. Read everything before you sign
Check that the paperwork matches the deal you agreed: the price, the APR, the loan length, and any add-ons. The CFPB's advice is simple: don't sign until you're satisfied.

## Dealer financing vs your own loan

There are two main ways to borrow:

- **Dealer-arranged financing.** The dealer sends your application to several lenders and offers you a loan. The CFPB explains that the lender gives the dealer a "buy rate," and the dealer may add extra interest on top as its compensation.
- **Direct financing.** You get a loan straight from a bank or credit union. The CFPB says this tends to be the cheaper option because you avoid the dealer's markup.

You can still accept the dealer's financing if it beats your preapproval, so ask the dealer whether they can do better. Compare the **APR and the total cost**, not only the monthly payment.

## Loan length: the monthly payment trap

A longer loan makes the monthly payment smaller, but you pay more interest and are more likely to owe more than the car is worth. The CFPB's auto loan guide gives an example: on a **$20,000** loan at **4.75%**, a 36-month term costs about **$1,498** in interest, while a 72-month term costs about **$3,024**, roughly twice as much. Some advisers suggest keeping car loans to five years or less.

## Add-ons are optional

At the end of the sale, you may be offered extended warranties or service contracts, GAP insurance, credit insurance, paint or fabric protection, and more. The CFPB notes these are optional, increase what you borrow, and can often be bought more cheaply elsewhere. The FTC adds that you can usually buy the car without them. If you want one, ask for the price and terms in writing, and compare.

## A worked example: Vic buys a used hatchback

Vic (a fictional example) finds a hatchback listed at **$14,000** and follows the steps:

| Step | What happens | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection ($150) | Mechanic finds worn brakes and two tyres, about $900 of work | Vic negotiates the price to **$13,100** |
| Add-ons | Vic declines a service contract and paint protection worth $1,400 | Not added to the loan |
| Down payment | $3,100 | Borrows **$10,000** |
| Financing | Credit union preapproval: 7% APR, 48 months | about $239 a month |

The dealer offers its own financing at **9% APR over 72 months**, about **$180 a month**. It looks cheaper each month, but:

| Loan | Monthly | Total interest | Paid off in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit union, 7%, 48 months | about $239 | about $1,500 | 4 years |
| Dealer, 9%, 72 months | about $180 | about $2,975 | 6 years |

Vic takes the credit union loan, saving roughly **$1,475** in interest and finishing two years sooner, and puts the $900 saved on the price toward the brake and tyre work.

(Fictional example with illustrative figures. Rates and costs depend on your credit, the car and the lender.)

## Buying from a private seller

Private sales can be cheaper, but you have fewer protections: the FTC's Used Car Rule applies to dealers, and private sales are usually as-is. Extra steps:

- **Check the title** is in the seller's name and has no lien.
- **Meet somewhere safe**, ideally at your mechanic's shop for the inspection.
- **Pay with a traceable method**, such as a cashier's check, and get a signed bill of sale.
- **Check your state's rules** for transferring the title and registration.

## After you buy

- **Know your return rights.** The FTC notes that federal law doesn't require dealers to give you a cancellation period, so check your state's rules and the contract.
- **Make sure the loan is final** before you drive away. The CFPB warns that a dealer may later ask you to accept a different rate if the financing wasn't finalised.
- **Set up automatic payments** and a monthly amount for repairs.

## Trade-ins: handle them separately

If you're trading in your old car, find out what it's worth before you go to the dealer: check typical trade-in values and get a written offer from another dealer or a car-buying service. Then negotiate the trade-in only after you've agreed the price of the car you're buying. If you still owe money on the trade-in, make sure the contract shows the old loan being paid off. Rolling an old loan balance into a new one means paying interest on a car you no longer own.

## Budget for the first year of ownership

A used car often needs some money soon after you buy it: tyres, brakes, a battery, or routine maintenance the last owner skipped. Use the inspection report as a guide and set aside an amount for the first year on top of the purchase price. A separate repair fund, topped up monthly, means a $600 repair is a planned cost rather than a credit card balance.

## Common questions

### Should I buy a used car with cash or a loan?
Cash avoids interest, but don't empty your emergency fund to do it. A modest loan with a low APR, paid off quickly, can be reasonable if it leaves you with savings for repairs and surprises.

### What does "as is" mean on a used car?
It means the dealer won't pay for any repairs after the sale. Anything that goes wrong is your cost. That makes an independent inspection before buying especially important.

### Is a certified pre-owned car worth it?
Certified cars usually come with an inspection and a manufacturer-backed warranty, and cost more. Compare the extra price with what the warranty covers, and still consider your own independent inspection.

### Is a bigger down payment better on a used car?
A larger down payment means borrowing less and paying less interest, and lowers the chance of owing more than the car is worth. Many people aim for a meaningful share of the price while keeping their emergency fund intact.
