Car registration isn't an emergency. It shows up every single year. So do holiday gifts, the annual subscription renewal, the dentist and the friend's wedding you said yes to in March.

These costs feel like surprises because they aren't monthly. A **sinking fund** fixes that: you save a small amount every month for a cost you know is coming, so when the bill arrives the money is already there.

## Sinking fund vs emergency fund

| | Sinking fund | Emergency fund |
|---|---|---|
| For | Costs you know are coming, just not monthly | Things you can't predict: job loss, medical bills, urgent travel |
| Amount | Calculated per cost | Typically 3–6 months of essential expenses (start with $1,000) |
| You spend it | On schedule, every year | Only in a real emergency, then refill it |

Mixing the two is how emergency funds quietly drain. If your "emergency" money paid for Christmas, it wasn't an emergency fund; it was an unlabelled gift fund.

## How to set up sinking funds in 20 minutes

### 1. List your irregular costs

Scroll your last 12 months of bank and card statements and note anything that isn't monthly. Common ones:

- Car: registration, insurance paid yearly, tyres, servicing
- Home: renter's insurance, furniture, moving costs
- Health: dentist, glasses, prescriptions, deductibles
- People: birthdays, holidays, weddings, travel to see family
- Subscriptions billed yearly: software, memberships, domain names
- Tech: replacing a phone or laptop every few years

### 2. Turn each into a monthly amount

Monthly amount = expected cost ÷ months until it's due.

| Fund | Cost | Due in | Monthly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Car registration | $180 | 12 months | $15 |
| Holiday gifts | $420 | 12 months | $35 |
| New phone | $720 | 24 months | $30 |
| Dentist + glasses | $300 | 12 months | $25 |
| **Total** | | | **$105** |

If something is due sooner, the monthly amount is higher for now. That's fine: it drops back once you've caught up.

### 3. Decide where the money lives

Simplest option: **one separate savings account** at an insured bank or credit union, with a spreadsheet or note that tracks how much of the balance belongs to each fund. Some banks let you create named "buckets" or sub-accounts inside one savings account, which does the tracking for you.

Keep it separate from checking so it doesn't look like spending money. A high-yield savings account earns some interest while it waits. Before choosing one, [check that it's actually insured](/blog/is-my-savings-app-fdic-insured/).

### 4. Automate the transfer

Set one automatic transfer of the total on payday. In the example above, that's $105 a month.

### 5. Spend it without guilt

When the registration bill comes, move $180 back to checking and pay it. That's the whole point. Nothing went wrong.

## What if I can't afford all of them yet?

Start with the two that hurt most last year. Even partial funding helps: $100 saved toward a $400 cost means you only need to find $300 when it lands. Add the rest as your budget frees up.

## Common questions

### How many sinking funds should I have?
Most people do fine with four to eight. Too many and tracking becomes a chore; group small ones (e.g. "subscriptions billed yearly").

### Are sinking funds the same as savings goals?
Similar mechanics, different job. A sinking fund is for a cost you'll definitely pay. A goal (a trip, a deposit) is something you're choosing to save for. Both can use the same approach.

### Do I need separate bank accounts for each one?
No. One savings account plus a simple tracker works. Named buckets are a nice extra if your bank offers them free.
