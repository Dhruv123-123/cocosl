A trip is one of the best things to spend money on, and one of the easiest to overspend on. The flight and hotel are the parts you plan; it's the meals, taxis, bag fees and "we're on holiday" purchases that turn a $1,200 trip into a $2,000 credit card bill.

This guide shows how to estimate the **full** cost of a trip, turn it into a monthly savings amount, and keep spending on track while you're away, so the only thing you bring home is photos.

## Step 1: list every cost, not just the big two

Most trip budgets fail because they only include travel and lodging. Use these categories:

| Category | What to include |
|---|---|
| Getting there | Flights, train or bus tickets, fuel and tolls, parking or a ride to the airport |
| Bags and seats | Checked and carry-on bag fees, seat selection |
| Lodging | Nightly rate plus taxes, resort or cleaning fees |
| Food | Every meal, coffee and snacks, per person per day |
| Getting around | Local transit, taxis or rideshares, car rental, fuel, parking |
| Activities | Tickets, tours, entrance fees |
| Documents and insurance | Passport or visa fees, travel insurance |
| Phone and money | International phone plan, card foreign transaction fees, ATM fees |
| Extras | Souvenirs, gifts, tips |
| Home costs | Pet sitting, plant watering, a missed shift |
| Buffer | 10% of everything above |

For food, a quick method is to look up a few typical menu prices for where you're going and multiply by the number of meals.

## Step 2: price it before you book

Spend half an hour with real prices:

- **Flights:** check fares for your dates and a few dates either side. Midweek is often cheaper. Add bag fees now, because they're easy to forget, and read what the fare includes. The Department of Transportation's air travel consumer pages explain your rights on refunds and delays.
- **Lodging:** compare the total including taxes and fees, not just the nightly rate.
- **Everything else:** use the table above and fill in an estimate for each line.

Add it all up, then add a **10% buffer**. That's your trip number.

## Step 3: turn it into a monthly savings goal

**(Trip number − what you've already saved) ÷ months until you go = your monthly savings goal.**

If you're paid every two weeks, halve it to get a per-paycheck amount.

Put the money in a separate savings account or a labelled "trip" bucket, and set up an automatic transfer on payday. This is a **sinking fund**: you save for a known future cost in advance, so it doesn't land on a credit card. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

If the monthly figure is too high, you have three levers: **go later** (more months to save), **go cheaper** (fewer nights, a different time of year, a less expensive destination) or **cut something now** (a subscription or two to fund the trip). ([Find and cancel forgotten subscriptions](/blog/find-and-cancel-subscriptions/).)

## Step 4: book in the right order

1. **Passport or documents first**, if you need them. US passport processing can take weeks, and fees are listed on the State Department's site.
2. **Flights**, once your savings plan shows you can afford the trip.
3. **Lodging**, ideally with free cancellation.
4. **Activities that sell out.** Leave the rest flexible.

Paying for big items as you save, rather than all at once, works well, as long as you only book what's already in the trip fund.

## Step 5: set a daily spending number for the trip

Before you leave, take everything that you'll spend on the trip itself (food, local transport, activities, extras) and divide by the number of days. That's your **daily number**.

Each evening, check what you spent. If you're under, the rest carries over. If you're over, you know to adjust tomorrow, not when the statement arrives.

A simple way to keep it visible: use a debit card linked to your trip account, or a note on your phone. ([The four-line budget's weekly number](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-you-will-keep/) works the same way.)

## A worked example: Ivy's five-night trip

Ivy (a fictional example) wants to take a five-night trip in **six months**, travelling solo. Ivy priced each line before booking:

| Cost | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Return flight, including one checked bag | $420 |
| Lodging: 5 nights × $110, with taxes | $550 |
| Food: 6 days × $55 | $330 |
| Local transport | $90 |
| Activities | $160 |
| Travel insurance | $45 |
| Souvenirs and extras | $80 |
| **Subtotal** | **$1,675** |
| Buffer (10%) | $170 |
| **Trip number** | **$1,845** |

Ivy already has **$300** in a trip savings bucket.

- Gap: $1,845 − $300 = **$1,545**
- Monthly: $1,545 ÷ 6 = about **$258**
- Per biweekly paycheck: about **$129**

That's a lot for Ivy's budget, so Ivy cancels two unused subscriptions ($28 a month) and cuts takeout by $60 a month, which covers about a third of it, and trims the rest from the "fun" budget line.

**On the trip:** spending money (food, transport, activities, extras) is $660 over 6 days, so Ivy's **daily number is $110**. On day 2 Ivy spends $150 on a cooking class and dinner, then eats in on day 3 to get back on track. Ivy comes home with $90 unspent from the buffer, which goes into the next trip fund.

## Fees that sneak in

- **Foreign transaction fees.** Many credit and debit cards charge around 3% on purchases abroad. Check your card's terms before you go, and consider one with no foreign transaction fee.
- **Paying in dollars abroad.** If a card machine or ATM offers to charge you in your home currency, it's usually worse than paying in the local currency.
- **ATM fees.** Both your bank and the local ATM may charge. Fewer, larger withdrawals can help.
- **Resort and cleaning fees** on lodging, and **bag and seat fees** on flights.
- **Phone roaming.** Check your plan's international options, or use Wi-Fi and a local SIM or eSIM.

## Travel rewards: helpful, but not a budget

Card points and airline miles can cut the cost of a trip. But they only save money if you pay the card off in full each month. Card interest at around 20% or more will cost far more than the rewards are worth. ([How credit card interest works](/blog/how-credit-card-interest-works/).) Put the trip on a card for the protections and the rewards if you like, then pay it off from the trip fund.

## If the trip is a group trip

Group trips have their own traps: someone books the expensive place, the restaurant bill gets split evenly whatever you ordered, and costs get settled weeks later. Agree on a total budget per person before anyone books, decide in advance how shared costs like a rental or a group meal will be split, and use a shared expense app or a simple spreadsheet so everyone settles up before the trip ends. If the group's plans are above your budget, it's fine to join for part of the trip or skip the most expensive activity.

## Paying for a trip that's already booked

If you've booked a trip before saving for it, the same method still works: list the remaining costs, divide by the weeks left, and save that amount from every paycheck. If part of it ends up on a card, make a payoff plan before you go, so the balance is cleared within a month or two of getting home.

## After you get home

Within a week, compare what you spent with your plan. Note which lines were too low and which were too high, and adjust your next estimate. Then restart the trip fund, even at a small amount: next year's trip is cheaper when it starts saving now.

## Common questions

### How much should I budget for food on a trip?
Look up typical menu prices for your destination and multiply by the meals you'll eat out. Many travellers save by having breakfast where they stay or buying snacks from a grocery store, and keeping restaurant meals for dinner.

### Is travel insurance worth it?
It depends on what you'd lose if the trip were cancelled or you got sick abroad. It's often worth considering for expensive non-refundable trips and international travel where your health insurance may not apply. Check what your credit card already covers before buying.

### Should I use cash or card when travelling abroad?
A card with no foreign transaction fee is usually the cheapest and safest option for most purchases, with a small amount of local cash for places that don't take cards. Always choose to pay in the local currency.

### How far ahead should I start saving for a vacation?
As early as you can. Six months gives you time to save a reasonable monthly amount and book flights at decent prices. For a big international trip, a year is more comfortable.
