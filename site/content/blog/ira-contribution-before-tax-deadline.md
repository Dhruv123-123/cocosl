Most tax moves have to happen by December 31. One of the few you can still make after the year is over is an **IRA contribution**. You can contribute to a traditional or Roth IRA **for 2026** right up until the tax filing deadline, **April 15, 2027**. If it's a deductible traditional IRA contribution, it lowers your 2026 tax bill, even though you're making it in 2027.

This guide explains the limits, how to make sure the money counts for the right year, how to choose between traditional and Roth, and a worked example of what a contribution in March saves.

*General education, not investment advice. Investing involves risk, including loss of principal.*

## The 2026 limits

- **Contribution limit:** **$7,500** across all your IRAs (traditional and Roth combined), or your taxable compensation for the year if that's less.
- **Catch-up for age 50 and over:** an extra **$1,100**, for a total of $8,600.
- **Deadline:** the due date of your 2026 return, **April 15, 2027**. A filing extension does *not* extend the IRA contribution deadline.

You need **earned income** (wages, salary, tips or self-employment income) to contribute. A spouse with little or no income can contribute to a "spousal IRA" based on the working spouse's income if you file jointly.

## Make sure it counts for the right year

Between January 1 and April 15, a contribution could be for either the previous year or the current year. Your IRA provider will ask which year you mean; if you don't say, many providers default to the **current** year. When you contribute online, look for a choice like "prior year contribution" or "2026 contribution", and check your confirmation. If you mail a check, write the tax year on it.

The provider reports contributions on **Form 5498**, which often arrives in May, after you've filed. That's normal. You report a deductible contribution on your return using your own records.

## Traditional or Roth?

You can split the $7,500 between them, but the total limit is shared.

**Traditional IRA:**
- Contributions may be **deductible**, lowering this year's tax (here, the 2026 tax).
- Money grows tax-deferred; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income.
- Whether it's deductible depends on whether you (or your spouse) are covered by a retirement plan at work, and your income. If you're not covered by a workplace plan, it's generally fully deductible. If you are, the 2026 deduction phases out between **$81,000 and $91,000** of modified AGI for single filers, and **$129,000 to $149,000** for married couples filing jointly where the contributor is covered.

**Roth IRA:**
- **No deduction now**, so it doesn't lower your 2026 tax.
- Money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
- For 2026, the ability to contribute phases out between **$153,000 and $168,000** of modified AGI for single filers, and **$242,000 to $252,000** for married couples filing jointly.

A common way to decide: if your tax rate is low now and you expect it to be higher later, Roth often makes sense; if your rate is high now, a deductible traditional contribution saves more today. ([Roth vs traditional IRA](/blog/roth-vs-traditional-ira/).) For the specific goal of lowering last year's tax bill, only a **deductible traditional** contribution does that.

## A worked example: Theo's March contribution

Theo (a fictional example) is single and earned **$42,000** in 2026. Theo's employer doesn't offer a retirement plan, so a traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible. In March 2027, while doing taxes, Theo sees a balance due and decides to contribute **$3,000** to a traditional IRA, marked as a **2026** contribution.

**Without the contribution:**

- Taxable income: $42,000 − $16,100 standard deduction = **$25,900**
- Tax: 10% of $12,400 + 12% of $13,500 = **$2,860**

**With the $3,000 contribution:**

- AGI: $42,000 − $3,000 = $39,000
- Taxable income: $39,000 − $16,100 = **$22,900**
- Tax: 10% of $12,400 + 12% of $10,500 = **$2,500**

The deduction saves **$360**: 12% of $3,000.

**There's a second benefit.** Theo's AGI is now $39,000, under the 2026 **Saver's Credit** limit of $40,250 for single filers. The Saver's Credit is worth 10%, 20% or 50% of up to $2,000 of retirement contributions, depending on income; at Theo's AGI, the 10% rate applies, so it's worth **$200**. Without the contribution, Theo's AGI would have been $42,000, too high to qualify.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Tax saved by the deduction | $360 |
| Saver's Credit | $200 |
| **Total 2026 tax reduction** | **$560** |
| Cost of the $3,000 contribution after tax savings | $2,440 |

So Theo moves $3,000 into retirement savings for a net cost of about $2,440, and the money is invested for decades. (Starting with the 2027 tax year, the Saver's Credit is scheduled to be replaced by a "Saver's Match" paid into your retirement account, so check the rules for future years.)

Theo also files a new W-4 for 2027, since the balance due was a sign of under-withholding. ([How to fill out a W-4](/blog/how-to-fill-out-a-w-4/).)

## If you don't have $3,000 spare

A contribution doesn't have to be large to count. Even $500 before April 15 lowers taxable income by $500 (about $60 in the 12% bracket) and can qualify you for the Saver's Credit if your income is within the limits. Some people use their refund for this: you can claim the contribution on your return, file early, and make the contribution from the refund, as long as the money reaches the IRA by April 15. If the contribution isn't made by the deadline, you'll need to amend the return.

A better long-term habit is to contribute monthly through the year, so there's no scramble in March. Even $100 a month is $1,200 by year end, and most IRA providers offer automatic monthly transfers. ([How to start investing with $100 a month](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-100-a-month/).)

## Traditional IRA deductions when you have a workplace plan

If you're covered by a 401(k) or similar plan at work, a traditional IRA contribution is still allowed, but the deduction depends on income. For 2026, single filers with modified AGI below $81,000 can deduct the full amount; between $81,000 and $91,000 the deduction shrinks; above $91,000 it isn't deductible. A non-deductible traditional contribution is allowed, but it doesn't lower your tax, and you need to track it on Form 8606 so you don't pay tax on it twice later. For most people in that situation, a Roth IRA (if eligible) or more in the workplace plan is simpler. ([First-job 401(k) setup](/blog/first-job-401k-setup/).)

## What to invest the money in

Contributing to an IRA and investing the money are two separate steps. A contribution often lands in a cash or money market position until you choose an investment. Many beginners use a single low-cost target-date index fund or a broad index fund, and check its expense ratio. ([Expense ratios explained](/blog/expense-ratios-explained/).) If you're opening your first IRA, compare providers on fees, fund choices and minimums. ([Where to open a Roth IRA](/blog/where-to-open-a-roth-ira/).)

Investments in an IRA can lose value, especially over short periods. Money you'll need within the next few years usually belongs in savings, not an IRA.

## Where the money comes from matters too

A last-minute contribution is a good move only if it doesn't leave you short. If making it would empty your emergency fund or mean carrying a credit card balance, the interest you'd pay could outweigh the tax saved. Build a starter emergency fund first, then contribute what you can. ([How to build an emergency fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)

## Common mistakes

- **Contributing for the wrong year.** Choose the prior year explicitly between January and April 15.
- **Going over the limit** across all your IRAs. Excess contributions are taxed 6% a year until fixed, so correct them before the deadline if you can.
- **Contributing without earned income.** You need taxable compensation at least equal to your contribution.
- **Assuming an extension gives more time.** It extends the filing deadline, not the contribution deadline.
- **Claiming a deduction you don't qualify for** because you're covered by a workplace plan and over the income range.

## Common questions

### Can I contribute to an IRA for last year?
Yes. You can make contributions for a tax year until the tax filing deadline for that year, usually April 15 of the next year. For 2026, the deadline is April 15, 2027. Tell your IRA provider which year the contribution is for.

### How much can I put in an IRA for 2026?
Up to $7,500 in total across all your traditional and Roth IRAs, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older, and no more than your taxable compensation for the year.

### Does a Roth IRA contribution lower my taxes?
Not now. Roth contributions aren't deductible, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Only a deductible traditional IRA contribution lowers this year's taxable income.

### Can I contribute to an IRA if I have a 401(k)?
Yes. Anyone with earned income can contribute to an IRA. Having a workplace plan only affects whether a traditional IRA contribution is deductible, depending on your income, and Roth eligibility depends on income regardless.
