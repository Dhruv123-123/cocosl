Sometimes April 15 arrives before you're ready. A corrected tax form hasn't shown up, a big life change made your return complicated, or you simply ran out of time. The fix is free and takes about five minutes: a **tax extension**, which gives you until **October 15** to file.

But an extension has one big catch that catches people out every year: **it gives you more time to file, not more time to pay.** This guide explains how to get one, how to estimate what you owe, and a worked example of what the extension saves and what it doesn't.

## What an extension does

- **Moves your filing deadline** for 2026 returns from April 15, 2027, to **October 15, 2027**.
- **Avoids the failure-to-file penalty**, which is generally 5% of the unpaid tax per month (or part of a month) the return is late, up to 25%.
- **Is automatic.** You don't need a reason, and the IRS doesn't approve or deny it.

What it doesn't do:

- **It doesn't extend the time to pay.** Any tax you owe is still due April 15. Unpaid tax after that date collects interest and a failure-to-pay penalty (generally 0.5% a month).
- **It doesn't extend the IRA contribution deadline.** Contributions for 2026 still have to be made by April 15, 2027. ([IRA contributions before the deadline](/blog/ira-contribution-before-tax-deadline/).)
- **It doesn't automatically cover your state return.** Many states accept the federal extension; some require their own form.

## How to request an extension

### 1. Estimate what you owe
Use last year's return, your W-2s and 1099s so far, and tax software to make your best estimate of your total 2026 tax. Subtract what was withheld and any estimated payments. A reasonable estimate is all that's required.

### 2. Choose how to request it
There are three ways, all free:
- **Pay all or part of your estimated balance** through IRS Direct Pay, your IRS online account, or a debit or credit card, and mark the payment as an **extension** payment. The IRS treats the payment itself as your extension request; no form needed.
- **File Form 4868 electronically** through IRS Free File or tax software, with or without a payment.
- **Mail a paper Form 4868**, postmarked by April 15.

### 3. Pay as much as you can by April 15
Even if you can't pay it all, paying most of it limits the interest and penalties. If you can't pay at all, still request the extension. ([Can't pay your taxes?](/blog/what-to-do-if-you-cant-pay-your-taxes/).)

### 4. Save the confirmation
Keep the confirmation number or the payment receipt showing it was marked as an extension.

### 5. File by October 15
Put the date in your calendar now, with a reminder in September. Filing earlier is fine.

## A worked example: Rowan waits for a corrected form

Rowan (a fictional example) sold some investments last year, and the brokerage has said a **corrected Form 1099-B** won't be ready until May. Rowan's draft return shows about **$1,200** owed. Three ways it could go:

**A: no extension, no payment, file in June.** Rowan doesn't file or pay until June 20, a little over two months late (which counts as three months, since part-months count). Penalties are calculated together: the failure-to-file penalty is 5% a month, reduced by the 0.5% failure-to-pay penalty for the same months. Over three months, that's roughly **15% of $1,200, about $180**, plus interest.

**B: extension plus full estimated payment.** On April 10, Rowan pays **$1,200** through IRS Direct Pay and marks it as an extension payment. The corrected form arrives in May; Rowan files in June. If the final tax is $1,200 or less, there are **no penalties and no interest**, and any overpayment comes back as a refund.

**C: extension, but the estimate was a bit low.** Rowan pays $900 with the extension and the final tax turns out to be $1,200. No failure-to-file penalty, but the **$300** unpaid since April collects the 0.5% monthly failure-to-pay penalty (about **$1.50 a month**) plus interest until it's paid. That's a few dollars, not $180.

| | Penalties (approximate) |
|---|---|
| A: no extension, no payment, filed 3 months late | about $180 + interest |
| B: extension + $1,200 paid | $0 |
| C: extension + $900 paid | a few dollars + interest on $300 |

The lesson: **the extension saves the big penalty, and the payment saves the rest.** A good-faith estimate, paid on time, is what keeps the cost close to zero.

## Extensions and refunds

If you're due a refund, an extension costs you nothing but time: there's no failure-to-file penalty when no tax is owed. You generally have three years from the original deadline to claim a refund, after which it's lost. But a refund is your own money, so unless you're waiting on a form, the best plan is still to file as early as you can.

An extension also doesn't change anything about how the IRS treats your return. It doesn't increase your chance of an audit, and it doesn't appear anywhere a lender or employer would see it.

## Who gets extra time automatically

- **People in federally declared disaster areas** often get postponed deadlines for both filing and paying. Check the IRS's disaster relief page.
- **US citizens and residents living abroad** generally get an automatic two-month extension to June 15 to file (and pay, though interest still applies from April 15).
- **Members of the military serving in a combat zone** get extra time, generally at least 180 days after leaving the combat zone.

## Should you file an extension?

An extension is a good idea when:

- you're waiting on a form or a corrected form;
- your situation is complicated this year (a business, a home sale, a move between states) and you want to get it right;
- you're working with a tax professional who's busy before April.

It's usually not worth it if your return is simple and you're due a refund. If you're getting money back, there's no penalty for filing late, but there's also no reason to wait for your own money. File as soon as you can.

If you're getting a refund and need more time anyway, you can still file the extension. It does no harm.

## How to estimate what you owe

You don't need an exact figure, just a reasonable one. Three quick ways:

1. **Start a draft return in tax software** with what you have, and add a rough figure for the missing form. Many programs show a running estimate of the refund or balance due.
2. **Use last year as a guide.** If your income and situation are similar, last year's total tax, minus this year's withholding and estimated payments, is a decent estimate.
3. **Round up.** If you're unsure, pay a little more than your estimate. Any overpayment comes back as a refund when you file, while an underpayment costs interest and penalties.

If you made quarterly estimated payments during the year, include them in the calculation. ([Self-employment tax and estimated payments](/blog/self-employment-tax-explained/).)

## Once you've extended: what to do before October

An extension is only useful if you use the time:

- **Chase the missing paperwork** early, rather than in September.
- **File as soon as you're ready.** There's no need to wait until October, and if your estimate was too high, filing earlier gets the overpayment back sooner.
- **Pay the difference if you underestimated.** If your draft shows you owe more than you paid in April, pay it as soon as you know, to stop interest and penalties growing.
- **Don't miss October 15.** For most people there's no second federal extension. Missing it brings back the failure-to-file penalty, counted from April.

## Don't forget your state

Most states with an income tax have their own extension rules. Some accept the federal extension automatically, some require a separate state form, and most still expect payment by the original deadline. Check your state tax agency's website before April 15.

## Common questions

### Does a tax extension give me more time to pay?
No. An extension gives you until October 15 to file, but any tax you owe is still due by April 15. Interest and a failure-to-pay penalty apply to anything unpaid after that date, so pay your best estimate when you request the extension.

### Is a tax extension free?
Yes. You can request one free through IRS Free File, tax software, a paper Form 4868, or by making a payment through IRS Direct Pay and marking it as an extension payment.

### What happens if I file late without an extension?
If you owe tax, you'll generally face a failure-to-file penalty of 5% of the unpaid tax per month, up to 25%, plus interest and a failure-to-pay penalty. If you're due a refund, there's usually no penalty, but you generally need to file within three years to claim it.

### Do I need a reason to get a tax extension?
No. Extensions are automatic for anyone who requests one by the deadline. The IRS doesn't need an explanation.
