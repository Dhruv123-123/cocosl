A starter emergency fund turns a flat tire from a crisis into an errand. Without one, every surprise goes on a credit card, and the interest makes the next surprise harder to handle.

$1,000 in 8 weeks is **$125 a week**. Here's where it can come from.

## Why $1,000 first?

The usual long-term target is **three to six months of essential expenses**. For most people starting out, that's a big, demotivating number. A first $1,000 is reachable in weeks and covers a lot of common surprises: a car repair, a vet bill, a last-minute flight, a deductible.

Once you have it, keep building toward the bigger target, alongside paying down high-interest debt.

## Where to keep it

- **Separate from checking**, so it doesn't look like spending money.
- **At an insured bank or credit union.** FDIC (banks) and NCUA (credit unions) insurance covers deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
- **Easy to reach**, within a day or two, but not linked to your debit card.

A high-yield savings account often pays far more interest than a standard savings account; the FDIC publishes the national average rate if you want a benchmark. If you use an app rather than a bank, [check who actually holds the money](/blog/is-my-savings-app-fdic-insured/).

## The 8-week plan

| Weeks | Move | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | **Sell two things you haven't used in a year**: an old phone, gaming gear, furniture, clothes | Often $100–$300 (illustrative) |
| 3–4 | **Pause two subscriptions and one delivery habit** for a month, and move the exact amount you would have spent on the same day | Varies; move it immediately |
| 5–6 | **One extra shift, weekend gig or a few hours of freelance work**, sent straight to savings before it touches checking | Varies |
| 7–8 | **Close the gap on purpose.** $40 short? Skip one dinner out and move the difference | Whatever's left |

Then automate it: set a weekly transfer of whatever you can sustain (even $25) so the fund keeps growing after week 8.

## The rules for using it

1. **Real emergencies only**: car repair, medical bill, urgent travel, a gap between jobs. Not sales, not holidays (that's what [sinking funds](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/) are for).
2. **Use it without guilt.** That's its job.
3. **Refill it before anything else.** After you dip into it, pause extra goals until it's back to $1,000.

## Common questions

### Should I pay off debt or build an emergency fund first?
Many people do both in order: a small starter fund first (so new surprises don't go on a card), then aggressive payoff of high-interest debt, then the full 3–6 month fund. Minimum payments always come first.

### Is $1,000 enough?
As a start, yes. The long-term target is 3–6 months of essential expenses, more if your income is irregular or you support others.

### Should I invest my emergency fund?
Generally no. The point is that it's there, at full value, when you need it. Investments can be down exactly when you need the money.

### What counts as essential expenses?
Housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments. Not eating out or subscriptions.
