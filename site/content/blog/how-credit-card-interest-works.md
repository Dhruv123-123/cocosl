Credit card interest is the most expensive money most people ever borrow. According to Federal Reserve data, the average interest rate on credit card accounts that were charged interest was about **22.4%** in August 2026. At that rate, a balance roughly doubles in a little over three years if you never pay it down.

And yet many cardholders never pay a cent of interest, because of one feature most people only half understand: the grace period. This guide explains how card interest is actually calculated, how to avoid it completely, and what happens to a $3,000 balance if you only pay the minimum.

## APR, explained

Every card has an **annual percentage rate (APR)**. Most cards have several:

- **Purchase APR:** the rate on things you buy. This is the one people usually mean.
- **Balance transfer APR:** the rate on debt moved from another card, often a low promotional rate for a set period, then a higher one.
- **Cash advance APR:** usually higher than the purchase rate, and with no grace period, so interest starts the day you take the cash. There's usually a fee too.
- **Penalty APR:** a higher rate some issuers can apply if you pay more than 60 days late.

Most card APRs are **variable**: they're tied to a benchmark (usually the prime rate) plus a margin. When the Federal Reserve raises or lowers rates, card rates generally follow within a billing cycle or two.

## How a yearly rate becomes daily interest

Issuers don't charge APR once a year. Most divide it into a **daily periodic rate** and charge interest on your balance every day. A typical method:

1. **Daily rate = APR ÷ 365.** At 24.99%, that's about 0.0685% a day.
2. **Average daily balance:** the issuer adds up your balance at the end of each day in the billing cycle and divides by the number of days.
3. **Interest for the cycle = average daily balance × daily rate × days in the cycle.**

On a $3,000 average daily balance over a 30-day cycle at 24.99%, that's about **$61.62** of interest for the month. Many issuers also compound daily, which means today's interest is added to the balance that tomorrow's interest is charged on. The effect over a month is small, but over years it adds up.

Two practical consequences:

- **When you pay matters.** A payment early in the cycle lowers your average daily balance for more days than a payment on the due date. Paying twice a month can trim interest slightly.
- **A purchase you carry costs interest from the day it posts** (once you've lost the grace period), not from the statement date.

## The grace period: how to pay zero interest

Most cards give you a **grace period** on purchases: if you pay your **full statement balance** by the due date, you pay no interest on the purchases from that statement. Federal rules require issuers to mail or deliver your statement at least 21 days before the payment is due.

The catch: if you **don't** pay the statement balance in full, most cards remove the grace period. Interest is then charged on the unpaid balance *and* on new purchases from the day you make them. To get the grace period back, you usually need to pay the full balance for one or two cycles in a row, depending on the card.

This is why "pay it off every month" is the most valuable credit card habit. It turns a card from a 24% loan into a free 21-to-55-day loan, with fraud protection and often rewards. Set up autopay for the full statement balance if your cash flow allows; if not, autopay the minimum as a safety net and pay the rest by hand.

## The minimum payment trap

Your minimum payment is designed to keep the account current, not to get you out of debt. A common formula is **1% of the balance plus that month's interest and fees**, with a floor (often $25 to $40). As the balance shrinks, so does the minimum, which is why paying only the minimum can take a decade or more.

Card statements must show how long it would take to pay off the balance with minimum payments only, and how much you'd pay in total. It's worth reading.

## A worked example: Jess's $3,000

Jess (a fictional example) has a **$3,000** balance on a card at **24.99% APR** and stops using the card. Here's how long it takes to clear, and what it costs, depending on what Jess pays each month:

| Monthly payment | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum only (1% + interest, $35 floor) | about 152 months (12.7 years) | about **$4,659** |
| $100 fixed | 48 months | about $1,756 |
| $150 fixed | 27 months | about $921 |
| $275 fixed | 13 months | about $439 |

Look at the first row. Paying the minimum, Jess would pay about **$4,659 in interest on a $3,000 balance**, more than the original debt, and still be paying more than 12 years later.

Now compare the first two rows. The minimum payment starts at about $92 (1% of $3,000 plus about $62 of interest), so the very first $100 payment is only $8 more. But a **fixed** $100 keeps paying $100 as the balance falls, while the minimum keeps shrinking. That single choice, to keep paying the same amount, cuts the payoff from almost 13 years to 4, and saves about $2,900.

Jess decides on $150 a month, paid in two $75 halves on each payday, which lowers the average daily balance a little more. It's done in a little over two years, for less than a third of the interest of the minimum.

The lesson: **pick a fixed payment and keep it**, even as the minimum falls. If you get a raise, raise the payment.

## How to pay less interest

In rough order of impact:

1. **Stop adding new purchases** to a card with a balance. Without a grace period, every new purchase is charged interest from day one. Use a debit card or cash while you pay it down.
2. **Pay a fixed amount, above the minimum.** Even $25 more a month makes a big difference over time.
3. **Ask for a lower rate.** Call the number on the back of the card and ask. If you've paid on time for a year, there's a fair chance of a reduction, and it takes ten minutes.
4. **Target the highest-rate card first** if you have several. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)
5. **Consider a 0% balance transfer**, if you can pay the balance off before the promotion ends and the fee is small. ([Balance transfer vs personal loan](/compare/balance-transfer-vs-personal-loan/).)
6. **Look at consolidation** if you have large balances on several cards and can qualify for a lower rate. ([When consolidation saves money](/blog/debt-consolidation-explained/).)

## Watch out for "deferred interest" offers

Some store cards and financing deals advertise "no interest if paid in full in 12 months". That's not the same as a 0% APR. With **deferred interest**, interest is quietly calculated from the date of purchase the whole time. If even a small part of the balance is left when the promotion ends, all of that interest is usually added to your account at once. A true 0% intro APR, by contrast, only starts charging interest on the remaining balance after the promotion ends. Read the terms for the words "deferred interest" or "if paid in full", and divide the balance by the number of months so it's gone in time.

## What interest does to your credit score

Interest itself doesn't affect your score, but the balances that cause it do. **Credit utilization**, the share of your card limits you're using, is one of the biggest factors in most scoring models. A $3,000 balance on a $5,000 limit is 60% utilization, which usually weighs on a score. As Jess pays down the balance, the score is likely to improve along with it. ## Common questions

### Is there interest if I clear the full statement balance?
Usually not on purchases. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, the grace period means no interest on those purchases. Cash advances and some balance transfers are charged interest right away, even if you pay in full.

### Why was I charged interest when I paid my balance?
The most common reason is that you paid the current balance in a month after carrying a balance, and interest had built up between the statement date and your payment (sometimes called trailing or residual interest). It can also happen if you paid less than the full statement balance, or had a cash advance. Paying the full statement balance for one or two cycles in a row usually restores the grace period.

### Does paying before the due date lower the interest?
Paying early in the cycle lowers your average daily balance, which slightly reduces interest if you carry a balance. It can also lower the balance reported to the credit bureaus, which may help your utilization. If you pay in full each month, the main thing is to pay by the due date.

### How is credit card interest calculated each month?
Most issuers divide the APR by 365 to get a daily rate, then apply it to your average daily balance for each day of the billing cycle. On a $3,000 balance at 24.99%, that works out to roughly $62 of interest in a 30-day month.
