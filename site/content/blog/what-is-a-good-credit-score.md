Credit scores are everywhere: on loan applications, apartment screenings, sometimes even insurance quotes. But the numbers come with little explanation. Is 680 good? Is 720 enough to get a good rate? Why does your bank show one number and your card app another?

This guide explains the score ranges, what each band typically means in practice, why you have many scores rather than one, and how to move up a band.

## The FICO ranges

FICO scores, used in most lending decisions, run from **300 to 850**. FICO describes the bands roughly like this:

| FICO score | Description |
|---|---|
| Below 580 | Poor |
| 580 to 669 | Fair |
| 670 to 739 | Good |
| 740 to 799 | Very good |
| 800 and above | Exceptional |

**670 or above is generally considered "good."** Above 740, many lenders treat you as a low-risk borrower and offer their better rates.

## VantageScore ranges

VantageScore, the other main score, also runs from 300 to 850 in its current versions but uses slightly different band labels. Many free score apps show VantageScore rather than FICO. The same habits raise both, but the numbers won't always match.

## What each band usually means

These are general patterns, not rules. Every lender sets its own criteria, and your income, debts and the type of loan matter too.

- **Below 580:** many mainstream lenders will decline, or offer only high-cost credit. Secured cards and credit-builder products are the usual starting points. Landlords may ask for a bigger deposit or a co-signer.
- **580 to 669:** you'll often be approved, but at higher interest rates. Some apartments and utilities may ask for deposits.
- **670 to 739:** most loans and cards are within reach, with reasonable rates.
- **740 to 799:** you'll typically see better rates and more card offers.
- **800 and above:** there's usually little extra benefit compared with the high 700s. Past about 760 to 780, many lenders already offer their best terms.

## Why the band matters: a cost example

The score itself doesn't cost you anything; the interest rate attached to it does. As an illustration, take a **$25,000** car loan over five years:

| APR | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 9.0% | about $519 | about $6,140 |
| 6.5% | about $489 | about $4,350 |

A 2.5-point difference in rate, the kind that can separate score bands, costs about **$1,790** over the loan. On a mortgage, the difference can be tens of thousands. (Rates are illustrative, not current offers.) ([How much car can I afford?](/blog/how-much-car-can-i-afford/).)

## Why you have many scores

You don't have one credit score. You have dozens:

- **Three bureaus.** Equifax, Experian and TransUnion each hold a slightly different report, because not every lender reports to all three.
- **Several models.** There are many FICO versions (including ones made for car loans and credit cards) and several VantageScore versions.
- **Different dates.** Scores update as your reports change, often monthly.

So a 712 in one app and a 698 on a lender's report can both be right. What matters is the **band** and the **direction**, not the exact number.

## What goes into the score

FICO's factors, in rough order of weight:

1. **Payment history (about 35%).** On-time payments matter most. A 30-day late payment can stay on your reports for up to seven years, though its impact fades.
2. **Amounts owed (about 30%).** Especially your **credit utilization**: card balances divided by card limits. Lower is better, and under 30% is a common guideline; many people with very high scores use under 10%.
3. **Length of credit history (about 15%).** The age of your oldest account and the average age of all of them.
4. **New credit (about 10%).** Recent applications and newly opened accounts.
5. **Credit mix (about 10%).** Having both cards and instalment loans helps a little.

## A worked example: Zane moves up a band

Zane (a fictional example) checks a free score and sees about **655**, in the fair range. Zane wants a car loan next year and pulls all three free reports to see why.

**What the reports show:**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Card A | $1,400 balance on a $2,000 limit |
| Card B | $1,600 balance on a $3,000 limit |
| Utilization | $3,000 ÷ $5,000 = **60%** |
| Late payments | One 30-day late payment, three years ago |
| Applications | None in the past year |
| Errors | None |

The late payment is old and fading, so the biggest thing Zane can change is **utilization**.

**Zane's plan:**

1. Set up **autopay for at least the minimum** on both cards, so there's no new late payment.
2. Pay **$420 a month** extra toward the balances, highest interest rate first, and stop putting new spending on the cards. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)
3. **No new applications** until the car loan.

**After five months**, Zane's balances total about $900, so utilization is **about 18%**. With lower utilization and another five months of on-time payments, it's reasonable to expect the score to rise, and moving into the good range is a realistic goal. Zane also pays less interest along the way.

(Fictional example. Score changes depend on your whole credit report and the scoring model, and nobody can promise a specific result.)

## How to check your score for free

- **Your free credit reports** from all three bureaus are available at AnnualCreditReport.com. Reports don't include a score, but they show everything a score is based on.
- **Your bank or card issuer** may show a free FICO score or VantageScore in its app.
- **Free score services** usually show a VantageScore.

Checking your own score is a soft inquiry: it never lowers it.

## How to move up a band faster

- **Lower your utilization.** Pay down balances, or pay before the statement date so a lower balance is reported. This often has the fastest effect.
- **Fix errors.** A wrong late payment or an account that isn't yours can drag a score down. Dispute it with the bureau. ([How to dispute a credit report error](/blog/how-to-dispute-a-credit-report-error/).)
- **Never miss a payment.** Autopay for the minimum is the safety net.
- **Keep old cards open**, especially ones without an annual fee.
- **Space out applications.** Apply only for credit you need.

If you're starting from no score at all, the path is different. ([How to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/).)

## Who looks at your score, and why

- **Lenders** use it to decide whether to approve you and at what rate, for cards, car loans, personal loans and mortgages.
- **Landlords** often check credit as part of a rental application. A lower score may mean a bigger deposit or a request for a co-signer.
- **Utility and phone companies** may use it to decide whether to ask for a deposit.
- **Insurers** in many states use credit-based insurance scores, which are related but not identical, when pricing car and home insurance.
- **Employers** may check your credit report (not your score) for some jobs, but only with your written permission.

Because so many decisions use it, a score in the good range saves money in ways you may not notice: smaller deposits, lower premiums and fewer extra checks.

## How long negative items stay on your report

- **Late payments:** up to seven years from the date of the delinquency.
- **Collections and charge-offs:** generally up to seven years.
- **Bankruptcy:** up to ten years, depending on the type.
- **Hard inquiries:** two years, though FICO only counts the last 12 months.

Their effect on your score fades over time, especially as you add new on-time history.

## What a good score can't do

A score helps you get approved and get a better rate. It doesn't tell a lender everything. Your income, existing debt payments and employment still matter, especially for mortgages, where lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio. And no score is worth paying interest to build: you can reach a very good score while paying every card in full each month.

## Common questions

### Is 700 a good credit score?
Yes. On the FICO scale, 700 is in the good range (670 to 739). You'll usually qualify for most cards and loans, though you may get better rates above 740.

### What's the highest credit score possible?
For FICO and current VantageScore versions, 850. But you rarely need it: many lenders already offer their best terms in the high 700s.

### Why is my credit score different on different apps?
Each app may use a different bureau's report, a different scoring model (FICO or VantageScore, and different versions of each) and data from a different date. Focus on the range and the trend.

### How quickly can my credit score go up?
Some changes, like paying down card balances, can show up within a month or two, once the lower balance is reported. Recovering from late payments or building a long history takes longer, often years.
