When you're an employee, tax comes out of every paycheck automatically. When you freelance, run a side business or have other income without withholding, nobody does that for you. The IRS still expects tax to be paid during the year, not all at once in April, and the way you do that is with **estimated tax payments**.

This guide explains who needs to pay them, the four due dates, how to work out an amount that avoids a penalty, and how to pay.

## What estimated tax is

The U.S. tax system is pay-as-you-go: tax is due as you earn income. Withholding from a paycheck handles that for employees. Estimated tax is the method for income that isn't subject to withholding, such as:

- self-employment and freelance income;
- side-hustle and gig income;
- interest, dividends and capital gains;
- rent from a property;
- prizes, alimony from older agreements, and other income.

Estimated tax covers both **income tax** and, for self-employed people, **self-employment tax** (Social Security and Medicare). ([Self-employment tax explained](/blog/self-employment-tax-explained/).)

## Who has to pay

The IRS says individuals, including sole proprietors, partners and S corporation shareholders, generally have to make estimated tax payments if they **expect to owe tax of $1,000 or more** when they file their return.

You may not need to if your withholding from a job already covers enough. If you have a job and a side income, one option is to increase the withholding from your paycheck instead of paying estimated tax separately. ([How to fill out a W-4](/blog/how-to-fill-out-a-w-4/).)

## The four due dates

For most people, the year is split into four payment periods:

| Income earned | Payment due |
|---|---|
| January 1 to March 31 | April 15 |
| April 1 to May 31 | June 15 |
| June 1 to August 31 | September 15 |
| September 1 to December 31 | January 15 of the next year |

The periods aren't equal (the second is only two months), and the IRS notes that if a due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the payment is on time if you make it on the next business day.

## How much to pay: the safe harbor rules

You don't have to predict your tax exactly. The IRS says most taxpayers avoid the underpayment penalty if they:

- **owe less than $1,000** after withholding and credits, or
- paid at least **90% of the tax for the current year**, or
- paid **100% of the tax shown on the prior year's return**, whichever of the last two is smaller.

The prior-year rule is the easy one: look up last year's total tax, divide by four, and pay that each quarter. You may still owe more when you file, but you generally won't face a penalty for underpaying. The IRS notes that **certain higher-income taxpayers** have special rules; IRS Publication 505 has the details.

## How to make estimated tax payments

### 1. Estimate this year's income
Use last year's income as a starting point and adjust for what you expect: new clients, a lost contract or a raise at your day job.

### 2. Work out the tax
The IRS says individuals generally use **Form 1040-ES** to figure estimated tax. It includes a worksheet. Tax software and the IRS online tools can help too.

### 3. Pick your method
Choose the prior-year safe harbor (simplest), 90% of this year's estimated tax, or a closer estimate if your income is changing a lot.

### 4. Divide by four
Split the annual amount into four payments, one for each due date. If your income is very uneven, IRS Publication 505 explains an annualised method that lets you pay less in quarters when you earn less.

### 5. Pay by each due date
The IRS lists ways to pay, including your IRS online account, Direct Pay from a bank account, by phone, or with the IRS app. Keep the confirmation.

### 6. Check in mid-year
If your income changes sharply, adjust the remaining payments up or down.

## A worked example: Wes, freelance designer

Wes (a fictional example) is a freelance designer with no tax withheld. Last year his return showed **$6,000** in total tax. This year he expects his income to grow, with a total tax bill of around **$8,000**.

| Option | Calculation | Yearly amount |
|---|---|---|
| 90% of this year's tax | 90% × $8,000 | $7,200 |
| 100% of last year's tax | 100% × $6,000 | $6,000 |
| Safe harbor (the smaller) | | **$6,000**, or **$1,500 a quarter** |

Wes pays $1,500 by each due date. When he files, he'll owe about **$2,000** more, but under the safe harbor he generally won't owe an underpayment penalty (assuming the special rules for higher-income taxpayers don't apply).

To be ready for both the quarterly payments and the balance, Wes moves **25% of every client payment** into a separate tax savings account the day it arrives. ([How to budget with irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-with-irregular-income/).)

## What happens if you don't pay enough

The IRS says you may have to pay a **penalty for underpayment of estimated tax** if you don't pay enough through withholding and estimated payments, and you can use **Form 2210** to check. The penalty works like interest on the amount you underpaid, for the time it was late, so paying late is better than not paying at all. The IRS also warns you may be charged a penalty even if you end up getting a refund, because the timing of payments during the year matters.

## Tips for freelancers and side hustlers

- **Open a separate tax savings account** and transfer a fixed share of every payment you receive.
- **Put the four dates in your calendar** with a reminder a week before each.
- **Track deductible expenses** as you go: they lower your tax, and you'll need records. ([Tax deductions vs tax credits](/blog/tax-deductions-vs-tax-credits/).)
- **Combine with withholding** if you also have a job: extra withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year, which can help if you've fallen behind on estimates.
- **Don't spend the tax money** on a slow month. If cash is tight, look at the budget first. ([Sinking funds, explained](/blog/sinking-funds-explained/).)

## If you can't pay a quarter

Pay what you can by the due date and make up the rest as soon as possible: penalties are based on how much is late and for how long. If you end up owing at filing time and can't pay in full, the IRS has payment plans. ([What to do if you can't pay your taxes](/blog/what-to-do-if-you-cant-pay-your-taxes/).)

## State estimated taxes

Many states that have an income tax also expect estimated payments from self-employed people, with their own forms and dates. Check your state tax agency's website, and put those dates in your calendar too.

## A simple quarterly routine

Estimated tax is easier as a habit than as a scramble. Once a quarter, about a week before each due date, take 20 minutes to:

1. **Add up what you earned** in the period and what you spent on business costs.
2. **Check the balance** in your tax savings account against what you planned to pay.
3. **Make the payment** online and save the confirmation in a folder for the year.
4. **Adjust if needed**: if this quarter was much bigger or smaller than expected, update the remaining payments.
5. **Top up or release** the tax savings account, so it covers the next payment and the expected balance at filing.

By the time you file, the work is mostly done, and you'll have a clear record of every payment for your return.

## Records to keep

Keep a simple file for each tax year with your income records, business receipts, the confirmation for every estimated payment, and your tax savings account statements. When you file, you'll list the estimated payments you made, so the confirmations make that quick and accurate. Keep the file for at least three years after you file.

## Common questions

### Do I need to pay estimated taxes on a small side hustle?
If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax when you file, after withholding and credits, you generally should. If your side income is small, raising your paycheck withholding can cover the extra tax instead.

### Can I pay all my estimated tax at once?
You can pay early, including the whole year's estimate with the first payment. Paying everything at the end of the year, though, can still mean a penalty for the earlier quarters you underpaid.

### What form do I use for estimated taxes?
Individuals generally use Form 1040-ES to figure estimated tax. You don't usually mail the form itself if you pay online; you just make the payment and keep the confirmation.

### What if my income drops partway through the year?
You can lower your remaining payments. Recalculate with the 1040-ES worksheet, or keep using the prior-year safe harbor if you prefer certainty. IRS Publication 505 explains the annualised method for uneven income.
