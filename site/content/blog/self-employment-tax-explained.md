The first year of freelancing usually ends with the same surprise: a tax bill much bigger than expected. That's not because freelancers pay a higher income tax rate. It's because they pay a tax that employees mostly never see: **self-employment tax**, the self-employed version of Social Security and Medicare.

This guide explains what self-employment tax is, how it's calculated, the deductions that lower it, the 2026 rules for 1099 forms, and a worked return for a freelancer, so you can see where every dollar goes and how much to set aside.

## What self-employment tax is

Employees pay **7.65%** of their wages in Social Security and Medicare tax (FICA), and their employer pays another 7.65%. When you work for yourself, you're both the employee and the employer, so you pay both halves: **15.3%**.

It breaks down as:

- **12.4% for Social Security**, on net earnings up to the year's wage base (**$184,500** for 2026). Above that, this part stops.
- **2.9% for Medicare**, on all net earnings, with an extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on earnings above $200,000 for single filers.

You owe self-employment tax if your **net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more** for the year. That's true even if you owe no income tax at all.

## How it's calculated

Self-employment tax isn't charged on your gross income. The steps:

1. **Start with your net profit:** income from your business minus business expenses. This is your Schedule C profit.
2. **Multiply by 92.35%.** This adjustment mirrors the fact that employees don't pay FICA on the employer's share.
3. **Multiply by 15.3%** (or 2.9% on the part above the Social Security wage base).

Then you get to **deduct half of the self-employment tax** when working out your income tax. It's an adjustment to income, so you get it whether or not you itemize.

Self-employment tax is on top of regular income tax, which is calculated in the usual way on your taxable income.

## Deductions that lower both taxes

Business expenses reduce your net profit, which lowers **both** self-employment tax and income tax. That makes them worth more to a freelancer than most deductions are to an employee: in the 12% bracket, a $100 business expense saves roughly $25 (about $14 of self-employment tax plus about $11 of income tax).

Common business expenses:

- **Software, subscriptions and equipment** you use for the work (a laptop, a camera, design software).
- **Business mileage.** For 2026, the IRS standard mileage rate is **72.5 cents a mile** for business driving. Keep a log of dates, miles and purposes; commuting to a regular workplace doesn't count.
- **Home office.** If you use part of your home *regularly and exclusively* for business, you can deduct it. The simplified method is **$5 per square foot**, up to 300 square feet ($1,500).
- **Your phone and internet**, for the share you use for business.
- **Professional costs:** fees for payment platforms, accounting, business insurance, training directly related to your current work.
- **Health insurance premiums** you pay yourself, if you're not eligible for an employer plan (this one lowers income tax but not self-employment tax).

What doesn't count: personal expenses, the share of a cost you use personally, and anything you can't back up with records.

## The qualified business income deduction

Many freelancers can also take the **qualified business income (QBI) deduction**, which lets you deduct up to **20% of your qualified business income**, limited to 20% of your taxable income (before this deduction). The 2025 tax law made it permanent. It lowers income tax, not self-employment tax, and you get it whether or not you itemize. At higher incomes, extra limits apply for some kinds of service businesses.

## 1099 forms in 2026

You might receive these forms in early 2027 for 2026 income:

- **Form 1099-NEC** from clients who paid you for services. For payments made in 2026, clients generally only have to send one if they paid you **$2,000 or more** (up from $600).
- **Form 1099-K** from payment apps and online marketplaces. The reporting threshold is **more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions** in a year.

The forms matter less than people think. **All of your business income is taxable whether or not you get a form.** If a client paid you $1,500 and sent nothing, you still report the $1,500. Keep your own record of every payment.

## A worked example: Remy's freelance year

Remy (a fictional example) is a single freelance video editor with no other job. In 2026:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Income from clients | $46,000 |
| Software and subscriptions | −$1,200 |
| New laptop | −$1,600 |
| Business mileage (2,000 miles × 72.5¢) | −$1,450 |
| Home office (150 sq ft × $5) | −$750 |
| Phone (business share) | −$600 |
| Other supplies and fees | −$400 |
| **Net profit** | **$40,000** |

**Self-employment tax:**

- $40,000 × 92.35% = $36,940
- $36,940 × 15.3% = **about $5,652**

**Income tax:**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | $40,000 |
| Half of self-employment tax | −$2,826 |
| **Adjusted gross income** | **$37,174** |
| Standard deduction | −$16,100 |
| Taxable income before QBI | $21,074 |
| QBI deduction (20% of $21,074, the smaller limit) | −$4,215 |
| **Taxable income** | **$16,859** |

Income tax on $16,859: 10% on the first $12,400 ($1,240) plus 12% on $4,459 (about $535), for **about $1,775**.

**Total federal tax: about $7,427.** That's about 16% of Remy's $46,000 gross, or about 19% of the $40,000 profit. Notice that self-employment tax (about $5,652) is three times the income tax. That's typical at this income, and it's why freelancers who only plan for income tax get caught out.

Remy had set aside **25%** of every payment, so the tax account holds about **$11,500**: enough for the federal tax with roughly $4,000 to spare for state income tax and a cushion. ([How to budget on irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-with-irregular-income/).) Without the $6,000 of expenses, Remy's federal tax would have been roughly $1,400 higher, which is why the mileage log and receipts were worth keeping.

## Paying during the year: estimated taxes

Because no employer withholds tax from freelance income, the IRS generally expects you to pay **quarterly estimated taxes** if you'll owe $1,000 or more for the year. The usual due dates are April 15, June 15 and September 15 of the tax year, and January 15 of the next year, moved to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or holiday.

Underpaying can mean a penalty, even if you pay everything by April. The simplest way to avoid it is the safe harbor: pay at least 100% of last year's total tax (110% if your AGI was over $150,000) in four equal parts. In your first freelance year, when last year's tax was small, that safe harbor can be very easy to meet. IRS Form 1040-ES has a worksheet, and you can pay online through IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account.

If you also have a job, you can increase the tax withheld from your paycheck instead of paying estimates, by filing a new W-4 with an extra amount in Step 4(c). ([How to fill out a W-4](/blog/how-to-fill-out-a-w-4/).)

## Records to keep

- **Every payment received**, with the client and date.
- **Receipts for every expense**, and what it was for.
- **A mileage log**, kept as you go (an app or a notebook in the car).
- **Your home office measurements** and a photo of the space.
- **Separate accounts.** A separate checking account for business income and expenses makes all of this much easier, and it shows clearly that expenses were for the business.

The IRS generally asks you to keep records for at least three years after you file.

## Common questions

### How much should I set aside for taxes as a freelancer?
Many freelancers set aside 25% to 30% of each payment as a starting point. At moderate incomes with some expenses, federal tax often comes to less, but state income tax and a cushion for a good year make the extra worthwhile. Check your real rate after your first return and adjust.

### Do I pay self-employment tax on a side hustle?
Yes, if your net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more for the year, even if you also have a job. You'll report the income and expenses on Schedule C and calculate self-employment tax on Schedule SE.

### What if a client didn't send me a 1099?
You still report the income. Clients only have to send a 1099-NEC above a threshold ($2,000 for payments made in 2026), but all business income is taxable whether or not a form arrives.

### Can I deduct my car?
You can deduct business driving, but not commuting to a regular workplace. Most freelancers use the standard mileage rate (72.5 cents a mile for 2026) and keep a log of each business trip. The alternative is deducting the business share of actual car costs, which needs more records.
