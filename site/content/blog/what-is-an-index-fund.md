*General education, not investment advice. Investing involves risk, including loss of principal.*

If you've started reading about investing, you've probably seen the advice to "just buy an index fund." It's common advice for a reason: index funds are a simple, low-cost way to own a small piece of many companies at once. But it helps to know what you're actually buying, what it costs and what the risks are before you put money in.

This guide explains what an index fund is, how it compares with an actively managed fund, why fees matter so much over time, and how to choose one.

## What an index is

An **index** is a list of investments that represents part of a market, plus a set of rules for what goes on the list. Some well-known indexes track the largest U.S. companies, the whole U.S. stock market, international stocks, or U.S. bonds. An index itself isn't something you can buy directly; it's a measuring stick.

## What an index fund is

An **index fund** is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that tries to match the performance of an index. Investor.gov, the SEC's investor education site, describes index funds as following "a passive investment strategy that is designed to achieve approximately the same return as a particular index before fees." The fund mostly holds the same securities as the index, rather than trying to pick winners.

When you buy one share of an index fund, you own a tiny slice of everything in that fund. One fund can hold hundreds or thousands of companies.

## Index funds vs actively managed funds

| | Index fund | Actively managed fund |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Match an index | Beat an index or reach a goal |
| How it picks investments | Follows the index's rules | A manager or team chooses |
| Trading | Usually less | Usually more |
| Fees | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| Result vs the market | About the index's return, minus fees | Could be higher or lower |

Investor.gov notes that because index funds trade less, they tend to have lower costs, and that index funds generally have lower fees than actively managed funds. It also points out that fees vary between funds and even small differences can matter over time.

## Mutual fund or ETF?

Index funds come in two main forms:

- **Index mutual funds** are bought and sold at the end of each trading day, at that day's price. Some have minimum first investments.
- **Index ETFs** trade on an exchange throughout the day like a stock. You can often buy a single share, or a fraction of one at some brokerages.

Both can track the same index at a similar cost. The right choice often depends on which your brokerage or retirement plan offers and how you like to invest. ([How to start investing with $100 a month](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-100-a-month/).)

## Why fees matter so much

Every fund charges an **expense ratio**: a yearly fee taken as a percentage of what you have invested. You don't get a bill; it comes out of the fund's returns. A 0.05% expense ratio costs $0.50 a year for every $1,000 invested; a 1.00% expense ratio costs $10. ([Expense ratios explained](/blog/expense-ratios-explained/).)

That sounds small, but the fee applies every year to a growing balance, so the gap widens over time.

## A worked example: Tess and the cost of fees

Tess (a fictional example) plans to invest **$100 a month for 30 years**, $36,000 in total. To show the effect of fees, assume a **hypothetical 6% average annual return before fees** for both funds. (This is an illustration, not a prediction: real returns vary, can be negative, and taxes and inflation are ignored.)

| Fund | Expense ratio | Hypothetical balance after 30 years |
|---|---|---|
| Index fund | 0.05% | about $99,500 |
| Actively managed fund, same return before fees | 1.00% | about $84,000 |

The only difference is the fee, and it costs Tess about **$15,500**, more than 40% of everything Tess contributed. An actively managed fund would need to beat its index by roughly the difference in fees, every year, just to keep up.

(Fictional example with hypothetical returns. Try your own numbers with the SEC's compound interest calculator on Investor.gov.)

## The risks

Index funds are diversified, but they still carry risk. Investor.gov's general warning on funds applies: you may lose some or all of the money you invest because the investments a fund holds can go down in value.

- **Market risk.** An index fund moves with its market. If the market falls 20%, a fund tracking it falls about the same.
- **Concentration.** Some indexes weight the biggest companies most heavily, so a few companies can make up a large share of the fund.
- **What the index holds.** A narrow index (one industry or one country) is less diversified than a broad one.
- **Tracking difference.** A fund won't match its index exactly, mainly because of fees.

Time helps with market risk. Money you might need in the next few years usually belongs in savings, not stock funds. ([How much emergency fund do I need?](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund-do-i-need/).)

## How to pick an index fund

### 1. Decide what you want to own
A broad U.S. stock index, a total world stock index, a bond index, or a mix. Many people start with one broad stock fund, or a **target-date fund** that holds several index funds and adjusts the mix as a target year approaches.

### 2. Compare expense ratios
Among funds tracking similar indexes, a lower expense ratio usually wins. Many broad index funds charge well under 0.20% a year.

### 3. Check minimums and account fees
Look at the minimum first purchase, whether the brokerage charges trading or account fees, and whether you can set up automatic investments.

### 4. Read the fund's summary
The summary prospectus explains the index it tracks, its holdings, its fees and its risks. It's usually only a few pages.

### 5. Choose the right account
Inside a 401(k), an IRA or a taxable brokerage account, the same fund can be taxed differently. Retirement accounts often come first. ([Roth vs traditional IRA](/blog/roth-vs-traditional-ira/).)

### 6. Automate and leave it alone
Set up a regular contribution and avoid reacting to every market move. Check in once or twice a year.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying several funds that hold the same things.** Three U.S. large-company index funds aren't three times as diversified.
- **Chasing last year's best fund.** Past returns don't predict future ones.
- **Selling in a downturn.** Selling after a fall locks in the loss.
- **Ignoring the account's own fees.** A low-cost fund in a high-fee account can still be expensive.
- **Investing money you'll need soon.** Keep short-term money in savings.

## Index funds inside a 401(k) or IRA

Many workplace retirement plans include at least one index fund, often labelled with the name of the index it follows, such as "S&P 500 Index" or "Total Bond Market Index." Your plan's fund list or fee disclosure shows each option's expense ratio, so you can compare them side by side. If your plan offers target-date funds, check what they hold and what they cost: some are built from low-cost index funds, others from actively managed funds with higher fees.

In an IRA, you choose the brokerage, which usually gives you access to a much wider range of index funds and ETFs. Check that the brokerage doesn't charge commissions or account fees that would eat into a small balance. ([Where to open a Roth IRA](/blog/where-to-open-a-roth-ira/).)

## How often to check your index fund

Once you've chosen a fund and set up regular contributions, there's little to do. Checking once or twice a year is usually enough: confirm your contributions are going in, that the fund's expense ratio hasn't changed, and that your overall mix of stocks and bonds still suits how long you'll invest. Checking daily mostly adds stress, because short-term moves say little about long-term results.

## Common questions

### Are index funds safe?
They're diversified, which reduces the risk of any one company failing, but their value goes up and down with the market. You can lose money, especially over short periods. How safe they feel depends on how long you can leave the money invested.

### How much money do I need to start?
Some index mutual funds have minimums, while many ETFs can be bought one share at a time, and some brokerages allow fractional shares. Through a workplace plan, you can often start with a small percentage of each paycheck.

### Is an S&P 500 fund the same as a total market fund?
No. An S&P 500 fund tracks about 500 large U.S. companies. A total market fund also includes mid-size and smaller companies. They often move similarly, but they aren't identical.

### Do index funds pay dividends?
Many stock index funds pass along dividends from the companies they hold. You can usually choose to receive them in cash or reinvest them automatically, and they may be taxable in a regular brokerage account.
