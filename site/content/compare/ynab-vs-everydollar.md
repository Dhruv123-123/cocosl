**YNAB** ("You Need A Budget") and **EveryDollar** are both built on **zero-based budgeting**: before the month starts, you give every dollar of income a job (bills, savings, spending, debt) until there's nothing left unassigned. The method is the same; the apps, the price and the philosophy around them are not.

Short answer:

- **Want the most flexible zero-based app, and willing to pay and learn:** YNAB.
- **Want a free, simple zero-based budget and don't mind typing transactions in:** EveryDollar's free version.
- **Follow, or want to follow, the Ramsey "baby steps" plan:** EveryDollar is built around it.

## How they compare

| | [YNAB](aff:ynab) | [EveryDollar](aff:everydollar) |
|---|---|---|
| **Method** | Zero-based: budget only money you already have | Zero-based: plan this month's income before it's spent |
| **Free option** | Free trial; free for eligible students (check the current offer) | Free version with manual transaction entry |
| **Paid version** | Subscription after the trial | Paid tier adds bank syncing and more features |
| **Bank syncing** | Included in the subscription | Paid tier only |
| **Budgeting with money you don't have yet** | No: you budget only cash on hand | Yes: you plan with expected income for the month |
| **Goals and debt** | Flexible targets for any category | Built around the Ramsey baby steps and debt snowball |
| **Sharing** | Share one budget with others on one subscription | Check current sharing options |
| **Learning curve** | Higher; plenty of free guides and workshops | Lower |

*Features and pricing as described by each company at the "checked as of" date above. Both change their pricing and features; confirm on each site before subscribing.*

## The biggest difference: which money you budget

**YNAB** asks you to budget only the money that's in your accounts right now. If your next paycheck hasn't arrived, you don't plan with it. That's what makes it powerful for people living paycheck to paycheck or on irregular income: you can never budget money you don't have. ([Budgeting with irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-with-irregular-income/).)

**EveryDollar** asks you to plan the whole month's expected income at the start. That's simpler and feels more like a traditional budget, but it assumes income arrives as expected.

## A worked first month

Kai (a fictional example) takes home **$3,200** a month, paid twice, and has $400 in checking on the 1st.

**In YNAB:** Kai can only assign the $400 on hand. It goes to rent first, which is due on the 1st but is $1,300, so Kai assigns all $400 and sees $900 still needed. When the first $1,600 paycheck arrives on the 5th, Kai finishes rent, then funds groceries and utilities. When the second arrives, Kai funds the rest. It's slower at first, but by month three or four many YNAB users have a buffer and start budgeting last month's income, which is the app's stated goal. ([Getting one month ahead](/blog/how-to-stop-living-paycheck-to-paycheck/).)

**In EveryDollar:** Kai enters $3,200 of expected income and assigns all of it on the 1st: rent, utilities, groceries, transport, a debt payment, savings and spending. The plan is complete on day one, and Kai tracks against it as the month goes. If a paycheck comes in smaller than expected, Kai adjusts the plan.

Neither is wrong. YNAB is stricter and better at breaking the paycheck-to-paycheck cycle; EveryDollar is quicker to set up and easier to understand on day one.

## YNAB

**Good at:** changing how you think about money. The "only budget what you have" rule, flexible targets and strong reporting help many people get a month ahead. Lots of free education.

**Watch out for:** it takes a few weeks to click, and it's a paid subscription after the trial.

**Skip it if:** you want something free, or an app that runs itself in the background.

More detail: [our YNAB overview](/blog/ynab-review/).

## EveryDollar

**Good at:** a clean, simple zero-based budget, with a usable free version for people willing to enter transactions by hand.

**Watch out for:** bank syncing needs the paid tier, and the app and its guidance are built around one specific plan (pay off all non-mortgage debt with the snowball method before investing, among other steps). If you disagree with parts of that plan, the app still works, but its prompts may not fit.

**Skip it if:** you want bank syncing without paying, or you'd rather prioritise higher-interest debt first. ([Avalanche vs snowball](/blog/debt-avalanche-vs-snowball/).)

## Common questions

### Is EveryDollar really free?
EveryDollar has a free version where you enter transactions yourself. Automatic bank syncing and some other features need the paid tier. Check what the free version includes when you sign up.

### Is YNAB worth paying for?
For many people who stick with it, yes, because it helps them get ahead of their bills and stop overspending. It's less worthwhile if you won't open it regularly. Use the free trial to see whether the method fits before paying.

### What is zero-based budgeting?
A method where you assign every dollar of income to a category (bills, savings, debt, spending) until income minus assigned money equals zero. It doesn't mean spending everything; savings is one of the categories.

### Can I switch from EveryDollar to YNAB?
Yes, though there's no direct import of your budget. Most people start fresh in the new app with their current balances and categories, which takes about an hour.
