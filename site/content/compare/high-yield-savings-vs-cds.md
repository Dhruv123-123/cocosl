A **high-yield savings account (HYSA)** and a **certificate of deposit (CD)** are both safe places for cash. At an FDIC-insured bank, both are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. The difference is the trade you make: a HYSA keeps your money available with a rate that can change; a CD locks your money up for a set term in exchange for a fixed rate.

Short answer:

- **Emergency fund, or money you might need any time:** HYSA.
- **Money for a goal with a known date** (a down payment next year, tuition in the fall), when the CD rate is higher than savings: a CD timed to that date.
- **Worried rates will fall:** a CD locks today's rate in. If you might need the money early, a **no-penalty CD** is the middle ground.

## How they compare

| | High-yield savings | Standard CD | No-penalty CD |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rate** | Variable: can rise or fall any time | Fixed for the term | Fixed for the term |
| **Access** | Any time (some banks limit monthly withdrawals) | Penalty to withdraw early, often several months of interest | Withdraw after a short initial period without a penalty, usually all at once |
| **Typical terms** | None | 3 months to 5 years or more | Often around 11 to 13 months |
| **Adding money** | Any time | Usually not after the opening deposit | Usually not |
| **Best for** | Emergency fund, short-term savings | Dated goals, locking in a rate | Locking a rate while keeping an exit |
| **Insurance** | FDIC or NCUA, up to the limits | FDIC or NCUA, up to the limits | FDIC or NCUA, up to the limits |

*CD and savings rates change often, sometimes weekly. We don't list rates here because they'd be out of date by the time you read this. Check each bank's current rates on its own site.*

## A worked example: $10,000 for a down payment in 12 months

Avery (a fictional example) has $10,000 saved for a down payment and plans to buy in about a year. Two options, with **hypothetical rates for illustration**:

- **HYSA at 3.8% APY** today, which could drift down if rates fall.
- **12-month CD at 4.2% APY**, fixed.

If the HYSA rate stayed at 3.8% all year, Avery would earn about **$380**. If it fell gradually to 3.0%, Avery might earn about **$340**. The CD pays about **$420** either way.

So the CD wins by roughly $40 to $80, as long as Avery doesn't need the money early. If Avery did need to break the CD after six months, a typical penalty of a few months' interest could wipe out most of the advantage.

Avery's choice: put **$8,000 in the 12-month CD** (money definitely not needed until the purchase) and keep **$2,000 in the HYSA** for closing costs that might come due earlier. That captures most of the CD's extra interest while keeping some cash flexible. ([Saving for a down payment, worked out](/blog/how-to-save-for-a-house-down-payment/).)

The amounts here are small, and that's the honest point: on $10,000, the choice between a good HYSA and a CD is worth tens of dollars, not hundreds. Picking a good account matters more than picking the perfect one.

## A CD ladder: fixed rates with regular access

If you like the fixed rate but don't want everything locked up, a **ladder** splits the money across several CDs that mature at different times. For example, $12,000 split into four $3,000 CDs of 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. Every three months, one CD matures; you can use the money or roll it into a new 12-month CD. After the first year, you have a 12-month rate on everything, with money coming free every quarter.

## When a HYSA is better

- **It's your emergency fund.** You can't predict when you'll need it, so a penalty to withdraw defeats the point. ([How to build an emergency fund](/blog/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).)
- **You'll keep adding money.** Most CDs don't accept deposits after you open them.
- **CD rates aren't meaningfully higher** than savings rates for the term you need. Sometimes short CDs pay barely more than a good savings account.
- **You expect rates to rise.** A HYSA rate usually moves up with the market; a CD would lock you into the old rate.

## When a CD is better

- **The money has a date**, and the CD term matches it.
- **You want certainty** about exactly what you'll earn.
- **You think rates may fall** and want to hold today's rate.
- **You'd be tempted to spend it.** A small early-withdrawal penalty can be a useful speed bump for money meant for a goal.

## Watch out for

- **Automatic renewal.** Many CDs roll into a new CD of the same term when they mature, at whatever rate applies then. Note the maturity date and the grace period (often 7 to 10 days) to withdraw or switch.
- **Promotional savings rates** that only last a few months, or require a minimum balance or direct deposit.
- **"Bump-up" or "step-up" CDs** that sound flexible but start at a lower rate.
- **Brokered CDs** bought through a brokerage. They can pay competitive rates but are sold on a secondary market if you exit early, where you may get back less than you paid.

## Common questions

### Can you lose money in a CD?
Not your principal, if the CD is at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union and within the coverage limits. You can lose some interest (and occasionally a little principal, if the penalty is bigger than the interest earned so far) if you withdraw before the term ends.

### Is interest from savings and CDs taxed?
Yes. Interest is generally taxable income in the year it's credited to your account, even on a CD you haven't cashed in yet. Banks usually send a Form 1099-INT if you earn $10 or more in a year.

### What happens when a CD matures?
You usually get a short grace period, often 7 to 10 days, to withdraw the money or choose a different term. If you do nothing, many banks automatically renew it for the same term at the current rate.

### Should I put my emergency fund in a CD?
Usually not. An emergency fund needs to be available immediately and without a penalty, which is what a high-yield savings account is for. Some people keep a larger fund partly in a no-penalty CD, but the first few months of expenses belong in savings.
