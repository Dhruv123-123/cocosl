## Paychecks and budgeting

### What's the difference between gross pay and take-home pay?

Gross pay is your salary before anything comes out. Take-home (net) pay is what lands in your bank account after federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), any state or local income tax, and pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) contributions and health insurance. On a $50,000 salary in 2026, a single filer in a state with no income tax takes home about {month:50000} a month before any 401(k) or insurance deductions, or about {paycheck:50000} every two weeks.

Budget from take-home, never from gross: the gap is usually 15–30%.

### How do I budget when I'm paid every two weeks?

Build the monthly budget on two paychecks, because most months have exactly two. Twenty-six paychecks a year means two months (occasionally three) bring a third check. Treat those as bonus months: put the whole third check toward a goal (the emergency fund, a debt, next year's insurance bill) instead of letting it vanish into that month's spending.

It also helps to split bills by paycheck. List every bill with its due date, then assign each one to the check that arrives before it. If one check is carrying most of the big bills, ask the biggest billers to move your due date; many card issuers and utilities will.

**Tools:** [take-home pay per paycheck](calc:take-home-pay) · [paycheck budget planner](shop:paycheck-budget-planner)

### What should I do if I can't pay all my bills this month?

Pay in order of what protects you most: housing, then utilities, food and medicine, then transport to work and insurance, then minimum payments on secured debts (car loan), and unsecured debts like credit cards last. Missing a card payment hurts your credit; losing your home or car hurts more.

Then call before the due date, not after. Many lenders, card issuers and utilities have hardship programs: a skipped or reduced payment, a lower interest rate for a few months, or a payment plan. Federal student loans have income-driven repayment plans that can cut the payment sharply. Ask what it does to your credit report and get it in writing.

**Tools:** [bill payment tracker](shop:bill-payment-tracker) · [see your real take-home](calc:take-home-pay) · [free 7-day money reset](/free/)

### Does the 50/30/20 rule work on a low income?

Often not as written. 50/30/20 puts 50% of take-home on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings and debt. On a low income, rent, food, transport and utilities alone can take 70% or more, so the rule makes you feel like you've failed before you start.

Use the same idea with honest numbers: write down what your needs actually cost, set a small but automatic savings amount (even 5%), and give what's left a weekly spending limit. Move the savings percentage up a point each time income rises or a bill goes away. A 70/20/10 or 80/15/5 split that you stick to beats a 50/30/20 you abandon in March.

More, with a worked example: [the 50/30/20 rule](/blog/50-30-20-rule/).

**Tools:** [50/30/20 worksheet](shop:50-30-20-budget-worksheet) · [budget plan for your salary](/budget/)

### How do couples split bills when one earns more?

The common options are 50/50, proportional to income, or pooling everything. Proportional is the one that usually feels fair when incomes differ: each person pays the share of joint bills that matches their share of combined income.

Example (made-up numbers): one partner takes home $3,600 a month and the other $2,400, so they split 60/40. If shared bills are $3,000, the first pays $1,800 and the second $1,200. Both keep some personal money, no permission needed. Revisit the split when either income changes.

Many couples run a joint account just for shared bills, funded by both on payday, and keep separate accounts for everything else.

**Tools:** [take-home pay calculator for both incomes](calc:take-home-pay) · [monthly budget planner](shop:monthly-budget-planner)

### What should I do with a raise so it doesn't disappear?

Decide before the first bigger paycheck arrives, or it gets absorbed by small upgrades. A simple rule: send at least half of the after-tax increase to goals and enjoy the rest. Do it with automation on the day the raise starts: raise your 401(k) contribution percentage, and raise your automatic transfer to savings or extra debt payment.

Remember the raise is smaller after tax. A $5,000 raise is about $415 a month gross; after federal tax and FICA in the 12% bracket it's roughly $335, before any state tax.

**Tools:** [see your new take-home pay](calc:take-home-pay) · [budget for your new salary](/budget/)

### Why was my bonus taxed so much?

Mostly it wasn't; it was withheld at a flat rate. Employers often withhold federal income tax on bonuses at the IRS's flat supplemental rate of 22% (37% on supplemental pay over $1 million), plus 7.65% Social Security and Medicare, plus state tax. That can take 30–40% off the check.

Withholding is a prepayment, not your final tax. When you file, the bonus is simply added to your income and taxed at your normal rates. If 22% was more than your actual rate on that money, the difference comes back in your refund; if you're in a higher bracket, you may owe a little.

## Housing, cars and big purchases

### Is the 30% rent rule still realistic?

As a ceiling it's a reasonable guide; as a target it often isn't possible in big cities. The 30% rule means rent of no more than 30% of gross income: $1,250 a month on $50,000, $1,875 on $75,000. In many large cities a typical one-bedroom costs more than that on a median salary.

If you're over 30%, the budget still works if the rest is tight: no car payment, little debt, and savings that are automatic. Over 40% of gross is where most people run out of room for anything else. Roommates, a longer commute, or a cheaper neighbourhood are the levers that move the number most.

**See the numbers:** [rent that fits each salary](/budget/) · [one-bedroom rents in 25 cities](/cost-of-living/)

### How much house can I afford on my salary?

Lenders traditionally use the 28/36 guideline: total housing cost (mortgage payment, property tax, home insurance, any HOA or mortgage insurance) under 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments together under 36%. On $75,000 a year that's about $1,750 a month for housing, and about $2,250 for housing plus car, student loan and card payments.

Lenders may approve you for more than that. What you can afford is what still leaves room for savings and repairs, which run around 1–2% of the home's value a year. Get a full payment quote with taxes and insurance, not just principal and interest.

**Tools:** [what your salary takes home](/budget/) · [home fund tracker](shop:home-fund-tracker)

### How much do I need saved for a house down payment?

Less than 20%, usually, but more than the down payment alone. Some conventional loans allow 3% down for qualifying buyers, and FHA loans allow 3.5% with a credit score of 580 or higher. Below 20% down on a conventional loan you'll pay private mortgage insurance until you reach 20% equity.

Also budget for closing costs (often 2–5% of the price), moving, and an emergency fund that's still there after you buy. On a $300,000 home with 5% down, that's $15,000 down plus roughly $6,000–$15,000 of closing costs.

**Tools:** [home fund tracker](shop:home-fund-tracker) · [savings goals spreadsheet](shop:savings-goals-sheet)

### How much should I spend on a car?

A common rule of thumb is to keep all car costs (payment, insurance, fuel, maintenance) under 10–15% of take-home pay. Another, "20/4/10", says put 20% down, finance for no more than four years, and keep total transport costs under 10% of gross income.

On {month:50000} a month of take-home (a $50,000 salary), 15% is about $530 for everything, which rules out most new-car payments once insurance is added. Price the insurance before you buy, and compare the total cost of the loan, not the monthly payment: stretching a loan to six or seven years lowers the payment but costs more interest.

**Tools:** [car expense tracker](shop:car-expense-tracker) · [take-home pay calculator](calc:take-home-pay)

### Should I pay rent with a credit card?

Usually not, if the landlord charges a fee. Card payment fees for rent are often around 2.5–3%, and most cards earn 1–2% back, so you pay more than you earn. Some rent payment services and cards waive or lower that fee; check the actual cost before signing up.

It can make sense to hit a sign-up bonus's spending requirement, but only if you pay the card in full. Carrying a rent-sized balance at a typical card rate of 20% or more is one of the fastest ways into debt.

## Credit cards and debt

### Should I stop 401(k) contributions to pay off credit card debt?

Not below the employer match. A match is an instant 50% or 100% return on the money you put in, which beats paying down even a 25% APR card. Keep contributing enough to get the full match.

Above the match, it's reasonable to pause or reduce contributions for a set period and send that money to high-interest debt, then restart them. Put a date on it (for example, "until the cards are paid off, then back to 10%") so the pause doesn't become permanent.

**Tools:** [your debt payoff date](/debt-payoff/) · [debt payoff calculator](calc:debt-payoff) · [how a 401(k) match works](/blog/401k-match-explained/)

### Does paying my credit card twice a month help?

If you carry a balance, a little: most cards charge interest on your average daily balance, so paying part of it earlier in the cycle lowers the interest. If you pay in full by the due date each month, you pay no interest on purchases either way.

It can also help your credit score in the short term. The balance reported to the credit bureaus is usually the one on your statement, so paying before the statement closes reports a lower balance, and lower utilization.

**Tools:** [debt payoff calculator](calc:debt-payoff) · [credit card payoff tracker](shop:credit-card-payoff-tracker)

### What happens if I only pay the minimum on my credit card?

Your account stays in good standing, but the balance can take many years to clear and cost a large share of the original amount in interest. Minimums are often 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, or around 2–3% of the balance, so as the balance falls, so does the payment.

Your statement has a "Minimum Payment Warning" box showing how long minimums would take and what paying it off in three years would cost each month. That second number is a good starting target. Even a fixed $50 on top of the minimum shortens the payoff by a lot.

**See the difference:** [debt payoff timelines](/debt-payoff/) · [$15,000 of debt, worked out](/debt-payoff/#example-15000)

### Should I use my savings to pay off a credit card?

Partly. Money in savings earns a few percent; card debt usually costs 20% or more, so paying it off is a guaranteed return. But don't empty savings to zero, or the next car repair goes straight back on the card.

A balanced approach: keep a starter cushion (around $1,000, or one month of essentials), put the rest toward the highest-rate card, then rebuild savings with the payment you no longer make. Before you do it, decide what you'll do differently so the card doesn't fill back up.

**Tools:** [debt payoff timelines](/debt-payoff/) · [emergency fund tracker](shop:emergency-fund-tracker)

### Is a debt consolidation loan a good idea?

It is if three things are true: the loan's APR (including any origination fee, which can be 1–10% of the loan) is clearly lower than your cards' rates, the monthly payment fits your budget, and you won't run the cards back up. It replaces several balances with one fixed payment and a fixed end date, which many people find easier.

It's a bad idea if it only lowers the payment by stretching the term, or if the cleared cards get used again; then you have the loan and new card debt. Compare it against a balance transfer card and against paying the cards down with the avalanche method.

A worked example on $12,000 of card debt: [debt consolidation explained](/blog/debt-consolidation-explained/).

**Tools:** [debt payoff calculator](calc:debt-payoff) · [balance transfer cards compared](/compare/best-balance-transfer-cards/)

### Can I negotiate a medical bill?

Yes, and it often works. Start by asking for an itemized bill and check it against your insurer's explanation of benefits for duplicate charges, services you didn't get, and coding errors. Then ask the billing office about financial assistance: nonprofit hospitals are required to have a written financial assistance policy, and many cover people well above the poverty line.

If you can pay part in one go, ask for a prompt-pay discount. If you can't, ask for an interest-free payment plan before you put it on a credit card. Get any agreement in writing.

## Saving on a tight budget

### How do I start saving when I live paycheck to paycheck?

Start smaller than feels useful, and make it automatic. Set a transfer of $10–$25 to a separate savings account on payday, so it moves before you can spend it. $25 a paycheck every two weeks is $650 in a year.

Then find one bill to cut (an unused subscription, a phone plan, insurance at renewal) and add that amount to the transfer. Saving is easier from money that was already leaving than from money you have to give up.

**Tools:** [find your money personality](/quiz/) · [paycheck savings challenge](shop:paycheck-savings-challenge) · [free 7-day money reset](/free/)

### How much should I have saved by 25 or 30?

There's no single right number, and comparing yourself to averages tends to be discouraging. A useful order is: a starter emergency fund, then three to six months of essential expenses, then retirement savings growing every year.

For retirement specifically, one widely quoted benchmark (Fidelity's) is about one year's salary saved by 30 and three times by 40. Treat it as a direction, not a test: what matters most is that you're contributing something now and raising it over time.

**Tools:** [net worth tracker](shop:net-worth-tracker) · [financial goals planner](shop:financial-goals-planner)

### How do I stop impulse spending online?

Add friction between wanting and buying. Delete saved cards from shopping sites and apps, turn off one-click buying, unsubscribe from sale emails and remove shopping apps from your phone's home screen.

Then use a waiting rule: anything non-essential goes in the cart or a note for 72 hours (or a week for bigger items). Most of it no longer feels necessary by then. A fixed weekly spending amount helps too, because a purchase becomes a choice between things you want rather than a vague "can I afford this?".

**Tools:** [find your money personality](/quiz/) · [daily spending log](shop:daily-spending-log)

### Does a no-spend challenge actually work?

It works best as a reset, not a lifestyle. For a set period (a weekend, a week, a month) you spend only on essentials you've listed in advance: rent, bills, groceries, fuel, medicine. Everything else waits.

What makes it work is the list and the follow-through: write down what counts as essential before you start, track every purchase, and decide in advance where the money you didn't spend will go, so it doesn't leak back out when the challenge ends. Afterwards, look at which "wants" you missed and which you didn't; that's your budget telling you something.

**Tools:** [no-spend challenge](shop:no-spend-challenge) · [savings challenge pack](shop:savings-challenge-pack)

### How much does the 52-week savings challenge save?

$1,378. You save $1 in week one, $2 in week two, and so on up to $52 in week 52. The catch is that the hardest weeks ($40–$52) land in November and December, when money is already tight. Doing it in reverse ($52 first, down to $1) puts the big weeks in January, when motivation is highest.

Want a different total? A fixed amount works too: $26.50 a week also reaches $1,378, and is easier to automate.

## Work, side income and money admin

### How much should I set aside for taxes on side-hustle income?

A common starting point is 25–30% of your profit. Self-employment tax alone is 15.3% (Social Security and Medicare) on 92.35% of your net self-employment earnings, and income tax comes on top. You generally have to file and pay self-employment tax once net earnings reach $400 in a year.

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more, the IRS expects quarterly estimated payments, due around April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15. Paying at least 100% of last year's total tax through withholding and estimates (110% if your income was over $150,000) protects you from underpayment penalties.

**Tools:** [side hustle income tracker](shop:side-hustle-income-tracker) · [cheapest ways to file](/blog/best-tax-software-for-simple-returns/)

### What should I do with my first paycheck?

Check the pay stub first: make sure the hours or salary are right and look at what was withheld. If tax withholding looks off, update your W-4.

Then set up the system while the habits are new: enroll in the 401(k) at least up to any match, split your direct deposit so a fixed amount goes straight to savings, and list your bills with due dates. Spend a little on something you'll enjoy; a plan with no fun in it doesn't last.

**Tools:** [take-home pay calculator](calc:take-home-pay) · [budget plan for your salary](/budget/) · [how a 401(k) match works](/blog/401k-match-explained/)

### What is net worth, and how do I calculate mine?

Net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. Add up assets (cash, savings, retirement and investment accounts, the realistic resale value of a car or home) and subtract liabilities (credit cards, student loans, car loan, mortgage, any other debts).

A negative number is normal early on, especially with student loans. The useful part is the trend: update it on the same day every month or quarter and you'll see debt payments and savings moving it, even when month-to-month spending feels the same.

A worked example: [how to calculate your net worth](/blog/how-to-calculate-net-worth/).

**Tools:** [net worth tracker](shop:net-worth-tracker) · [monthly money review](shop:monthly-money-review)

### How much should one person spend on groceries?

It varies a lot by city, diet and how much you cook. The USDA publishes monthly food plans at four cost levels (thrifty, low-cost, moderate and liberal) for each age and sex; they're a sensible reference for a home-cooked diet.

The more useful number is your own: add up the last three months of grocery spending, divide by three, and set a weekly amount slightly below it. Plan meals around what you already have, shop with a list, and keep takeout in a separate line so you can see it.

**Tools:** [grocery budget tracker](shop:grocery-budget-tracker) · [daily spending log](shop:daily-spending-log)

### Do I need a financial advisor?

Most people with a simple situation (a salary, a 401(k), some debt, saving for an emergency fund) can do fine with a budget and low-cost index funds. An advisor earns their fee for complex situations: a business, an inheritance, stock compensation, or planning for retirement income.

If you hire one, look for a fee-only fiduciary, who is legally required to act in your interest and isn't paid commissions on what they sell you. Check them on the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site and FINRA BrokerCheck before you sign.

**Tools:** [are robo-advisers worth it?](/blog/are-robo-advisors-worth-it/) · [financial goals planner](shop:financial-goals-planner)

### Do cash envelopes still work if I mostly pay by card?

The idea works; the cash is optional. The point of envelopes is a fixed amount per category that you can see shrink. You can get the same effect with separate savings "buckets" (several online banks offer them), a prepaid card for one category, or a running total on paper.

Many people use real cash only for the one or two categories where they overspend, such as eating out or groceries, and pay everything else by card or direct debit.

**Tools:** [cash envelope trackers](shop:cash-envelope-trackers) · [weekly budget planner](shop:weekly-budget-planner)

### Should I change my W-4 to get a smaller tax refund?

If you get a big refund every year, probably yes. A refund means too much tax came out of your paychecks, so you lent the IRS money for free. Adjusting your W-4 puts that money into each paycheck instead: a $2,400 refund is about $92 more per paycheck on a two-week schedule.

Aim to owe or get back a small amount, not zero exactly. Use the IRS's free Tax Withholding Estimator, then give your employer a new W-4. If a big refund is the only way you manage to save, keep it, or adjust the W-4 and send the extra straight to savings with an automatic transfer.
