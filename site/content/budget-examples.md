## 30000

**The choice: a one-bedroom alone or a shared place, in Oklahoma City.** Take-home on $30,000 is about $2,190 a month. A fair-market one-bedroom in Oklahoma City is $1,017 with utilities: 46% of take-home, before a phone, food or getting to work. Splitting a two-bedroom ($1,244) brings rent to $622.

With the shared place, the bills line of $1,314 a month works: $622 rent, $45 phone, $120 car insurance and $140 fuel for a car that's already paid off, and about $385 of groceries and other basics. That leaves $175 for funds (car repairs, gifts, renewals), $219 for goals, and about $111 a week to spend. At $219 a month, a $1,000 starter emergency fund takes about five months.

Living alone instead takes $395 more a month. It can only come out of goals and the weekly number, which leaves almost nothing for either. The other thing that breaks this budget is a car payment: even $300 a month is more than the whole goals line.

## 40000

**The choice: which city, when rent is the whole question.** Take-home on $40,000 is about $2,860 a month, and 30% of gross is $1,000 for rent. Of the 25 largest US cities, only El Paso has a fair-market one-bedroom under that ($969).

In El Paso, the $1,430 bills line looks like this: $969 rent with utilities, $50 phone, $130 car insurance, $110 fuel and $171 for groceries and basics. Tight, but it holds, and the rest of the plan stays intact: $286 for funds, $429 for goals and $165 a week.

The same salary in Phoenix (a $1,493 one-bedroom) puts rent alone above the entire bills line. That's the point at which a roommate or a cheaper neighbourhood matters more than any amount of coupon-clipping: the goals line ($429) would build a $4,290 emergency fund in ten months in El Paso, and close to nothing in Phoenix alone.

## 50000

**The choice: the car.** Take-home on $50,000 is about $3,530 a month. Say rent is $1,250 (30% of gross, about what a one-bedroom costs in Houston at $1,261). The bills line is $1,765, so after rent there's $515 a month for everything else in it: phone, insurance, fuel, groceries.

Now the car. A $25,000 new car financed at 7% over five years is about $495 a month, which uses up almost all of that $515 on the payment alone. A $12,000 used car at the same rate over four years is about $287, leaving about $228 for the other bills. Either way, insurance and fuel have to fit too, so the realistic version is the used car plus a smaller grocery number, or the new car paid for out of the goals line ($529 a month).

That $208 difference in payments, put into goals instead, is about $2,500 a year: a $1,000 starter emergency fund two and a half times over.

## 60000

**The choice: rent versus a student loan.** Take-home on $60,000 is about $4,199 a month, and the bills line is $2,100. Say a federal student loan payment is $300 a month. Then a $1,500 apartment (30% of gross) leaves $300 for phone, insurance, transport and groceries, which isn't enough for most people.

Two ways to make it fit: rent at $1,350 instead (Indianapolis or Columbus one-bedrooms are both around $1,340), which frees $150, or ask your loan servicer whether an income-driven repayment plan would lower the payment. Federal loans have them; private loans usually don't.

Either way, the goals line of $630 a month is real money at this salary. Pointed at an emergency fund first, it reaches three months of bills ($6,300) in ten months. After that, it's the line that pays the loan off early or fills a Roth IRA.

## 75000

**The choice: what the raise is for.** Going from $60,000 to $75,000 raises take-home from about $4,199 to $5,133 a month: $934 more. It's very easy to spend all of it without deciding to. A nicer apartment at $1,875 instead of $1,500 takes $375. A car payment upgrade of $350 takes most of the rest. What's left is about $209, less than a quarter of the raise.

The plan for this salary fixes goals at 20% of take-home, $1,027 a month, before the weekly number. In practice that means: on the day the raise starts, raise the automatic transfer to savings and the 401(k) percentage, then upgrade whatever still fits.

This is also the salary where your top federal rate goes from 12% to 22%. Only the dollars above the line are taxed at 22%, so the raise is still worth $934 a month after tax, not less.

## 100000

**The choice: a job offer in a pricier city.** On $100,000 in Austin (no state income tax), take-home is about $6,598 a month. After a fair-market one-bedroom at $1,530, that leaves $5,068.

Now an offer of $110,000 in San Diego. California income tax brings take-home to about $6,685, and a one-bedroom there is $2,335. That leaves $4,350 a month: $718 less than Austin, despite the $10,000 raise.

That doesn't make the offer wrong. Career, people and weather count too. But it's the number to negotiate with. To match Austin after rent and tax, the San Diego offer would need to be about $124,000. Run any offer through take-home pay and local rent before comparing salaries.

## 150000

**The choice: how much to put in the 401(k).** On $150,000, take-home is about $9,483 a month and the top federal rate is 24%. Contributing the 2026 maximum of $24,500 to a traditional 401(k) saves 24% of it in federal tax, about $5,880. So take-home only falls by about $18,620 a year, to roughly $7,931 a month.

That's why this plan's goals line is 25% of take-home ($2,371 a month): at this income, a full 401(k) plus an emergency fund and other goals is realistic, and the tax saving pays part of the cost.

If you can't max it out, contribute at least enough for the full employer match, then increase by a point or two each year.

## 200000

**The choice: where savings go after the 401(k).** Take-home on $200,000 is about $12,411 a month before any retirement contributions. The goals line here is $3,103 a month, more than a maxed-out 401(k) needs, so the question is where the rest goes.

At this income a single filer is over the income limit for contributing directly to a Roth IRA, even after a full 401(k) contribution lowers it. The usual next steps are an HSA if your health plan qualifies, a taxable brokerage account in low-cost index funds, or the "backdoor" Roth route, which is worth reading up on (or asking a tax professional about) before you try it.

Taxes are the second-biggest line after housing. That makes pre-tax accounts the first stop, and it's why the state you live in matters more as income rises: at this salary, California costs about $1,198 a month more than Texas.
