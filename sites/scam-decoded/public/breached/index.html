Was my data breached? What to do now

Got a letter or email saying a company lost your information? Work down this list in order. Most steps take a few minutes, everything here is free, and every link goes to the official site. Tick the boxes as you go. Nothing you tick is saved or sent anywhere, and the page never asks for your details.

  1. Read the notice and look for the list: email and password, card number, bank account, Social Security number, date of birth, or health information. Each one needs different steps below. To confirm the breach is real, go to the company's own website by typing its address, not by tapping a link in the message.

  2. Type haveibeenpwned.com into your browser and search your email address. It's a free, long-running site that lists the known breaches your address appears in. You only enter your email, never a password. If it shows older breaches you'd forgotten, do step 3 for those accounts too.

  3. Start with the breached account, then email, banking and shopping accounts that used the same password. Give each account its own password; a password manager makes that easy. Turn on two-step verification wherever it's offered.

  4. Use the number on the back of your card or on your statement. Ask for a new card number, and read your statements every month for charges you don't recognise, even small ones.

  5. A credit freeze stops new accounts being opened in your name. It's free, it doesn't affect your score, and you can lift it when you need credit. You have to place it with each of the three bureaus:

    • Equifax credit freeze
    • Experian credit freeze
    • TransUnion credit freeze

    Then check your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com, the official site, for accounts you didn't open.

  6. Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN. It's a free 6-digit number that stops anyone else e-filing a federal return with your Social Security number, and a new one is issued every year.

  7. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov data breach page lists exactly what to do for each type of information that was exposed. If someone has already used your details, report it at IdentityTheft.gov to get a personal recovery plan.

  8. Breach news brings fake follow-ups: "your account was compromised, call us now", fake settlement payouts, and offers to "remove your data" for a fee. A real company won't ask for your password or a one-time code. Contact it on a number or website you found yourself. Not sure about a message? Paste it into our free checker. More on these in scams that follow a data breach.

Good to know

  • Fraud alert vs freeze. A fraud alert is also free: you contact one bureau, it tells the other two, and lenders must take extra steps to check it's you. A freeze is stronger. Many people use both.
  • Your children's information. If a breach included your child's details, you can freeze a child's credit too. Contact each bureau.
  • Paid "monitoring". Many breach notices offer free credit monitoring. It can be useful, but it tells you after something happens. A freeze stops it from happening.
