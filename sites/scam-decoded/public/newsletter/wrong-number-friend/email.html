This one starts politely and takes weeks. By the time money comes up, it feels like a friendship.

This week's scam: the wrong number

Spotted this week (fictional example): "Hi Emma, are we still on for dinner Saturday? … Oh sorry, wrong number! You seem nice though. Where are you based?"

Weeks of friendly chat later, your new friend mentions how well they're doing on a crypto trading platform, and offers to show you. The app shows your balance growing. When you try to withdraw, there's a 'tax' or 'fee' to pay first.

The tell

Anyone you've only met online who brings up investing, especially in crypto, on a platform they chose. Fees to withdraw your own money mean there's no money.

What to do

  1. Don't invest through a link or app someone you met online sent you.
  2. Check any adviser or firm at Investor.gov, and talk to someone you trust before moving money.
  3. Already invested? Stop paying 'fees', and report it to the FBI at IC3.gov and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Beware of 'recovery' services that charge to get it back.

Quick hits

  • Social media is where most of these start. In FTC data, losses to scams that began on social media were about eight times higher in 2025 than in 2020.
  • Deepfake ads with famous faces selling investments are fake. A famous face selling an investment in an ad is a classic sign of a scam.

Your 5-minute job this week

Reply to no wrong-number texts this week. Delete and block. It's the cheapest scam protection there is.
